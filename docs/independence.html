Is it a remix?

a Claudyssey · methodology note · August 2026, revised after external review

The question, and what can be tested

When this translation was shared publicly, the most common objection was immediate and reasonable: a language model has read every famous English Odyssey — Lattimore, Fagles, Wilson, and the rest are in its training data — so its "translation" must really be a re-synthesis of theirs. One commenter called the opening "a crappy mishmash of Lattimore and Fagles"; another wrote that it "clearly combines a few translations line by line."

Part of that objection is untestable, and we concede it up front: prior translations live in the model's weights regardless of what it was prompted with, just as they live in the memory of any human translator working today. No output analysis can establish what a model — or a person — was shaped by. What can be measured is reuse: whether the text of this translation reproduces the wording of its predecessors, at what lengths, and how that compares with how much human translators of the same poem reproduce each other. This page reports those measurements, states exactly what they do and do not establish, and takes the accusation's strongest form seriously — including a word-by-word look at the very lines the accusation was made about.

Method

Nine texts: this translation (~124,000 words) and eight English Odysseys spanning 271 years — Pope (1725), Cowper (1791), Butcher & Lang (1879), Palmer (1891), Butler (1900), Murray (1919 — the Loeb prose facing the very Greek text this project translated), Lattimore (1967), and Fagles (1996). Each is trimmed to translation body only: prefaces, introductions, and footnotes removed, so that quoted or editorial matter cannot masquerade as translation (this matters — see "What copying looks like" below). Everything is lowercased, punctuation and possessives stripped, and proper names normalized across traditions (Ulysses→Odysseus, Minerva→Athena, Lattimore's Telemachos→Telemachus), so spelling conventions can neither hide nor simulate overlap.

For every pair of texts, two measurements: the fraction of one text's n-word sequences (n = 4…8) appearing anywhere in the other, taking the larger of the two directions; and every maximal verbatim shared passage of 12 or more words, with no cap on length. All 28 human-vs-human pairings are computed with the identical procedure and reported in full — the controls are the whole point, since any two literal translations of the same Greek converge substantially without either copying the other.

Public-domain texts come from Project Gutenberg and the Perseus/Scaife library; the script downloads them and prints a hash of each cleaned corpus. Lattimore and Fagles, still in copyright, were measured from privately held copies — only statistics are published, never their text — so a public clone of the analysis script reproduces the public-domain rows exactly and the copyrighted rows given your own copies.

Read plainly, the two tables say three things:

Overlap tracks literalness of method, in humans and machine alike. Among humans, the literal-prose pairs occupy the entire top of the table while Cowper and Pope — translating the identical poem — share 0.1%. This translation's affinities follow the same gradient: highest with the most literal predecessors (Murray, Lattimore), lowest with the poets. That is the profile an independent literal translation would produce; it is not the profile of a pastiche of the famous poetic versions.

But this translation's literal-text affinities are high in the distribution, and we say so. Its Murray (8.9%), Lattimore (6.2%), and Butcher & Lang (5.3%) figures exceed every human-vs-human pairing except Murray · Butcher & Lang — a single towering pair at 15.2%. That pair is the panel's ceiling, not its norm (the median human pairing is about 0.5%), and it is not a certificate of innocence by association: Murray postdates Butcher & Lang by forty years, worked in the same archaizing literal-prose tradition, and Loeb translators consulted their predecessors — some of that 15.2% may itself be inheritance rather than independent convergence. The honest reading is not "inside the human range" but this: the translation's affinity to literal predecessors is comparable in kind to what closely allied human translations show, is well below the one human pair known to be closely allied, and cannot — from these numbers alone — be decomposed into "same method, same Greek" versus "model influence." What the numbers do bound is verbatim reuse, which is the next section.

The Fagles half of the accusation finds no support at all. 1.7% of 5-grams, five shared runs of 12+ words, none of 16+, and the longest (14 words) is a passage Fagles also shares verbatim with Murray. Fagles sits below four different human pairings.

Bounding verbatim reuse

The "stitching" version of the accusation — the text was assembled from pieces of prior translations — was tested against the union of all eight at once: an n-gram counts as matched if it appears in any of them. Result: 81.6% of this translation's 5-grams and 95.7% of its 8-grams appear in none of the eight; even at 4 words, 68.6% appear nowhere. The longest passage shared with any predecessor, anywhere in 12,107 lines, is 29 words (with Murray, the facing translation of its own source text).

To confirm the test can actually detect what it claims to rule out, we built the thing being alleged: a synthetic splice alternating verbatim 12-token chunks of Murray, Butcher & Lang, and Lattimore. The splice scores 67% on the same union test — three and a half times this translation's figure — and lights up the shared-run counts instantly. Verbatim assembly at the scale of half-lines or longer is loudly detectable, and it is not what this text is.

Equally important is what this does not establish. A mosaic built of fragments shorter than four words, or a paraphrase that alters one word in every window, would pass the union test — n-gram statistics cannot see it. So the defensible conclusion is exactly this: the translation was not assembled to any substantial degree from verbatim passages of four or more words drawn from these eight translations. Claims about finer-grained dependence — a phrase here, a reading there — are addressed next, at the site of the actual accusation.

The opening lines, word by word

The accusation was made about the proem specifically: that its first line "combines" Lattimore's man of many ways and Fagles's man of twists and turns into man of many turnings. N-gram statistics can't adjudicate a three-word phrase; the Greek can.

ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, μοῦσα, πολύτροπον, ὃς μάλα πολλὰ / πλάγχθη, ἐπεὶ Τροίης ἱερὸν πτολίεθρον ἔπερσεν
man [acc.] · to-me · tell · Muse · much-turning [acc.] · who · very much · was-driven-to-wander · after · of-Troy · holy · citadel · he-sacked

This translation renders: "Tell me the man, Muse — the man of many turnings, who was driven / wandering far, once he had sacked Troy's holy citadel." Four observations, each checkable against the gloss:

"Tell me the man" keeps Homer's bare accusative — ἄνδρα is the direct object of ἔννεπε. Every alleged source inserts an "of": Murray "Tell me of the man," Lattimore "Tell me, Muse, of the man," Fagles "Sing to me of the man." The one construction in the line that is uniquely this translation's is also the one closest to the Greek — the opposite of what borrowing predicts.

"Man of many turnings": πολύτροπος is πολύ ("many") + τρόπος ("turn"). "Turnings" is the root-literal rendering — closer to the Greek than Lattimore's "ways," Fagles's "twists and turns," or Murray's "devices," and identical to none of them. The shared "man of many ___" frame proves nothing about lineage: Greek attaches a compound adjective to "man," English has no adjective "much-turned," so nearly every literal translator lands on the same syntactic frame independently — as Murray, Palmer, Butcher & Lang, and Lattimore all did across a century.

"Driven wandering" renders πλάγχθη, the passive of a verb meaning "drive off course" — which is why "driven" appears in Lattimore and Fagles too. Convergence on a verb's core meaning is what translation is.

"Troy's holy citadel": where Lattimore and Murray agree with each other ("sacred citadel"), this translation goes its own way ("holy") — again the opposite of the borrowing pattern. And an honest concession: μάλα ("very") goes untranslated in line 2. Critics counted that fairly; human translators drop and add small words on the same grounds of meter and idiom.

What copying actually looks like

The corpus supplied its own demonstration, and it sharpened during review. Butler's Project Gutenberg file contains a 253-word verbatim match with Butcher & Lang — because Butler's preface quotes their rendering of the proem at length in order to mock it. (An earlier version of this page reported that match as 60 words: our search routine capped run length at 60, a defect caught by an external reviewer. The corrected figure is larger, which strengthens the point.) Once prefaces and notes are trimmed away and only translation bodies compared, the Butler–Butcher & Lang figure collapses to three shared runs, longest 14 words. That is the difference between quotation and convergence: real reproduction announces itself in hundreds of consecutive words on the first string search. Nothing remotely like it exists between this translation and any predecessor.

A cross-poem aside: against Fagles's Iliad — his voice, a different poem — this translation shares 0.30% of 5-grams (longest run: 8 words, all stock formulas), of the same order as the 0.20% that Murray's 1919 Odyssey shares with it. No formal test of style is claimed here; the point is only that no gross Fagles house-style signal appears.

Limits, and a note on this page's history

What these measurements cannot do: establish what shaped the model (conceded above); detect dependence carried in fragments under four words, in syntax, or in interpretive choices — the debts every translator, human or machine, owes their predecessors; or say anything about literary quality, copyright, or the ethics of machine translation, which are real questions outside this page's scope.

What would strengthen it: Wilson (2017) — the critic's own comparator and the nearest human analogue in method — plus Fitzgerald, Green, or Johnston; line-aligned comparison rather than anywhere-in-book matching; and syntactic or semantic similarity measures with proper baselines. Her row will be added if a measurable copy becomes available.

Finally, provenance. This page was prepared with the same class of model that made the translation — a conflict of interest we cannot remove, only expose. The first version of this analysis was reviewed adversarially by a different lab's model (the same reviewer lineage used in this project's two-agent review passes), which caught the capped-run bug, the paratext contamination, and several claims that outran the evidence; this version incorporates those corrections, and the corrected claims are narrower. The method is fully disclosed, the corpora are hashed, and the script reruns the entire table from a public clone, so the numbers — though not our framing of them — can be checked by anyone with the texts and an afternoon.
