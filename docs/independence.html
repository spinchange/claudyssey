Is it a remix?

a Claudyssey · methodology note · August 2026

The question

When this translation was shared publicly, the most common objection was immediate and reasonable: a language model has read every famous English Odyssey — Lattimore, Fagles, Wilson, and the rest are in its training data — so its "translation" must really be a re-synthesis of theirs. One commenter called the opening lines "a crappy mishmash of Lattimore and Fagles"; another wrote that it "clearly combines a few translations line by line."

The objection deserves a better answer than a promise about process. Saying "the model was only given the clean Greek" persuades no one, and shouldn't: prior translations live in the model's weights regardless of the prompt, just as Lattimore and Fagles live in the memory of any human translator working today. Emily Wilson had read her predecessors too. Influence is universal and unfalsifiable. Derivation — actually reproducing other translations' wording — is a claim about the output, and the output can be measured. So we measured it.

Method

Take the full text of this translation (12,107 lines, ~124,000 words) and the full text of eight English translations spanning 271 years: Pope (1725), Cowper (1791), Butcher & Lang (1879), Palmer (1891), Butler (1900), Murray (1919 — the Loeb prose facing the very Greek text this project translated), Lattimore (1967), and Fagles (1996). Lowercase everything, strip punctuation and possessives, and normalize proper names across traditions (Ulysses→Odysseus, Minerva→Athena, Lattimore's Telemachos→Telemachus), so that spelling conventions can't hide overlap. Then compute, for every pair of texts:

— the fraction of one text's n-word sequences (n-grams, for n = 4…8) that appear anywhere in the other, and
— every maximal verbatim shared passage of 12 or more consecutive words, including the single longest.

The crucial part is the control group. Any two literal translations of the same Greek converge, because the Greek constrains them — so raw overlap numbers mean nothing until you know what human translators score against each other by the same metric. Every comparison below is therefore run both ways: this translation against each human, and the humans against each other.

Public-domain texts came from Project Gutenberg and the Perseus/Scaife library. Lattimore and Fagles, still in copyright, were measured from privately held copies; only statistics are published here, never their text.

Three things stand out.

1. The overlap ranking follows literalness, not fame. This translation resembles Murray most (8.9%), then Lattimore (6.2%), then the other literal prose versions, with the free-verse Fagles (1.7%) and the loose Butler (1.6%) near the bottom and the couplet-and-blank-verse poets at zero. That is exactly the profile of an independent literal translation — and exactly not the profile of a pastiche of the famous poetic ones. The same gradient appears among the humans: literal Butcher & Lang track literal Murray at 13.6%, while free Cowper and free Pope, translating the identical poem, share 0.1%.

2. Every number sits inside the human range. The highest overlap this translation shows against anyone (8.9%, with Murray) is well below what two independent Victorian-era literal translations score against each other (13.6%). The longest passage it shares with any translation — 29 words, with Murray — is shorter than the 32 words Butcher & Lang share with Murray. Its 62 long shared runs with Lattimore compare with 476 between Butcher & Lang and Murray. Wherever a human-vs-human yardstick exists, this translation measures as more independent than the human pair.

3. The stitching hypothesis fails outright. Against the union of all eight translations at once — any five-word sequence matching any of them counts — 81.6% of this translation's 5-grams and 95.7% of its 8-grams appear in none of them. A text assembled from prior translations, even loosely paraphrased, cannot produce those numbers across 12,107 lines.

The Lattimore row, read honestly

One result deserves the least charitable reading available. Against every translation but one, this text overlaps the humans less than other humans do. The exception is Lattimore: 6.2% against Murray's 3.5%, 62 long runs against Murray's 23. Against Lattimore — and only against Lattimore — this translation tops the panel.

The benign explanation is method. Lattimore's is the panel's only line-for-line, literal, modern-register verse translation — precisely this project's discipline — and the whole table shows convergence tracking method. But the panel contains no human translation of matched method to control for that, so the benign explanation cannot be fully separated from some real Lattimore gravity in the model's weights. What can be bounded is the size: read as uncharitably as possible, the Lattimore affinity is half the ordinary convergence of two independent human literal translations, its 11 shared runs of 16+ words exactly equal the Murray–Palmer human pair, and its longest shared passage (24 words) is under the human record. And the specific accusation — a mishmash of Lattimore and Fagles — fails on its second term: Fagles contributes almost nothing (1.7%, no shared run over 14 words, and that run is one Fagles also shares verbatim with Murray).

What copying actually looks like

The corpus supplied its own demonstration. The single longest verbatim match between any two texts in this study — 60 consecutive identical words — is between Butler's file and Butcher & Lang. Not because Butler translated that way: his preface quotes Butcher & Lang's rendering of the proem in order to criticize it. Genuine reproduction leaves a fingerprint of that size instantly, on the first string search. Nothing remotely like it exists between this translation and anyone.

A bonus negative control: against Fagles's Iliad — his voice, a different poem — this translation shares 0.30% of 5-grams, statistically indistinguishable from the 0.20% that Murray's 1919 Odyssey shares with it. There is no detectable Fagles house style here.

Limits

This analysis bounds verbatim and near-verbatim reuse; no string metric can rule out subtler debts — a reading adopted here, an interpretive choice inherited there. Human translators owe the same debts. Wilson (2017) and Fitzgerald (1961) are not yet in the panel; Wilson, as the nearest modern line-conscious verse translation by a human, would be the best remaining control, and her row will be added if a measurable copy becomes available. The Palmer and Lattimore texts came from scans, whose OCR noise slightly depresses their rows — equally for every pair, so the comparisons stand. Finally: this page was prepared with the same class of model that made the translation, a conflict of interest we can't remove — which is why the method is fully disclosed and the scripts ship in the repository, so the numbers can be checked by anyone with the texts and an afternoon.
