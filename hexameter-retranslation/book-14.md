# The Odyssey — Book 14

*The hut of Eumaeus: the slave's welcome, the second Cretan tale, the cloak story.*

1 But from the harbor he climbed by a rough and trackless footpath,
2 over the wooded land by the heights, where Pallas Athena
3 had shown him the noble swineherd, the man who most deeply
4 cared for the goods and estate that noble Odysseus had gathered.
5 There in the forecourt he found him seated, where round him a farmyard
6 had been built high up, in a place with a wide view about it,
7 fair and spacious and broad with a running way; and the swineherd
8 built it himself for the swine of his lord who had gone from the island,
9 with no help from the mistress nor help from old lord Laertes,
10 built it of quarried stones and topped it with thorn of wild pearwood;
11 and outside he had driven stakes all around on both sides there,
12 thick-set and crowded, split from the dark heartwood of the oak tree.
13 Inside the yard he had fashioned a dozen pens for the swine there,
14 close by one another, as bedding and lairs for the she-swine;
15 and in each pen fifty sows that lay upon the ground there
16 had been enclosed, breeding females; the boars slept outside them,
17 many fewer in number, for these were consumed and diminished
18 by the godlike suitors at feasts, since ever the swineherd
19 sent them the best of the well-fattened hogs of the drove there;
20 and the boars were in number three hundred and sixty together.
21 Beside them four hounds, like savage beasts, always were sleeping,
22 dogs that the swineherd had reared, that captain and master of servants.
23 He himself was fitting and binding sandals under his own feet,
24 cutting the leather from hide of an ox, well cured and shining.
25 The others had gone, each man in a different way, with the gathered
26 swine, the three; but the fourth he had sent to the city perforce there,
27 driving a hog to the arrogant suitors against his own wishing,
28 so that, after the slaughter, their hearts might be glutted with meat there.
29 Suddenly then the loud-baying dogs caught sight of Odysseus;
30 these with barking and yelping rushed straight upon him; but Odysseus
31 sat himself down in his craft, and his staff fell loose from his hand there.
32 There by his own stockyard he would have endured a disgraceful
33 wound and affliction, but quickly the swineherd ran after them,
34 darting through the foredoor in haste, and the hide dropped out of his hand there.
35 Shouting aloud, he drove off the dogs, each one in a different
36 direction, pelting them hard with a thick rain of stones, and he told him:
37 “Old man, the dogs came close indeed to tearing and mangling
38 you all at once, and on me you would have poured down shame and reproach there.
39 Yet on me the gods have bestowed other griefs and lamentings;
40 for I sit here grieving and mourning a godlike master,
41 while for other men I tend and fatten the tusked swine for eating;
42 and he somewhere wanders in want of food, in his longing,
43 over the people and city of men who speak alien language,
44 if indeed he still lives and still looks on the light of the sunlight.
45 But come now, follow me, old sir, and let us go to the shelter,
46 so that you too, when your spirit has taken its fill of food and wine there,
47 may tell me what land is your own and what troubles you have suffered.”
48 So he spoke, and the noble swineherd led him on to the shelter,
49 brought him within and sat him down, and he heaped up thick brushwood,
50 and over it spread the hide of a shaggy and wild mountain goat there,
51 where he himself used to sleep, large and furry; and glad was Odysseus
52 that he received him thus, and he spoke and addressed him by saying:
53 “May Zeus grant to you, stranger, and all the other immortals,
54 that thing which above all else you desire, since you welcomed me kindly.”
55 Then in answer to him you spoke, Eumaeus the swineherd:
56 “Stranger, it is not right for me, not even if one still poorer
57 came here than you, to dishonor a guest; for from Zeus are all strangers
58 and all beggars; and a gift, though little, is precious and welcome,
59 such as comes from us, for this is the portion of bondmen forever,
60 living in fear when the masters who hold all the power above them
61 are but young men. For the gods have indeed tied up his homecoming,
62 his, who would have loved me warmly and granted possessions unto me,
64 house and a portion of land and a wife much courted by many,
63 such things as a kindly master gives to his household dependent,
65 one who has labored much for him, and a god gives increase upon it,
66 just as increase is given to this work of mine, where I stay here.
67 In return my master would greatly have prospered and honored me, had he
68 grown old here; but he is dead. Would that Helen's whole breed had perished,
69 sunk to destruction before, since she loosed the knees of so many;
70 for he too went forth for the sake of the honor of Agamemnon,
71 went to horse-breeding Ilium there, to battle against the Trojans.”
72 So he spoke, and swiftly he tightened his tunic close with a girdle,
73 then he went to the sties where the gathered litters of piglets were penned there.
74 From there he took out two and brought them, and slaughtered them both there,
75 singed them and cut them in pieces and spitted them over the firewood.
76 Then when he had roasted all, he brought it and set it by him there,
77 hot on the spits themselves, and over it sprinkled white barley;
78 and in an ivywood cup he mixed the wine that was honey-sweet for him;
79 then he sat down across from his guest, and urged him onward, saying:
80 “Eat now, stranger, what is set before household servants for portion:
81 Pork then; yet fatted swine the suitors are eating in plenty,
82 keeping no thought in their hearts for the gods’ overseeing or pity.
83 Truly the blessed gods do not love deeds that are ruthless and lawless,
84 but they honor justice and honor the rightful works of men.
85 Even men that are hostile and savage, raiders on foray,
86 living upon foreign land when Zeus grants booty unto them,
87 once they have filled up their ships they depart for returning homeward,
88 even on them there falls in the heart a strong fear of requital.
89 But these men also know something; they heard some voice of a god there,
90 telling of his grim ruin; therefore they are not willing in justice
91 either to woo or go back to the homes that belong to them, rather
92 here at their ease they consume his possessions with arrogant wastefulness.
93 For as many as nights are and days that come down from great Zeus’ hand,
94 never once do they slaughter one victim alone, nor only two ones;
95 wine as well they keep draining in insolence, drawing it freely.
96 For indeed his livelihood once was beyond all measure and telling;
97 no man here among heroes possessed so much, not on the dark mainland,
98 nor in Ithaca self; no, not twenty men together
99 have such wealth in their hands; and I now will recount it before you.
100 Twelve are the herds on the mainland, and just so many sheep-flocks,
101 just so many swine-droves, and so many broad-pastured goat-herds,
102 fed by hired strangers and also by herdsmen that are his own people.
103 Here meanwhile at the farthest edge are eleven goat-herds in all,
104 broad-ranging, grazing, and over them stand excellent keepers.
105 Each one of these men, every day, drives a beast to the suitors,
106 whichever of all the rich-fattened goats may appear to be finest.
107 But as for me, I keep these swine and guard them from destruction,
108 and I choose out the best of the pigs and send it before them.”
109 So he spoke, and the other kept eating the meat and the wine there
110 eagerly, saying no word, but in heart sowing doom for the suitors.
111 But when he had supped and had satisfied fully his spirit with victuals,
112 then the man filled up for him the cup from which he himself drank,
113 filled it with wine to the brim, and he gave it; the guest took it gladly,
114 and rejoicing at heart he addressed him with winged words, saying:
115 “Dear friend, who was the man that purchased you with his possessions,
116 being so wealthy indeed and so strong, as you tell in your speaking?
117 Yet you say that he perished because of Agamemnon’s honor.
118 Tell me his name, in case I may know such a man if I meet him.
119 Zeus perhaps knows this thing, and the other immortal gods also,
120 whether by seeing I might report him; for far have I wandered.”
121 Then in answer the swineherd replied, that commander of people:
122 “Old sir, no man that came here wandering, bringing news of that master,
123 ever would persuade his wife or the son that he cherished dearly.
124 Rather, men wanting support, mere vagabonds roaming about here,
125 utter lies at random and do not wish to tell what is truthful.
126 Whoever comes wandering into the people and land of Ithaca,
127 going then to my mistress, talks over deceits and inventions.
128 She gives welcome kindly and loves him and asks him each matter,
129 and from her eyelids tears drop down as she mourns while she listens,
130 as is the way of a wife whenever her husband has perished afar off.
131 You as well, old father, would quickly have fashioned a story,
132 if some man were to give you a mantle and tunic for clothing.
133 But by now his skin from his bones have the dogs and the swift birds torn off,
134 and the life-breath has left him; or fishes have eaten him in the sea-depth,
135 and his bones on the mainland are lying somewhere on the seashore,
136 covered around and concealed in a multitude deep of the sand there.
137 Thus he has perished away from us there, and behind him has left grief
138 unto all of his friends, and to me most of all beyond others;
139 never again shall I find so gentle a lord, wherever I travel,
140 not even if I should come once more to the house of my father and mother,
141 there where first I was born and they themselves reared and sustained me.
142 Yet not even for them do I grieve with a sorrow so heavy, although I
143 long with my eyes to behold them again in the land of my fathers.
144 No, but longing for gone Odysseus consumes me and takes me.
145 Him, my friend, though absent, I am ashamed even now to name loudly,
146 since exceedingly he loved me and cared for me deep in his spirit.
147 Still I call him my dear one, although he is far off and absent.”
148 Then to him again spoke resourceful divine Odysseus:
149 “Friend, since you utterly now deny, and no longer are saying
150 that he will come, but always your heart is set fast in mistrusting,
151 therefore I shall not speak merely so, but swear you an oath on it:
152 home will come Odysseus; and let there be good-news reward for me
153 instantly, once in his going he reaches his own halls in safety:
154 clothe me then in a mantle and tunic, fair garments to wear there.
155 But before that time, though I am in need beyond measure, I take nothing.
156 For as hateful to me as the gates of Hades is that man
157 who, yielding to poverty, speaks out deceit and false stories.
158 Let Zeus witness now first among gods, and the hospitable table,
159 and the hearth of noble Odysseus, to which I have come as a suppliant:
160 surely all these things will be brought to pass as I tell you.
161 Within this very round of the year Odysseus will come here.
162 As this month wanes away, and the next one rises upon us,
163 home he will make his return, and he will punish whoever
164 here in this place dishonors his wife and his glorious young son.”
165 Then in reply you answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd:
166 “Old man, I for one will not pay for this good news you bring me,
167 nor will Odysseus come ever again to his household.
168 Drink now in peace, and let us remember some different matter;
169 do not remind me of these things. My heart in my breast here
170 aches whenever a man calls back my noble lord to my mind.
171 But come, let us leave aside this oath; and yet may Odysseus
172 come as I wish in my heart, and as Penelope wishes,
173 and old man Laertes, and godlike Telemachus also.
174 Now it is rather the boy that I mourn with grief unforgetting,
175 Telemachus, whom Odysseus begot. The gods reared him splendid,
176 like a young shoot, and I thought he would be among all men
177 in no way less than his own dear father, wondrous in stature
178 and in beauty; but someone, immortal or mortal, has harmed him,
179 struck his mind within; and he went for word of his father
180 on to holy Pylos. But the lordly suitors are waiting
181 there for his journeying home, so that utterly perishes nameless
182 out of Ithaca the line of godlike Arceisius.
183 But let us leave him now, whether he may be taken or flee them,
184 whether the son of Cronos may stretch out his hand there above him.
185 But come, old sir, tell me now of your own grief and affliction,
186 and tell me this truthfully too, so that I may know it clearly:
187 who are you among men, and from where? Where is your city?
188 Where are your parents? On what kind of ship did you come here?
189 How did the sailors convey you to Ithaca? Who were they saying
190 they were? For I do not suppose you arrived here on footpath.”
191 Then resourceful Odysseus made answer and spoke in reply to him:
192 “Therefore I shall tell all these things to you with precision.
193 If there were food for us two for a long while before us,
194 and sweet wine in the hut while we sat here within it,
195 feasting in quiet, while others attended their work and their labor,
196 easily then for a whole turning year without ceasing
197 could I speak and still not accomplish the tale of my spirit's
198 griefs, all the toils I endured by the ordinance given from heaven.
199 I declare that my lineage springs from broad Crete's people:
200 I am the son of a rich man; and many sons in addition
201 grew up and were born in his halls, true sons by his wedded wife there.
202 Me a purchased mother bore, a concubine in his household.
203 Yet Castor son of Hylax, whose stock I declare that I come from,
204 honored me equally there with the children born in the household.
205 He in his time was honored among the Cretans like a god was,
206 for his prosperity, wealth, and the glory of sons in abundance.
207 But indeed death-dooms came on him and bore him away to the dwelling
208 down in the house of Hades; then his proud sons divided
209 all of the livelihood up, and they cast lots over the portions.
210 But to me they gave very little, and marked out a house-lot.
211 Yet I took for my wife a woman from men of great holdings,
212 won for my own valor, since I was no weak and vain fellow,
213 nor one who hangs back from war. But now all that has left me.
214 Still, I think, by the stubble that stands you may judge of the grainfield.
215 For a great ruinous trouble beyond all measure has gripped me.
216 Truly Ares gave me boldness, and Pallas Athena,
217 and man-shattering force, whenever I chose for an ambush
218 pick of my men, while planting evils for hostile fighters.
219 Never once did my proud heart forebode death in its counsels,
220 but much the first I would leap forth and strike with the spear-point
221 any foe among enemy men who gave way to my foot-rush.
222 Such was I in war. But the work of the field never pleased me,
223 nor house-management, which is what nourishes glorious children.
224 No, but to me there were always dear the oar-driven vessels,
225 and wars, and well-polished spears, and the flight of arrows,
226 grim things indeed, which make other mortals shudder and tremble.
227 But these things were dear to me, such as a god placed within me.
228 For one man finds delight in one kind of labor, another in others.
229 For before ever the sons of Achaea embarked against Troy-town,
230 nine times over I led both men and swift-faring vessels
231 out against foreign men, and much came into my keeping.
232 From the spoil I would choose what my heart desired, and much afterward
233 used to fall to me by lot; so quickly my household increased then,
234 and thereafter among the Cretans I grew to be feared and respected.
235 But when at last Zeus of the wide-seeing counsel devised there
236 that hateful road which loosened the knees of so many mortals,
237 then they commanded that I and glorious Idomeneus
238 should lead the ships unto Ilios. There was no contriving
239 any refusal; the people's harsh rumor was heavy upon us.
240 There for nine years we sons of Achaea made war all together,
241 In the tenth year we sacked the city of Priam and started
242 homeward with our ships, but a god dispersed the Achaeans.
243 Yet against pitiful me Zeus the deep-counseling plotted evils.
244 One month only I stayed, taking joy in my children,
245 in my lawful wife and my wealth; but after that time then
246 my own spirit urged me to voyage seaward to Egypt,
247 after I fitted my ships with my comrades like gods beside me.
248 Nine ships I fitted out, and swiftly the people assembled.
249 Then for six days in order my trusty companions kept feasting,
250 while I myself provided abundant beasts for the offerings,
251 both to sacrifice to the gods and to furnish their banquet.
252 But on the seventh day, when we had embarked from broad Crete's island,
253 we sailed on under Boreas, fresh and fair for our sailing,
254 easily, as though with the stream; and not one ship among them
255 came to any harm, but uninjured all and untroubled
256 there we sat, while the wind and the helmsmen guided them onward.
257 On the fifth day then we came to the fair-flowing Nile of Egypt,
258 and there in the river of Egypt I moored my curving ships there.
259 There indeed I commanded my trusty companions
260 there by the ships to remain and to guard the seafaring vessels,
261 and I sent out lookouts to go to the posts of observance.
262 But they gave way to their pride, yielding, following their own force,
263 and at once they ravaged the splendid fields of the Egyptians,
264 carried away their women and little innocent children,
265 and killed the men; and quickly the outcry reached to the city.
266 Hearing the shouting, as soon as the dawn was beginning to show there,
267 they came, and the whole plain filled full of footmen and horsemen,
268 and with the flash of bronze; and Zeus who delights in the thunder
269 cast ruinous panic on my companions, and no man among them
270 dared to stand and face them, for doom closed round us on all sides.
271 There they killed many men of us with the sharp bronze weapon,
272 others they led away living, compelled to work as their bondmen.
273 But for me Zeus himself put this design in my spirit—
274 would that I there had died and had met with my destiny in Egypt,
275 since there was still more sorrow waiting there to receive me—
276 straightway I lifted the well-wrought helmet down from my head then,
277 and the shield from my shoulders, and cast the spear from my hand there.
278 Then I went to the king where he stood by the front of his chariot,
279 clasped his knees and kissed them; and he protected and pitied me,
280 set me into his car and carried me home while I wept there.
281 Truly then many men kept rushing against me with ashwood spears,
282 eager to kill me, for they were enraged beyond all measure;
283 but he held them back, for he feared the anger of Zeus the protector
284 of strangers, who most of all is indignant at evil actions.
285 There for seven full years I remained in that land, and I gathered
286 much wealth among Egyptian men, for all of them gave me gifts there.
287 But when the eighth returning year had come upon me,
288 then there came a Phoenician man well versed in deceits there,
289 a cheat, a man-devouring rogue, who had wrought many evils.
290 He beguiled me with craft in his heart, so that we came together
291 into Phoenicia, the place where his house and possessions were lying.
292 There I remained beside him through the full course of a year there.
293 But when the months and the days were now being completed,
294 as the year wheeled round again and the seasons came onward,
295 then on a seafaring ship he put me aboard bound for Libya,
296 plotting his lies, so that I might help him convey his cargo,
297 and that there he might sell me and take an uncountable ransom.
298 I followed with him on the ship, though suspecting it, under compulsion.
299 On she ran with Boreas, fresh and fair for the voyaging onward,
300 over the midst of the sea past Crete; but Zeus planned their destruction.
301 But when we had left behind us Crete, and no other country
302 anywhere could be seen, but only the sky and the seaways,
303 then the son of Cronos set a dark cloud over the vessel,
304 over the hollow ship, and the sea grew dark beneath it.
305 Zeus at the same time thundered and cast his bolt at the vessel;
306 all of the ship reeled round when struck by the bolt of the thunder-god,
307 and it filled with sulfurous smoke; and all men fell from the vessel.
308 Like to the sea-crows they drifted around the black ship there,
309 borne on the waves; and the god took away their day of returning.
310 But to me Zeus himself, though I had pain in the heart there,
311 placed in my hands the immense tall mast of the dark-prowed vessel,
312 so that I still might somehow escape from the evil before me.
313 Round it I wound myself fast and was borne by the baneful stormwinds.
314 Nine days I was borne, and on the tenth in the blackness of night then
315 a great rolling wave drove me near to the land of the Thesprotians.
316 There did Pheidon the hero, the king of the Thesprotians, save me,
317 asking no price; for his own dear son had encountered and found me,
318 broken by cold clear air and by weariness, led me homeward,
319 raising me up by the hand until we came to his father's palace,
320 and he put clothing around me, a cloak and a tunic upon me.
321 There I heard news of Odysseus; for he indeed was saying
322 he had received him as guest and had loved him, bound for his homeland,
323 and he showed me the treasures, all that Odysseus had gathered,
324 bronze and gold in abundance, and iron laboriously fashioned.
325 Even to a tenth generation one more man might still live on it;
326 such were the stores of the lord that lay in the halls of the master.
327 And he said he had gone to Dodona, that there he might listen
328 there from the high-leaved oak to the counsel of Zeus our father,
329 how he might journey back to the rich-furrowed people of Ithaca,
330 whether returning at last openly, or else in secret.
331 He swore it himself in my presence, and poured out drink in his palace,
332 that a ship had been hauled down and companions stood all in readiness,
333 men who would send him away to his own dear fatherland safely.
334 But he dispatched me first, for it chanced that there was a vessel
335 of Thesprotian men on the way to wheat-rich land of Dulichium.
336 He urged them to send me with care to the king, to Acastus;
337 but for those men an evil design was pleasing within them
338 touching my fate, so that pain might become my full portion thereafter.
339 But when far from the land the seafaring ship had departed,
340 straightway they plotted for me the day of slavery and bondage.
341 Off of me then they stripped both cloak and tunic, the garments I carried,
342 and around me they cast some other wretched rag and a tunic,
343 tattered and torn, the same that you can behold with your own eyes;
344 and by evening they came to the fields of clear-seen Ithaca.
345 There they bound me fast in the well-benched ship where I sat there,
346 tightly with twisted cord, while they themselves, disembarking,
347 hastened along by the beach of the sea to take their evening meal.
348 But the gods themselves unwound and loosened the bond that was on me
349 very easily; and then round my head I drew my foul rag closely,
350 and by the smooth steering-plank I slipped down and entered the seawater,
351 breast first, then with both of my hands I pulled through the water,
352 swimming, and very soon I left them all far in the distance.
353 Then I climbed where there stood the dense growth of a many-flowered thicket,
354 and I lay there crouched; and they went about with loud lamentation.
355 Yet it did not appear to their minds more profitable, further,
356 to search any more; so back once again they made for the vessel,
357 back to the hollow ship; but the gods themselves hid and concealed me
358 very easily, and guided me on to the steading and sheepfold
359 of a man who had knowledge; for still it is fated I live on.”
360 Then in answer you spoke, Eumaeus, swineherd and good man:
361 “Ah poor stranger, unlucky indeed, you have greatly disturbed me,
362 telling each thing in order, the sufferings and wanderings you have borne through.
363 Yet in good order one point is not, and you never will win me,
364 speaking of great Odysseus; and why should one such as you are
365 lie to no purpose at all? I know very well in my own heart
366 of my master's return, how hated he was by all the immortals,
367 utterly, since they did not let him be killed among Trojans,
368 or in the arms of his friends, when at last he had wound up the war-web.
369 Then the Panachaeans would have made him a burial mound there,
370 and for his son thereafter he would have won mighty glory.
371 But now ingloriously Harpies have swept him away and have taken him.
372 As for me, I stay here apart among swine; and I do not go cityward,
373 unless circumspect Penelope somewhere should urge and command me
374 to come, whenever tidings have come in from somewhere or other.
375 Then they all sit around and ask the man questions on all things,
376 both those who grieve for the lord who has long been absent and vanished,
377 and those who rejoice while they eat up his substance without paying.
378 But for me it is not dear at all to inquire and ask further
379 after the time when an Aetolian man deceived me with story,
380 one who had killed a man and had wandered over much country,
381 and he came to my dwelling; and I embraced him and loved him fully.
382 He declared he had seen him in Crete, beside Idomeneus’ household,
383 mending his ships, which storms and tempests had shattered and broken;
384 and he said he would come either in summer or else in the autumn,
385 bringing abundant possessions, with comrades like gods in attendance.
386 And you, old man of many griefs, since a god has now brought you before me,
387 do not seek my good will by falsehoods, and do not enchant me;
388 for it is not for this that I shall respect you or love you,
389 but from fear of Zeus, god of strangers, and pity for your condition.”
390 Then in answer to him spoke Odysseus, resourceful and steadfast:
391 “Surely there is in your breast a heart that is wholly untrusting,
392 seeing that not even swearing an oath can persuade you or move you.
393 But come now, let us establish between us a binding agreement;
394 and hereafter let gods who possess Olympus bear witness between us.
395 If indeed your master comes back to this house as I tell you,
396 clothe me in cloak and in tunic, in garments, and send me in safety
397 off to Dulichium, there where my own dear heart has desired to go to;
398 but if your master does not come home, as I now am declaring,
399 then urge on your serving-men to take me and throw me headlong
400 down from a great sheer rock, so another poor man may shrink from deception.”
401 Then in reply the noble swineherd spoke and addressed him:
402 “Stranger, that surely would win me fair glory and noble repute there
403 both among men of the present and men of the ages hereafter,
404 if, when I brought you into my hut and had given you guest-gifts,
405 then I should kill you again and take your dear life-breath from you.
406 Then with a willing heart I could pray to Zeus son of Cronos.
407 But now it is time for supper; and quickly my comrades within here
408 ought to be, so in the hut we may make us a hearty meal ready.”
409 Thus then the two of them spoke such words to one another there.
410 And now the swine and the men who were herding them came up near them.
411 Then indeed they penned up the sows in their places for sleeping,
412 and an unspeakable din of the pigs arose in the fold-yard.
413 Then to his own companions the noble swineherd called out:
414 “Bring the best of the hogs, so that I may sacrifice for
415 this guest from far away; and we also shall profit thereby, we
416 who for long have endured and suffered distress for these white-tusks,
417 while other men eat up our toil and pay us no payment.”
418 So then he spoke, and with pitiless bronze he was splitting the kindling.
419 They then brought in a hog, very fat, of five years of age there.
420 Him they next set beside the hearth; and the swineherd did not then
421 fail to remember the deathless gods, for his thoughts had true goodness.
422 But beginning the rite, he cast in the fire the head-bristles
423 from the white-tusked boar, and he prayed to the company of gods
424 that wise Odysseus might come again home to his own house.
425 Then, lifting a billet of oak that was left from the chopping,
426 he struck, and the life left the boar; and the rest slit the throat there.
427 Quickly they singed him and cut him apart; and the swineherd then made ready
428 firstlings from every limb of the flesh, laying them in fat richness,
429 and these he cast in the fire, sprinkling them over with barley.
430 The rest they carved into pieces and ran them through spits for roasting,
431 roasted them carefully through, and when all was done drew them off there,
432 and they laid them all in the trenchers together. The swineherd
433 stood there dividing the feast, for his mind knew fitting measure.
434 Then he cut and allotted the whole into seven portions.
435 One of these, with a prayer, he set for the Nymphs and for Hermes,
436 Maia’s son; and the other six he divided to each one.
437 But Odysseus he honored with chine unbroken and lengthy
438 from the white-tusked hog, and he gladdened the heart of his master.
439 Then resourceful Odysseus spoke and addressed him directly:
440 “Would that, Eumaeus, you might be dear to father Zeus truly
441 even as you are dear to me, since you honor me, though I am wretched.”
442 Then in reply you answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd:
443 “Eat, my uncanny guest, and delight yourself in what is here for us.
444 One thing a god gives, another he leaves; it is as he chooses,
445 whatever may please his own heart, for indeed he can do all things.”
446 So he spoke, and he burned the first cuts for the gods everlasting;
447 then he poured out the flaming wine and set it in the hands there
448 of Odysseus, sacker of cities; then sat by his portion.
449 And Mesaulios served them the bread, whom the swineherd had purchased
450 for himself all alone while his lord had been gone and far absent,
451 apart from his mistress and old Laertes not knowing of it.
452 Him from the Taphian men he had bought with possessions of his own.
453 Then to the good fare lying before them they stretched out their hands there.
454 But when they had put from themselves the desire for drinking and eating,
455 then Mesaulios took away the bread, and the others toward sleeping,
456 filled full with bread and with meat, were turning and moving to bed there.
457 Then there came on a moonless and evil night, and Zeus sent rain down
458 all through the night, while the mighty wet West Wind blew unceasing.
459 Then Odysseus spoke among them, testing the swineherd’s spirit,
460 whether perhaps he would take off his cloak and bestow it upon him,
461 or else urge one of his comrades to give it, since he cared for him deeply:
462 “Listen to me now, Eumaeus, and all you companions besides here;
463 boasting, I think, I will utter a word; for the wine gives commandment,
464 maddening stuff, which compels even one who is prudent to sing out,
465 makes him laugh softly, and rouses him up to dance in his gladness,
466 and sends forth many a word that were better to leave still unspoken.
467 But since I have shouted it out at the first, I will hide nothing.
468 Would that I still were young, and my strength still stood firm within me,
469 as when beneath Troy we prepared and led forth an ambush.
470 There Odysseus was leader, and Atreus’ son Menelaus;
471 with them I was commander as third, for they themselves asked it.
472 But when at last we had come by the city and steep wall of Ilion,
473 there in the thick underbrush all around and outside the city,
474 among reeds and marsh-grass, crouched low underneath our armor,
475 we lay; and there came on us an evil night with the North Wind,
476 freezing; and over us snow was falling like hoarfrost in whiteness,
477 cold, and around on the shields the rime and the ice-crust were forming.
478 There all the others had cloaks and tunics to cover them warmly,
479 and at their ease they slept with their shoulders wrapped under their shields there.
480 But I, fool, had left my cloak behind with my comrades departing.
481 in my own foolishness, since I had not thought I would shiver;
482 no, I had followed with only my shield and my glittering war-belt.
483 But when the night was divided in thirds, and the stars had gone onward,
484 then I spoke to Odysseus, who lay there close beside me,
485 nudging him there with my elbow; and quickly he listened and heard me:
486 “Seed of Zeus, son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
487 I will not any longer remain with the living; this storm-cold
488 masters me, since I have no cloak; some god has deceived me
489 into being in one tunic only; now there is no escape left.”
490 So I spoke, and at once in his heart he conceived this design there,
491 such as that man was in planning as well as in standing to battle;
492 and then speaking low, from behind me, he said me this counsel:
493 “Silence now, lest some other Achaean happen to hear you.”
494 So he spoke, and upon his elbow he rested his head, then he told them:
495 “Hear me, my friends; in my sleep there came me a god-given dream-vision.
496 Far indeed from the ships have we come; but let one man be ready
497 to tell Agamemnon, the son of Atreus, shepherd of peoples,
498 that he should urge more men from beside the ships to come toward us.”
499 So he spoke, and then Thoas arose, Andraemon’s son, in his swiftness,
500 springing up, and he laid down his cloak of the deep purple color,
501 and ran on his way to the ships; and I in that man’s cloak there
502 lay there gladly at rest, till Dawn on her golden throne shone.
503 If only now I were young, and my strength were abiding within me,
504 then would one of the swineherds here in the folds give a cloak to me,
505 both for kindness and out of respect for the worth of the wearer;
506 now they dishonor me, since I have evil clothing upon me.”
507 Then in reply you answered him, Eumaeus the swineherd:
508 “Old man, the tale that you have recounted is wholly blameless;
509 not one word have you spoken beyond what is fitting and useful.
510 Therefore you shall not lack for clothing, nor anything else of
511 all that befits a much-suffering suppliant once one has met him,
512 now; but at daybreak again you will shake your own rags on your shoulders.
513 For there are not many cloaks here, nor spare tunics for changing,
514 here to be worn; there is only one garment for each man among us.
515 Yet whenever the dear son of great Odysseus comes to us,
516 he himself will give you a cloak and a tunic to wear here,
517 and he will send you wherever your heart and your spirit command you.”
518 So saying, he sprang to his feet, and set him a bed by the firelight,
519 and upon it he threw down the fleeces of sheep and of she-goats.
520 There Odysseus lay down, and over him then he cast a cloak on,
521 thick and large, which he kept there beside him, a change for his wearing,
522 to put on whenever a monstrous storm of the winter should rise up.
523 So then Odysseus slept there, and beside him the young men
524 slept as well. But the swineherd was not at all pleased by the bedding
525 there in that place, to be sleeping away from the swine that he guarded;
526 rather he went outside and made ready his armor; Odysseus
527 was glad that away though he was, the man cared still for his substance.
528 First of all he slung his sharp sword around his stout shoulders,
529 then around him he put a dense cloak to beat back the wind-blasts,
530 and he took up the skin of a goat that was large and well-fattened,
531 and he took up a sharp-pointed spear, for defense against dogs and men too.
532 Then he went to lie down where the white-tusked swine were accustomed,
533 sleeping under a hollowed rock, in the lee of the North Wind.
