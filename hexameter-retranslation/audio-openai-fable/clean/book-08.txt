The Odyssey — Book VIII

The games; the song of Ares and Aphrodite; the wooden horse, and the weeping guest.

When early Dawn had appeared with her beautiful rose-colored fingers,
up from his bed rose the sacred strength of the lord Alcinous;
Zeus-born Odysseus, sacker of cities, arose from his bedding.
Then the sacred strength of the lord Alcinous led him
there to the meeting place of Phaeacians, built by the vessels.
Coming together, they sat themselves down on the stones that were polished,
close to each other; but Pallas Athena went through the city,
taking the form of a herald who served wise lord Alcinous,
planning the homeward return of the great-hearted lord Odysseus.
Standing beside every man in the city, she spoke and addressed them:
“Come this way, leaders and counselors of the Phaeacian people,
into the meeting place, so you may hear of the wandering stranger
newly arrived at the house of the wise and renowned Alcinous,
after his sea-roads, a man who resembles the gods in his body.”
Thus having spoken, she stirred every man's strength and spirit to action.
Swiftly the meeting place and the seats became full with the people,
gathering there; many marveled at seeing the son of Laertes,
battle-wise lord Odysseus, for Pallas Athena had poured there
marvelous grace over both of his shoulders and over his head too,
making him taller to look at and broader as well in his body,
so he might be beloved by all of the people Phaeacian,
also be dreaded, revered, and accomplish the many athletic
trials in which the Phaeacians intended to test Odysseus.
Then, when they all had assembled and come to the meeting together,
lord Alcinous stood and addressed all the people before him:
“Hear me, Phaeacian leaders and counselors, while I am speaking
what in the heart in my breast has commanded me now to declare here.
This foreign man, and I know not who he may be, has arrived here,
whether from people who live in the east or the west, to my palace,
urgently begging a homeward escort and asking assurance.
Come then, as always before, let us hasten the stranger's returning.
No mortal man, no one at all who arrives at my household,
waits for a long time grieving because he is lacking an escort.
Come, let us haul down a black-hulled vessel to shine in the salt sea,
newly constructed, and let two-and-fifty young men be selected
all through the city, as many as ever have proven the finest.
Bind all the oars to the benches securely, and come to the dry land;
afterward make your way swiftly here to my house for a banquet.
I shall provide an abundance of food for you all in the palace.
These are the orders I give to the young men; but all of you others,
scepter-bearing kings, come into my beautiful palace,
so we may welcome the stranger inside; let no one reject it.
Call also godlike Demodocus here, the renowned singer;
more than to anyone else the divinity gave him a song-craft,
bringing delight however his heart may inspire him to singing.”
Thus having spoken, he led, and the scepter-bearers were following;
meanwhile, a herald departed to bring the renowned singer.
Then two-and-fifty young men, the selected crew of the city,
went, as the king had commanded, along to the beach of the barren
sea; and when they arrived at the ship and the edge of the water,
into the deep water first they launched the black-hulled vessel,
then in the black ship duly they stepped both the mast and the sails there,
fitting the oars to the thole-pins securely with straps made of leather,
all in appropriate order, and opening out the white sailcloth.
Out in the water they moored her securely, and afterward journeyed
into the great and beautiful house of the wise Alcinous.
All the verandas and courtyards and rooms of the palace were crowded,
filled with the gathering people, and many were young men and old men.
Twelve sheep there Alcinous slaughtered to serve them at banquet,
eight white-tusked hogs and a pair of the shambling cattle;
these they skinned and prepared, making ready the beautiful feasting.
Then the herald approached them and led the respected singer,
whom above others the Muse loved, and gave him both evil and goodness:
taking his eyesight away, she provided the sweetness of singing.
Then Pontonous set there for him a silver-studded
chair at the feast's middle place, against the great pillar reclining;
there on a peg he suspended the clear-voiced lyre above the
head of the singer, and showed how his hands could take hold of the lyre there.
Near him he placed a basket of food and a beautiful table,
also a cup full of wine he could drink when his heart should desire it.
All of their hands reached out to the excellent food spread before them.
Then, when desire for food and for drinking was put from their bodies,
Muse then inspired the singer to sing of the glories of heroes,
choosing a song whose fame at the time had ascended to heaven:
how great Odysseus quarreled with Achilles, the son of
Peleus, once in the rich feast held for the gods, and the two men
clashed in their terrible words, while lord of men Agamemnon
inwardly felt great joy that the Achaean best men were quarreling.
Thus lord Phoebus Apollo had told him in earlier prophecy,
there in the sacred Pytho, when he crossed the threshold of stonework,
asking the god; for the great first wave of disaster was rolling
over the Trojan and Danaan peoples by counsel of great Zeus.
These things the famous singer was singing; but lord Odysseus
took up the great purple cloak in the strength of his hands and he drew it
over his head, concealing the beautiful face he was bearing;
shame held him fast lest the people should see tears under his eyebrows.
Every time that the godlike singer would pause from the singing,
he would then wipe all the tears from his face and uncover his forehead,
lifting the two-handled cup and pouring the wine to immortals.
Yet when the singer began once again, and the best Phaeacians
urged him to sing, since they felt in their hearts great joy in the story,
then great Odysseus covered his head once again and lamented.
All of the others observed not a tear the great hero was shedding;
only Alcinous marked and observed what befell Odysseus,
sitting beside him and hearing his heavily burdened groaning.
Straightway he spoke to the oar-loving people of all Phaeacia:
“Hear me, Phaeacian leaders and counselors gathered around me.
Now we have filled our hearts with the feast we have equally shared here,
also the lyre that is always the rich banquet's faithful companion.
Come then, let us go outside and make trial of every
contest, so this foreign man may relate to his family later,
once he arrives at his home, by how much we surpass other people,
both in the boxing and wrestling and jumping and swiftness of running.”
Thus having spoken, he led, and the people departed behind him.
Then the herald hung up on its peg the clear-voiced lyre,
taking Demodocus' hand and conducting him out of the great hall,
leading him there on the selfsame road that the other Phaeacian
nobles had followed, intending to marvel at all the athletic games there.
There to the meeting place they went, while a crowd beyond counting
followed behind in their thousands, and many fine young men arose there.
Up came Acroneus, Ocyalus, Elatreus with him,
Nauteus and Prymneus and Anchialus and lord Eretmeus,
Ponteus, Proreus, Thoon, and young Anabesineus,
Amphialus, Polyneus’ son, whose own father was Tecton;
also Euryalus rose, like Ares destroyer of mortals,
child of Naubolus, best in the beauty and form of his body,
after the faultless Laodamas, out of the whole of Phaeacia.
Three sons born to the blameless Alcinous also arose there:
lord Laodamas, Halius, and the godlike Clytoneus.
First they began to make trial of each in the race of the runners.
Out from the turning place was the course stretched ahead, and together
rapidly all of them flew, and the dust of the plain rose around them.
Blameless Clytoneus proved by far to be best of the runners;
just as the width of a mule-team furrow extends over fallow,
so far he came to the crowd in advance, leaving all men behind him.
Next they began to make trial of painful and wearisome wrestling;
there Euryalus defeated the best of the men in the contest.
Lord Amphialus proved himself first of the men in the long jump;
far beyond others Elatreus threw with the discus;
boxing was won by the excellent son of Alcinous, Laodamas.
Then, when they all in their hearts had taken delight in the contests,
lord Laodamas, the son of Alcinous, spoke to the others:
“Come then, friends, let us ask if the stranger possesses experience
in any contest or learned it; in build he is not an unworthy
man, for his thighs and his calves are strong, and the arms that he carries,
massive the neck, and his strength is great; he is not without youthful
vigor, but manifold troubles have broken him down in affliction.
Nothing is worse than the sea for reducing the strength of a mortal,
shattering even the man who is formed with tremendous endurance.”
Then Euryalus spoke to Laodamas, giving an answer:
“Lord Laodamas, what you have said is appropriate measure.
Go there yourself and challenge the stranger, addressing him openly.”
Then, when the excellent son of Alcinous heard what was spoken,
standing in front of the people, he spoke to the lord Odysseus:
“Come then, fatherly stranger, and try the athletic contests,
if you have learned one; you likely have knowledge of contests and game-craft.
No greater glory exists for a man while he lives on the earth here
than what he gains as a work of his own strong feet and his own hands.
Come, make a trial, and scatter the cares from your heart in the contest.
Now your returning shall not be delayed for a long time, for already
launched is the ship and the men who shall travel beside you are ready.”
Then resourceful Odysseus spoke and addressed him in answer:
“Lord Laodamas, why do you urge me in mockery this way?
Cares in my heart are much closer to me than athletic contests.
Much I have suffered already and labored in manifold troubles;
now in your meeting place I am sitting and longing for homeward
travel, entreating the king and entreating all of the people.”
Then Euryalus spoke to him openly, mocking Odysseus:
“Stranger, indeed, you do not appear like a man of the contests,
such as are practiced in many events by the rest of mankind here,
rather a man who is sailing to places in ships of the many
benches, a ruler of sailors who all give attention to trading,
keeping the freight in his memory, watching the cargo and profits
seized in his greed; you do not resemble an athlete to me here.”
Looking beneath his brows, resourceful Odysseus answered:
“Stranger, you spoke without courtesy, like an irrational mortal.
So it is true that the gods do not grant to all people together
every grace, not beauty of form and intelligence and speaking.
One mortal man may be slighter than others in bodily presence,
yet some god wreathes words with a crown all around his appearance.
People look on with delight, while never confusedly he speaks,
sweetly and modestly, shining among all the people assembled;
then, when he walks through the city, they look at the man as a god there.
Yet there is another resembling immortals in bodily beauty,
but no grace like a crown has surrounded the words that he utters.
So it is now with your striking form; not even a god could
make you more handsome, but wholly devoid is the mind in your body.
You have aroused in my breast and my spirit a heart that is angry,
speaking without any measure. I am not unpracticed in contests,
whatever you may declare, and I reckon myself with the foremost,
while I could put my confidence yet in my youth and my own hands.
Now I am held in the grip of affliction and sorrow, for much I
suffered while cutting through warfare of men and the ruinous sea-waves.
Even so, after manifold evils, I shall try the contests,
since your insult has bitten my heart and your words have provoked me.”
Thus did he speak and sprang up still wearing his cloak, and he seized a
discus, the largest and thickest, of weight far greater than all those
used by Phaeacian men as they challenged each other in discus.
Whirling around, from his powerful hand he released it; the stone sang,
humming, and down to the earth then crouched all the men of Phaeacia,
oar-loving people renowned for their ships, at the onrush of rock-flight.
Past every mark it flew, lightly coursing away from his hand there.
Taking the form of a man, then Athena marked out the landing,
standing beside it and speaking a word to the lord Odysseus:
“Even a blind man, stranger, could pick out the sign of your casting,
feeling around with his hands, since it lies not among the great crowd of
others, but far in advance. Have confidence here in this contest:
no one among the Phaeacians shall reach it or cast far beyond it.”
Thus did she speak, and the much-enduring lordly Odysseus
felt great joy, for a loyal companion appeared on the field there.
Now with a lighter heart, he addressed the Phaeacian people:
“Young men, match that throw, and soon afterward I can assure you
I shall send forth another as far, and perhaps even farther.
All of the rest, every man whose heart and whose spirit command him,
come and make trial, since all of your people have moved me to anger,
boxing or wrestling or running; in nothing shall I make refusal,
any Phaeacian except for the host Laodamas.
He has befriended me here; who fights with the man who receives him?
Witless and wholly unworthy is any man who proposes
rivalry in the athletic games with his host in a strange land;
such a man cuts his own knees from beneath him and ruins his standing.
None of the others shall I now reject or regard with dishonor;
I shall become well acquainted and make trial face to face with him.
Not without skill am I found in the various contests of mankind.
Well do I know how to handle the bow that is smoothly constructed;
first of my company I could strike down an enemy warrior,
even when numerous comrades stood close at my side in the battle,
all of them shooting their arrows against an opposing battalion.
Only Philoctetes surpassed me in shooting the bow in
Troy's open country, whenever Achaeans contested in archery.
Yet I declare myself far beyond every other man living,
all of the mortals now living who eat their bread on the earth here.
Those who existed before I shall never presume to compete with,
neither Heracles nor with Eurytus, lord of Oechalia,
who with the bow made trial against the immortal divinities.
This is the reason great Eurytus died when his life was still youthful;
age never found him at home, for Apollo was angry against him,
striking him dead, since he dared to engage him in contest of archery.
I throw the spear farther than any man sends forth an arrow.
Only in running I fear one among the Phaeacian people
might pass me by, since greatly beyond any measure the sea-waves
broke me among them, and no provision was steady aboard ship;
therefore the joints of the limbs in my body are loosened and weary.”
Thus did he speak, and they all held themselves in a motionless silence.
Only Alcinous spoke and addressed the great lord Odysseus:
“Stranger, none of the words you have spoken among us are spoken
gracelessly; you are intending to show us the strength that attends you,
angered because this man on the field has arisen beside you,
taunting your prowess, which never a man would dishonor who knew well
how in his heart to express himself justly and speak in due measure.
Come now, listen to what I shall tell you, so later you may tell
some other hero, when you are sitting at home in your palace,
feasting beside both your wife and your children, and holding in memory
all of our prowess, the works that great Zeus has granted us also,
handed without interruption from days of the fathers before us.
We are not faultless as boxers at all, and neither as wrestlers;
fleetly we run on our feet, and with vessels we are the most skillful.
Always the feast and the lyre and the dances are dear to our people,
changes of clothing and warm bathing waters and beds for our pleasure.
Come then, all of Phaeacia's finest and most skillful dancers,
dance, so the stranger may tell his own people when homeward returning
how we surpass all other mankind in the skills we are given,
seamanship, running, and dancing together with singing and music.
Let someone hurry away and bring to Demodocus quickly
all of the clear-voiced lyre, which lies in the palace somewhere there.”
Thus spoke godlike Alcinous; then the herald arose there,
going to bring from the house of the king the hollow-formed lyre.
Nine chosen stewards of contests arose with the herald together,
officers set by the people to order events at assemblies;
smoothly they leveled a dancing place and made wider the fine ring.
Then the herald approached and carried the clear-voiced lyre there
for Demodocus, who walked to the middle, while standing around him
boys in the earliest flower of youth, all skilled in the dance steps,
struck with their feet at the radiant floor; and the lord Odysseus
watched the bright flicker and flash of their feet and marveled within him.
Striking the lyre, then the singer began a most beautiful song of
love between Ares and lovely-crowned goddess Aphrodite,
how they had first lain together in secret in lordly Hephaestus'
house, and how Ares gave manifold gifts and dishonored the marriage
bed of the lord Hephaestus. Yet there came one with a message:
Helios, Sun, had seen the two gods as together they lay there.
Then, when Hephaestus had heard the heart-stinging message delivered,
into his forge he journeyed, while deep in his heart he was brooding
evil; he placed the enormous anvil upon its foundation,
hammering chains that were never to break or be loosened, to hold them.
Then, when he fashioned the trap in his anger against god Ares,
into the chamber he went, where his own marriage bed had been placed there;
round every bedpost there he poured chains in a circle,
while many more from above, from the beam of the roof, he suspended,
fine as a spider's web, which none could discover by eyesight,
even the blessed gods, for they had been made with surpassing deception.
Then, when he poured the whole snare in a circle all over the bedstead,
he made a show of departing for Lemnos, the strongly built city,
which above all other places to him was the dearest of countries.
Ares who held the gold reins was not keeping watch without eyesight;
he saw the famous craftsman Hephaestus leaving the palace,
then made his way to the home of the widely renowned Hephaestus,
longing to lie in the love of the lovely-crowned Cytherea.
She had come newly from visiting father, the powerful son of
Cronos, and just had sat down when lord Ares entered the household,
taking her hand in his own and addressing the goddess directly:
“Come, my beloved, and lie in the bed, taking pleasure together;
lord Hephaestus is no longer at home, and I think he is now in
Lemnos, among the Sintians there with their wild-sounding language.”
Thus did he speak, and the thought of lying beside him was welcome.
Both of them went to the bed and lay down, while around them descended
all of the chains cleverly made by Hephaestus, the god of invention;
neither could move any limb or raise up the weight of a body.
Then they discovered the truth, that no avenue now could release them.
Close to them came the famous and powerfully armed Hephaestus,
turning around on his road before coming to Lemnos the island;
Helios watched for the god and told him the whole of the story.
Into his palace he went with his heart weighed down by the sorrow;
there in the gateway he stood, and terrible anger possessed him.
Crying aloud in a terrible voice, he called all the gods there:
“Father Zeus, and you other divinities blessed and deathless,
come here and look at a laughable thing that is wholly unbearable,
how Aphrodite, the daughter of Zeus, has always dishonored
me for my lameness and loves Ares, destroyer of people,
since he is handsome and straight in his legs, while I was created
crippled in body. Who else bears the blame? No one except my
parents together; I wish they had never at all brought me forth here.
Look at them now, as they lie with each other in love and are sleeping
there in my own marriage bed, while the sight makes my heart eaten by sorrow.
Yet I do not think they shall wish to remain there lying together,
even a moment, although they are deeply in love with each other;
soon they shall lose the desire to sleep. Yet the snare and the bindings
hold them until her own father pays back every one of the bride-gifts,
all I provided him there for this dog-eyed daughter of his house,
beautiful though she may be, for she holds not her heart within measure.”
Thus did he speak, and the gods came gathering into the bronze-floored
palace; Poseidon arrived, great god who encircles the earth-land;
Hermes the runner arrived, and the far-working lordly Apollo.
Yet all the goddesses stayed in their homes from a sense of the shame there.
Gods who provide all blessings were standing together in doorways;
then in the blessed gods rose laughter that never could be quenched,
seeing the clever device of Hephaestus, the god of invention.
Then one god would glance at his neighbor and speak in this manner:
“Wrongdoing does not prosper; the slow one captures the swift one.
See how Hephaestus, although he is slow, has captured god Ares,
fleetest of all of the gods who inhabit the heights of Olympus,
lame though he is, by his craft. Now the fine for adultery waits him.”
Thus did the gods speak words in this manner between one another.
Then lord Apollo, the son of great Zeus, spoke out to lord Hermes:
“Hermes, son of great Zeus, giving good things and guiding the traveler,
would you be willing, restrained under chains that were heavy and mighty,
there in the bed to lie close beside golden Aphrodite?”
Then the guide and the slayer of Argus replied to Apollo:
“If that were only to happen, far-shooting lordly Apollo!
Let even thrice the innumerable number of bindings
hold me, and all of you gods look on, with the goddesses also;
still would I gladly lie there beside golden Aphrodite.”
Thus did he speak, and the deathless divinities broke into laughter.
Only Poseidon laughed not, but continually pleaded
there with renowned Hephaestus the craftsman to set Ares free from
bondage; and raising his voice he spoke to Hephaestus in winged words:
“Release him, and I promise you this before all the immortals:
Ares shall pay all the fine you demand that is fitting and lawful.”
Then the famous and powerfully armed Hephaestus answered:
“Do not command me, Poseidon, great god who encircles the earth-land.
Worthless indeed is the pledge that is taken in trust for a worthless
man. How could I bind you fast in the sight of the gods everlasting
if Ares casts off both his binding and debt and escapes me?”
Then the great Earth-shaker lord Poseidon replied to Hephaestus:
“Even if Ares should cast off his debt and escape from the binding,
I shall myself pay all that he owes, and the fine shall be given.”
Then the famous and powerfully armed Hephaestus answered:
“It is not possible or right that I now should refuse you.”
Thus having spoken, the strength of Hephaestus released all the binding.
Then, when the two were set free from the bonds that were mighty around them,
straightway they sprang to their feet; Ares went off to the Thracians,
while Aphrodite who loves laughter went to the island of Cyprus,
Paphos, where she possesses a precinct and altar made fragrant.
There all the Graces bathed her and rubbed her with oil everlasting,
such as shines over the gods who continue in life without ending;
then they arrayed her in beautiful clothes, a great wonder to look on.
These things the famous singer was singing; and lord Odysseus
felt great delight in his heart as he heard, with the other Phaeacians,
people who loved the long oar and were famous for beautiful vessels.
Then Alcinous told Laodamas and Halius only
now to perform in the dance, since no one could rival their dancing.
Then in their hands they took a beautiful ball that was colored
purple, the work of the skilled craftsman Polybus, made for the young men.
One, having bent himself backward, would throw it toward shadowy cloud-land;
then the other would leap from the ground and would easily catch it,
light as he hung in the air, before his feet came again to the earth-land.
Then, when they tried their skill with the ball going straight up above them,
both of them danced on the grain-giving earth as they cast it between them,
rapidly throwing it backward and forward, while all the other
young men struck out the time at the ring, and an uproar arose there.
Then great Odysseus spoke to the glorious lord Alcinous:
“Lord Alcinous, most renowned among all of the people,
you made a boast that your dancers were best; it has come to completion.
Awe and amazement possess me at heart as I look at their dancing.”
Thus did he speak, and the sacred strength of the lord Alcinous
felt great joy; straightway he spoke to the oar-loving whole of the people:
“Hear me, Phaeacian leaders and counselors gathered around me.
This foreign man now seems to me to possess understanding.
Come then, as fitting, provide him a gift in our guest-friendship.
Twelve renowned kings hold their command in the country around us,
serving as leaders, and I am myself to be counted the thirteenth.
Let every one bring a freshly washed mantle and tunic before him,
also a talent in weight of the gold that is precious and costly;
let us collect them together at once, so the stranger may take them
into his hands and return to the feast with a heart full of gladness.
Let Euryalus make restitution to him, using language
also a gift, for the word that he spoke was not one of due measure.”
Thus did he speak, and they all gave approval and urged it together;
each of them sent off a herald to carry a gift from his household.
Then Euryalus spoke to Alcinous, giving an answer:
“Lord Alcinous, most renowned among all of the people,
I shall make full restitution to this foreign man, as you order.
I shall present him this sword, made wholly of bronze, with a handle
fashioned of silver upon it, and round it a newly cut ivory
scabbard; the gift shall be worth a great deal to him later.”
Thus having spoken, the silver-studded sword he set in the hands of
lord Odysseus, addressing the stranger in winged words:
“Hail, fatherly stranger. If some harsh word was unwisely
blurted, may storm winds catch it and carry it far in the distance.
May all the gods let you see your wife and return to your homeland,
since you have suffered long, far away from the people who love you.”
Then resourceful Odysseus spoke and addressed him in answer:
“Hail to you warmly, my friend, and may gods give you happiness always.
May you hereafter never experience longing to have this
sword you have given to me, after making amends in your language.”
Thus did he speak, and he slung the silver-studded sword round his shoulders.
Then the sun entered the west, and the famous gifts lay before him.
Heralds of lordship carried the gifts to the house of Alcinous;
there all the sons of the blameless Alcinous met them and took them,
placing the beautiful gifts with the reverend queenly mother.
Then the sacred strength of the lord Alcinous led them
into the palace, and there they sat on the high seats in order.
Then the strength of Alcinous spoke to the queenly Arete:
“Wife, bring a beautiful chest, now the best one you have in the palace,
laying yourself a freshly washed mantle and tunic within it.
Heat a great cauldron of bronze on the fire, so the water is warmed there,
so that the stranger, when bathed and when seeing collected in order
all of the gifts which the blameless Phaeacian nobles have brought him,
may in the feast take delight and in hearing the song of the singer.
I shall present him the beautiful cup I possess in my household,
fashioned of gold, so he may remember me all of his lifetime,
pouring to Zeus and the rest of the gods in his own great palace.”
Thus did he speak; then Arete commanded the serving women
swiftly to set on the fire a large tripod cauldron for bathing.
They set the great bathing cauldron directly on top of the bright fire,
pouring the water within, and they fed burning wood underneath it.
Fire wrapped all round the belly of bronze, and the water was heated.
Meanwhile, out of her chamber Arete brought forth a most lovely
chest for the stranger, and laid in it all of the beautiful presents,
clothing and gold the Phaeacian people had given Odysseus;
also she laid in a mantle and tunic of beautiful weaving.
Raising her voice, she spoke to the lord Odysseus in winged words:
“Look to the lid for yourself now, and fasten a knot on it quickly,
lest some man rob you of treasure when later you make the long voyage,
sleeping your sweet sleep aboard while the black-hulled ship carries onward.”
Then, when the much-enduring and lordly Odysseus heard her,
straightway he fitted the lid to the chest and securely he tied it,
using the intricate knot that the queenly Circe had taught him.
Straightway the housekeeper called him to enter the bath for his washing;
joyfully then his heart saw the warm bathing water before him,
since such attendance had not been provided to him for a long time,
not since he left the home of Calypso, the beautiful-haired one;
while in her home he had always been tended the same as a god there.
Then, when the serving women had bathed and anointed his body,
casting around him a beautiful cloak and a tunic for clothing,
out of the bath he came and went to the men at the wine feast.
Nausicaa, who received from the gods her surpassing beauty,
stood by the pillar supporting the roof that was strongly constructed;
there she beheld great Odysseus and marveled as she looked upon him.
Raising her voice, she addressed great Odysseus in winged language:
“Farewell, stranger; and someday, when you are home in your country,
hold me in memory, since first to me you owe the price of your lifetime.”
Then resourceful Odysseus spoke and addressed her in answer:
“Lord Alcinous' daughter Nausicaa, hear what I tell you:
only may Zeus so grant it, loud-thundering husband of Hera,
that I return to my home and behold the glad day of my homecoming;
then I shall pray to you even there as I pray to a goddess,
always through all of my days, for you saved my life, gentle maiden.”
Thus did he speak, and he sat by the side of the king Alcinous.
Already portions of meat were served and the wine bowl was mixing;
then the herald approached them and led the respected singer,
lord Demodocus, honored among all the people, and sat him
there in the midst of the feasters, against the great pillar reclining.
Then resourceful Odysseus called to the herald beside him,
cutting a piece from the chine of a white-tusked boar, with a larger
part still remaining, while rich fat lay in abundance around it:
“Herald, take this meat now and place it before Demodocus,
so he may eat, and I shall show him respect though I carry my sorrow.
Singers receive from all people throughout the expanse of the earth-land
honor and reverence as their portion, because the immortal
Muse has instructed their pathways and loves every tribe of the singers.”
Thus did he speak, and the herald carried and placed it within the
hands of the hero Demodocus, who received it with joy in his spirit.
All of their hands reached out to the excellent food spread before them.
Then, when desire for food and for drinking was put from their bodies,
then resourceful Odysseus spoke to Demodocus openly:
“Lord Demodocus, I give you praise beyond all other mortals.
Either the Muse, child of Zeus, gave you teaching, or lordly Apollo,
since you correctly in order sing the Achaeans' destruction,
all they accomplished and suffered, the manifold toil of Achaeans,
just as if you were there for yourself or had heard from a witness.
Come now, change your song's pathway and sing how the wooden horse was
built by Epeius together with Pallas Athena, a cunning
trap great Odysseus took to the heights of the fortress of Trojans,
filled with the warriors who sacked Ilium's city around them.
If you recount me this story as well and in proper proportion,
then I shall speak out at once and relate to the whole of humanity
how some divinity favored you, giving you godlike singing.”
Thus did he speak, and the singer began, by a god stirred to singing,
starting the song where the Argives embarked on the well-benched vessels,
sailing away when they cast burning fire on all of the war camp,
while other men with renowned Odysseus already were sitting
there in the Trojans' place of assembly, concealed in the horse-form,
since the Trojans themselves pulled the horse to the height of the fortress.
There it remained, while the Trojans talked endlessly, never deciding,
seated around it, and three different counsels were pleasing among them:
either to split the hollowed wood with the merciless bronze blade,
or to drag it to heights and cast it below from the cliff rocks,
or let it stand as a mighty gift to appease the immortals.
This was the way the affair would be ended at last by their portion;
Fate had appointed them all to be ruined whenever the city
took in the great horse made of wood, wherein all the Argives' best warriors
waited, conveying the doom and the death of the Trojans among them.
Then he sang how the sons of Achaeans had pillaged the city,
pouring out of the horse and abandoning hollow concealment.
Then he sang how they ravaged the high city's streets, each in his pathway,
how great Odysseus went like murderous Ares directly
into the house of Deiphobus, godlike Menelaus beside him;
there, he sang, Odysseus dared the most terrible combat,
conquering finally through great-hearted Pallas Athena.
These things the famous singer was singing; but lord Odysseus
melted, and out from beneath both eyelids the tears wet his cheekbones.
Just as a woman will weep when she falls on her well-beloved husband,
one who has fallen in front of his city and all of his people,
striving to ward the pitiless day from his town and his children;
seeing him dying and gasping the last of his breath, she around him
pours out her body and wails in a high clear voice, while behind her
spearmen strike at her back and her shoulders with spears, and they lead her
into captivity, there to endure all the labor and sorrow;
wasted away are her cheeks by the grief that arouses most pity.
So were the pitiful tears great Odysseus shed from his eyebrows.
All of the others observed not a tear the great hero was shedding;
only Alcinous marked and observed what befell Odysseus,
sitting beside him and hearing his heavily burdened groaning.
Straightway he spoke to the oar-loving people of all Phaeacia:
“Hear me, Phaeacian leaders and counselors gathered around me;
let Demodocus now hold back from the clear-voiced lyre he is playing,
since what he sings does not bring equal joy to all of the people.
Ever since we began eating supper, and since the divine
singer arose to perform, our stranger has never yet halted
his lamentation and grief; some mighty sorrow surrounds him.
Come then, let our singer now stop, so that we may take equal
pleasure together, both foreign guest and the people who host him.
Thus it is better by far, since all things are made for the guest we
honor, his homeward escort and gifts that in love we have given.
Just like a brother, the foreign stranger and suppliant must be
dear to the man whose intelligence has even a little attainment.
Do not conceal any longer beneath well-considered evasions
anything I ask you, for openly speaking is nobler.
Tell us the name that your father and mother have used there to call you,
also the people who live in the town and in places around it.
No mortal man in humanity ever is wholly without name,
neither the noble nor base, once he has been born among people;
parents give names to all children alike at the time they have borne them.
Tell me the land and the people you claim and the city you dwell in,
so that our vessels may carry you there with their thought for a pilot.
Ships of Phaeacians carry no helmsmen among them and never
carry the steering oars that the vessels of other men carry;
ships themselves understand every thought and the mind of the people,
knowing the cities of every humanity, knowing the rich fields;
swiftest of all things they cross every gulf of the sea and the salt waves,
covered in mist and in cloud, and they never have any fear of
coming to harm or enduring the ruin of wreck on their pathways.
Yet I once heard my father Nausithous speak in this manner:
he would declare that Poseidon regarded our people with anger,
since we give every man safe escort without any distinction;
therefore he said that a beautiful ship of Phaeacian people,
coming to home through the mist-covered sea from a voyage of escort,
would be destroyed by the god, and a mountain would cover the city.
Thus our elder had spoken; the god may bring it to fullness,
or leave the matter undone, in whatever way pleases his spirit.
Come now, tell me these things, and relate every matter with truth here:
where have you wandered and into what countries of mankind have you entered?
Tell of the people and cities where excellent houses are standing,
both of the violent, savage, and those who are strangers to justice,
also the friendly to strangers, with god-fearing hearts in their bodies.
Tell me the cause why you weep and lament in the heart you are bearing,
hearing the fate of the Argive Danaans and city of Ilium.
Gods made that destiny happen and spun the destruction for mortals,
so that among generations to come there might always be singing.
Did some relative dear to you die at the walls of the Trojans,
excellent man, some father-in-law or the husband of daughter,
those who are closest to us after those of our blood and our lineage?
Or did a companion die there, a man who was dear to your own heart,
excellent man, since never is less than the love of a brother
love for a comrade who carries clear thought and a true understanding?”
