The Odyssey — Book XIX

The interview by the fire; the scar; the contest announced.

But in the hall there stayed behind noble Odysseus,
plotting the suitors’ slaughter together with Pallas Athena.
Quickly he spoke then out to Telemachus winged words:
“Telemachus, you must lay up the war-gear away in the inner room,
every piece; and the suitors, if longing they ask you about them,
you must put them aside with gentle and soft-spoken wording:
“Out of the smoke I removed them, because they no longer resembled
what kind of arms Odysseus left here when he journeyed to Troy-land;
no, they are blackened all over, wherever the breath of the fire came.
And besides, some god put even this greater thought in my spirit:
lest somehow, drunken with wine, when you kindle a quarrel among you,
you should wound one another and bring disgrace on the feasting
and on the courtship; for iron itself draws a man onward.’”
So he spoke, and Telemachus obeyed his beloved father.
Then he called to the nurse and spoke to Eurycleia:
“Nurse, come now, shut up the women apart in their quarters for me,
while I put in a chamber the gear and the armor of my father,
beautiful things, which all through the house uncared-for the smoke dims,
since my father went away; and I was still then but a small child.
Now I desire to lay them aside where the breath of the fire will not reach.”
Then in return the dear nurse Eurycleia answered him:
“If only, my child, you would truly take thought and good prudence at last,
caring now for the house and guarding all of its substance.
But come, who then will carry a light as he goes there before you,
since you do not allow the maidservants to go out, who would light you?”
Then wise Telemachus spoke again and answered her:
“This stranger here; for I will not keep any man in idleness
who touches my meal-bin, even if he has come from afar off.”
So he spoke, and for her the uttered word grew no wings then.
She locked the doors of the strong-built and well-inhabited hall.
Then up they sprang, Odysseus and his glorious son there,
carrying inward the helmets and bossed shields together
and the sharp-pointed spears; and before them Pallas Athena,
holding a golden lamp in her hand, made wonderful brightness.
Then Telemachus quickly addressed his father and said this:
“Father, indeed I behold with my eyes here a marvel full great.
Surely the walls of the hall, and the handsome cross-beams within it,
and the fir-wood rafters, and pillars that tower aloft there,
shine in my eyes as though from the blaze of a burning fire.
Truly some god is within, one of those who possess wide heaven.”
Then resourceful Odysseus answered him, saying in turn:
“Silence now, keep it within your mind, and do not be asking.
Such indeed is the custom of gods who hold high Olympus.
But you go now to bed, and I shall remain here behind you,
so I may still provoke the maidservants a while, and your mother;
and she in her grief will inquire of me closely on every matter.”
So he spoke, and Telemachus went through the hall and departed
onward into his chamber beneath the bright glare of the torches,
where indeed he would sleep in the past whenever sweet sleep came on.
There then he lay down also and waited for Dawn on her godly throne.
But in the hall there stayed behind noble Odysseus,
plotting the suitors’ slaughter together with Pallas Athena.
Then from her chamber came out circumspect and wise Penelope,
like to Artemis truly, or golden Aphrodite.
Beside the hearth they placed for her the chair where she used to be seated,
inlaid and turned with ivory and silver, which once a craftsman,
Icmalius, made, and beneath it he fixed a stool for the feet there,
joined to the chair itself, and over it broad fleece was thrown.
There then sat down circumspect and wise Penelope.
Then from the hall came in the white-armed women attendants.
They took away much bread from the boards, and the tables as well there,
and the cups from which those overweening men had been drinking.
Then from the braziers they cast the fire down onto the hearth-floor,
and heaped much wood upon them afresh, for light and for warming.
Then Melantho upbraided Odysseus a second time, saying:
“Stranger, will you still even now annoy us all through the night here,
wandering round through the house, and spying upon all the women?
Out through the doors with you, wretch, and be glad for the meal you have eaten,
or soon enough you will go when struck with a blazing torchbrand.”
Then with a lowering look wise Odysseus answered and said to her:
“Strange woman, why do you set on me with a spirit so angered?
Is it because I am filthy, and wear poor clothes on my body,
and because I beg through the land? For necessity drives me to do it.
Such indeed are the beggars and men who wander in sorrow.
I too once dwelt in a house among men in the old days,
blessed and wealthy, and often I gave to a vagrant who came there,
such as he might be, and whatever the need was that brought him.
Slaves I had beyond number, and many other possessions,
all by which men live well and are called prosperous and wealthy.
But Zeus son of Cronos destroyed it, since somehow it pleased him.
Therefore see to it, woman, lest you too lose all the splendor
by which now among all the maidservants you are distinguished;
lest at some time your mistress, angered, deal harshly against you,
or Odysseus return; for still there remains some hope of him.
But if indeed he has perished that way and no homecoming waits him,
still by Apollo's favor his son is already such now,
Telemachus; and no woman within these halls can escape him
doing some reckless wrong, since he is no longer so childish.”
So he spoke, and circumspect Penelope listened and heard him,
and she rebuked the handmaid and spoke out a word and addressed her:
“Certainly, shameless and bold one, bitch without fear, I perceive you
doing this great deed, which you will wipe from your own head surely.
For you knew everything well, since from my own mouth you had heard it,
how in my halls I intended to question the stranger
about my husband, since I am afflicted with careful grief for him.”
So she spoke, and then to the steward Eurynome said this:
“Eurynome, bring here a chair, and spread fleeces upon it,
so that the stranger may sit there and speak out his word and may hear me;
for I desire to question him closely and learn all about him.”
So she spoke, and the woman came quickly, eager in service,
brought a well-polished chair and threw on top of it fleeces;
then there sat down at last long-enduring noble Odysseus.
Then among them circumspect Penelope started speaking:
“Stranger, this first thing now I myself shall ask you directly:
who among men are you, and from where? Where are city and parents?”
Then resourceful Odysseus in answer spoke thus before her:
“Lady, no mortal anywhere over the boundless earth could
find any fault with you; for your fame reaches heaven's wide spaces,
like the fame of some blameless king, who in fear of the immortals
rules over many men, both numerous and mighty in valor,
and upholds justice; and under him the black earth is yielding
wheat and barley, and the trees are heavy and loaded with fruit there;
the flocks bear steady increase, and the sea provides men with fishes
through his good government, and the people flourish beneath him.
Therefore ask me now in your halls of all the other matters,
but do not question my lineage and fatherland any further,
lest you fill my spirit still more full of painful afflictions
as I remember; for I am indeed most burdened with sorrows.
It is not fitting that I in another man's house should be sitting
weeping and mourning; for grieving without cease is the worse thing.
Else some maidservant may take offense at me, or you as well may,
and may say that with wine I am making my mind swim in teardrops.”
Then circumspect Penelope in answer addressed him and told him:
“Stranger, indeed my beauty and form and comeliness wholly
were destroyed by the deathless gods when the Argives departed
sailing to Ilium, and with them went my husband Odysseus.
If only he might return and care for the life that is in me,
then would my fame be greater and thus would my beauty be fairer.
But now I am consumed with grief, for the god has driven so many
evils upon me. For all the best men who hold sway in the islands,
both in Dulichium and in Same and wooded Zacynthus,
and those who inhabit clear-seen Ithaca here all around me,
woo me against my will, and they are devouring the household.
Therefore I give no thought to strangers and suppliants coming,
nor even to heralds, who are workers in service of the people;
rather, in longing for dear Odysseus I waste away inwardly.
They are urgent for marriage, while I keep twisting out cunning.
First of all for me a god breathed this design in my spirit:
having set up in my halls a great loom, to weave there a garment,
fine-spun and very wide; and immediately I said to them:
“Young men, my suitors, since noble Odysseus has perished,
wait, though eager to marry me, until I finish this fabric,
lest the threads that I spin should perish and come to nothing utterly,
as a burial shroud for the hero Laertes, against the time when
deadly fate of destructive death shall at last overtake him;
lest some Achaean woman throughout the land should reproach me,
if a man of great wealth should lie there without any winding-sheet on.”
So I spoke, and their proud hearts in their breasts were persuaded.
Then by day I would weave at the great loom all through the daytime,
but by night I unraveled it, once I had set there the torches.
Thus for three years I escaped their notice and kept them persuaded;
but when the fourth year came and the seasons were passing upon us,
with the months wearing down and many long days completed,
then at last through my women, those bitches who cared for nothing,
they came upon me and caught me and chided me sharply with outcries.
So I completed it then, though unwillingly, under compulsion.
Now I can neither evade this marriage nor find some other
counsel; and my own parents are urgently pressing me to wed him,
while my son is distressed at the men who consume all his livelihood,
knowing it now; for already he is enough of a grown man
to care for his house, and to him Zeus grants honor and glory.
But even so, tell me your lineage, and from what place you come from;
for you are not sprung from the oak of old tale, nor from the rock.”
Then resourceful Odysseus made answer and spoke to her, saying:
“Revered wife of Odysseus, son of Laertes,
surely you will not cease from asking and probing my kindred?
Yet I will tell you indeed; though truly you give me over
to sorrows more than I bear; for this is the lot of a mortal,
when from the land of his fathers a man has long been absent,
wandering over the cities of men and suffering anguish.
Even so, I will tell what you ask me and press me to answer.
There is a certain land, Crete, in the midst of the wine-dark sea there,
fair and fertile, encircled by waters; and men are within it
many, past all numbering, and there are ninety cities.
Tongue is mingled with tongue; and among them are Achaeans,
and Eteocretans great-hearted, with Cydonian peoples beside them,
Dorians in their three tribes, and splendid Pelasgians.
There among them is Knossos, a mighty city, where Minos
ruled for nine-year seasons, companion of great Zeus in counsel,
father of my own father, great-hearted Deucalion.
Deucalion begot me and lordly Idomeneus with me;
but he in the curved ships had gone on his way unto Ilion,
going with Atreus’ sons; but my glorious name is Aethon,
younger by birth; but he was the elder and better man by far.
There I saw Odysseus, and I gave him the gifts of a stranger;
for the might of the wind had brought him down into Crete there,
as he was making for Troy, blown off his course past Malea.
He brought his ship to a halt at Amnisos, where is the cavern
of Eileithyia, in difficult harbors, and barely escaped the storm-blasts.
Straightway he went up into the town and inquired for Idomeneus;
for he said that he was his cherished and honored guest-friend.
But for him it was then already the tenth or eleventh dawn there
since he had gone with the curved ships onward inside unto Ilion.
I took him then to my house and gave him generous welcome,
warmly cherishing him, since abundant stores were within it;
and for the rest, for the comrades who followed along beside him,
from the public stores I collected meal and the glowing wine there,
and cattle to sacrifice, so that their hearts might be satisfied.
There for twelve days’ space the noble Achaeans remained there;
for the great North Wind penned them in, nor would let them on dry land
even stand; some hostile divinity had stirred it against them.
But on the thirteenth day the wind fell, and they launched on the seaway.”
So he spoke many falsehoods, making them all seem like truth there.
And as she listened, her tears kept flowing, her fair flesh was melting,
even as snow melts away on the peaks of the towering mountains,
snow that the East Wind has melted, when West Wind has heaped it upon them;
and as the snow is dissolving, the rivers are filled in their flowing:
so her beautiful cheeks were melting with tears as they poured there,
weeping for her own husband who sat there close beside her. Odysseus
in his heart had pity to see his own wife in her weeping,
but his eyes in his head stood fixed as if they were horn or iron,
motionless there beneath his lids; and in cunning he hid back his tear-drops.
But when she had fully her fill of tear-laden lamentation,
then once more she answered him, speaking aloud these words to him:
“Now, stranger, I think that I shall make trial of you in this matter,
whether in truth in your halls you received and gave welcome to my husband,
there with his godlike companions, as now you declare that you did.
Tell me what sort of garments he wore about his body,
and what manner of man he was, and the comrades who went on beside him.”
Then resourceful Odysseus made answer and spoke to her, saying:
“Lady, it is a hard thing, after so long an interval,
to tell it; for this is already the twentieth year fully
since from that place he departed and left my own country behind him.
Yet even so, I will tell you as my heart pictures him inwardly.
Noble Odysseus wore a mantle of wool, sea-purple,
doubled; and on it a brooch had been fashioned entirely of gold-work,
fitted with twin tubes for the pin; and before it a wonder was wrought there:
in its forepaws a hound held a dappled fawn, and was gripping it,
while it writhed; and all who beheld it kept marveling at it,
how, though made all of gold, the one gripped the fawn and was throttling it,
while the other, eager to flee, kept struggling hard with its forefeet.
I noticed too the tunic around his body, bright-shining,
like the skin that is over a dried-up onion in thinness;
so soft it was to the touch, and bright as the blaze of the sunlight.
Truly many women gazed upon him there and marveled.
And another thing I will tell you; do you lay it within your heart now:
I do not know if Odysseus wore these things when he left home,
or whether one of his comrades gave them aboard the swift vessel,
or perhaps some guest-friend gave them, since to many Odysseus
was beloved; for few among all the Achaeans could match him.
I too gave him a bronze sword there and a twofold mantle,
handsome and sea-dark purple, and a tunic with tassels,
and with all due honor I sent him aboard his good benched vessel.
And indeed a herald, a little the elder than he was,
went with him too; and I will tell you plainly what sort he was.
Rounded across the shoulders, dark of skin, and curly-headed,
Eurybates was his name; and Odysseus honored him greatly
over the rest of his comrades, because he knew his mind truly.
So he spoke, and in her he aroused still greater desire for weeping,
when she had recognized all the signs Odysseus described surely.
But when at last she had had her fill of the tearful lamenting,
then in reply she addressed him and spoke these winged words:
“Now then, stranger, though earlier you stirred my pity deeply,
in my halls from this time onward you shall be dear and respected.
For I myself gave him these garments, the very ones you mention,
folding them out from the chamber, and fastened the shining brooch there
for him to be a delight; but never again shall I greet him,
coming back to his home and his own dear fatherland's country.
By an evil fate in a hollow ship went great Odysseus
to look upon evil Ilios, not to be named among mortals.”
Then resourceful Odysseus in answer addressed her and said this:
“Revered wife of Laertes' son, of noble Odysseus,
do not now any longer consume your fair skin with your grieving,
neither melt your heart away as you mourn for your husband. I blame you
not in the least; for any woman laments for her husband,
lawfully wed, with whom in love she has borne her children,
even if he be less than Odysseus, whom they liken to godheads.
But now cease from your weeping, and listen closely to my story;
for I will tell you the truth exactly and will not deceive you:
I have already heard of Odysseus and his homecoming,
nearby, in the rich-settled land of the Thesprotian people,
living; and he is bringing abundant and splendid possessions,
begging his way through the land. Yet his trusty companions
he lost, and his shapely ship, on the wine-dark sea water,
coming away from the island Thrinacia; for against him
Zeus and Helios were angered, because his companions killed cattle.
They all perished together in the loud-resounding sea-surge;
but as for him, on the keel of the ship the wave cast him shoreward,
onto the land of the Phaeacians, close akin to the immortals.
They from the heart gave him honor even as if to a god there,
and they gave him many possessions, and wished of themselves too
to convey him home unharmed. And Odysseus long since
would have been here, but this seemed more gainful to his own spirit:
to gather wealth while passing over the face of much country.
So beyond all mortal men in the matter of profits and prudence
Odysseus knows many ways; no other man could contend with him.
So to me spoke Pheidon, the king of the Thesprotian people;
and he swore this oath to me there, pouring libation at homeward:
that the ship had been hauled down and the comrades stood ready,
those who would send him away to his own dear fatherland's country.
But me he dispatched before him; for there came there a vessel
of Thesprotian men, sailing to wheat-rich Dulichium.
And he showed me the treasure, all that Odysseus had gathered;
it would indeed maintain yet another man to the tenth generation,
so many lordly possessions were laid in the palace for him there.
But he said that he had gone to Dodona, there to listen
for the design of Zeus from the high-haired oak of the god there,
how he might come again to his own dear fatherland's country,
either openly now, or in secret, after so long a time absent.
Thus he is safe, and indeed he will come very soon now,
very near, no longer far from his friends and his fatherland's country;
yet even so upon this I will give you a solemn oath now.
Let Zeus now know it first, highest and best of the immortals,
and the hearth of noble Odysseus, to which I have now come here:
surely all these things will be brought to pass as I tell you.
Within this very circuit of time will Odysseus come hither,
as this month wanes away and the next one rises upon us.”
Then again thoughtful Penelope answered him, saying:
“Ah if only, stranger, this word might indeed be accomplished.
Then very soon you would know my friendship and many gifts from me,
so that whoever might meet you would call you blessed indeed there.
But in my heart I foresee it thus, and thus it shall happen:
neither will Odysseus come home, nor will you get an escort,
since there are no masters such as that in this household,
such as Odysseus was among men, if ever indeed he was one,
to receive honored strangers and send them on their journey.
But wash him now, handmaids, and prepare him a bedstead,
with bedding and cloaks and bright-glossed blankets upon it,
so that being warmly covered he may await Dawn's golden throne.
And at earliest morning bathe him and anoint him with olive oil,
so that indoors by Telemachus there he may mind his own supper,
seated within the great hall; and more grievous for any among them,
whoever afflicts him with soul-destroying taunting and malice:
here he will get nothing done hereafter, however fiercely enraged he.
For how, my guest, will you learn whether I surpass the other women
in good sense and in the thoughtful counsel alive in my spirit,
if in my halls you feast there unwashed and clad in foul raiment?
Men are but creatures that flourish a little while only.
One who is harsh in himself and who harbors harsh understanding,
on him all mortals call down pains yet to come while he liveth,
while he lives, and when he is dead all men make mock of him after.
But if a man is blameless himself and knows what is blameless,
then strangers abroad bear wide the fame of that man among all men,
carrying it over mankind, and many declare he is noble.”
Then resourceful Odysseus answered her, saying in turn:
“Revered wife of Odysseus, son of Laertes, hear me:
truly to me cloaks now and shining blankets have grown hateful
from the time when I first left behind me the snowy hillsides of Crete,
setting forth in the long-oared ship on the course of my journey;
and I will lie down now as before through many unsleeping nighttimes.
For indeed many nights on an unlovely couch I have suffered,
lying awake and awaiting divine Dawn seated on her fair throne.
Nor at all does the washing of feet bring delight to my spirit,
neither shall any woman lay hold on this foot that is mine here,
none of the women who serve as attendants throughout your household,
unless there be some old woman, long-tried, who knoweth what is fitting,
one who has borne in her heart as many afflictions as I have;
such a woman I would not begrudge if she handled my feet here.”
Then thoughtful Penelope answered him, speaking in turn:
“Dear guest, never yet has a man of far-wandering strangers,
one so wise in discernment, come on to my house as a loved one,
for with such clear understanding you speak every word that is prudent.
There is an old woman with me who has shrewdness stored in her spirit,
she who nursed that unhappy man well and cherished and reared him,
taking him in her hands when first his mother had borne him.
She shall wash your feet, though age has made her feeble and weakened.
But come now, arise, prudent Eurycleia, and hear me:
wash a man of your master’s own age; for perhaps now Odysseus
has by this time such feet, and such hands, as belong to this stranger;
for in misfortune mortals grow old very quickly indeed.”
So then she spoke, and the old woman covered her face with her two hands,
and she shed warm tears, and uttered a mournful saying:
“Ah me, my child, I am helpless for you; in truth Zeus above all men
hated you, though you held in your breast a god-fearing spirit.
For no other mortal ever burned for Zeus who delighteth in thunder
so many rich thigh-pieces, or offered such splendid hecatombs,
as you gave unto him, praying that you might come to a season
of sleek old age, and might rear your glorious son to manhood.
But now from you alone has he utterly taken the day of returning.
So, I suppose, women in foreign houses mocked also at that other,
women of far-off strangers, whenever he reached some glorious dwelling,
just as all these hounds here are mocking at you, every one of them.
Therefore avoiding the insult and many disgraceful affrontings
you did not let them wash you; but me, not unwilling at all here,
the daughter of Icarius, prudent Penelope, has ordered.
Therefore your feet I shall wash, for Penelope’s sake and your own too,
and because for your sake my heart is stirred deep within me
by griefs. But come now, attend to the word that now I shall tell you:
many much-suffering strangers indeed have arrived here before us,
yet never so far do I say I have looked on one so resembling
Odysseus in body and voice and feet as you now resemble him.”
Then resourceful Odysseus answered her, speaking in turn:
“Old woman, so say all those who have looked with their own eyes upon us,
both of us together, that we are exceedingly like one another,
even as you yourself in your careful thought have spoken and judged it.”
So then he spoke, and the old woman took up a beautiful basin,
that all-shining vessel wherein she washed feet, and she poured in
much cold water, and after that added the hot for the washing.
Meanwhile Odysseus sat down close by the hearth and quickly thereafter
turned himself toward the dark; for straightway he thought in his spirit
she might feel and perceive the scar, and all would become manifest.
Then she came near and began to wash her lord, and at once she knew it,
knew the scar that a boar once gave him by striking with white tusk,
when he had gone to Parnassus to Autolycus and his sons there,
to the noble father of his own mother, who far surpassed all mortals
both in thievery and in the oath; and a god himself gave these to him,
Hermes, since he would burn to him well-pleasing thigh-pieces of victims,
lambs and kids; and the god went gladly beside him and helped him.
Once Autolycus came to the fertile land and the people of Ithaca,
and he found there the son newly born of his own dear daughter.
Then Eurycleia placed the child on the knees of his loved one,
when he had ceased from supper, and spoke a word and addressed him:
“Autolycus, you yourself must now find what name you will give him,
your own dear daughter’s child; for he has been prayed for by many.”
Then in return Autolycus answered her, speaking this to her:
“Son-in-law dear and my daughter, then give him the name that I tell you.
Since I have come here burdened with wrath against many people,
both against men and against women throughout the grain-giving wide earth,
therefore let Odysseus be the name by which he is called now.
And for my part, whenever he comes to the vigor of manhood
and to Parnassus, the great house of his mother’s people,
there I will give him gifts and send him homeward rejoicing.”
For the sake of those things Odysseus came, to receive noble presents.
Then Autolycus himself and the sons of Autolycus round him
clasped his hands in welcome and greeted him with gentle sayings;
and Amphithea his grandmother, mother of his own mother,
folded Odysseus in her arms and kissed his head and both bright eyes there.
Then did Autolycus call to his glorious sons in the hall there,
bidding them furnish the feast; and they listened and heard his urging.
Straightway then they led in a bull, a male in his fifth year.
This one they flayed and dressed, and they carved up all of his body,
skillfully jointed the meat, and ran the pieces through spits there,
roasted them well with care, and divided to each man his portion.
So then all through the day until the sun went down in the evening
they feasted, and no heart lacked anything of the equal banquet.
But when the sun went down and the darkness came on in the twilight,
then indeed they lay down and took for themselves sleep’s gift.
But when Dawn had appeared, the young one, rose-fingered Eos,
forth they went to the hunt, both the dogs and the men themselves also,
even the sons of Autolycus; and noble Odysseus
went there among them. They climbed the steep mountain all mantled
deep in forest, Parnassus, and quickly they reached windy foldings.
Then did the Sun strike fresh on the tilled fields of mortal men there,
rising from out of the deep-swirling stream of Ocean,
and they came to a glen, the beaters; and before them the hounds there,
searching the tracks, went on; and behind them followed in order
all of the sons of Autolycus; and noble Odysseus
went with them close by the hounds, brandishing his long-shadowing spear.
There in a dense thicket a huge wild boar lay couched and hidden.
Into that place no force of the wet-blowing winds ever entered,
neither did shining Sun with his beams ever strike through upon it,
neither could rain pass through from one side straight on to the other,
so exceedingly dense it was, and leaves lay fallen there thickly.
Then there came round the boar the clatter of feet of the hunters,
both of men and of dogs, as they pressed on; and out of the thicket
facing them rose he, his ridge-bristles lifting in terror,
fire in his eyes; and he stood there very close to them. First then
rushed in Odysseus, lifting on high in his stout hand the long spear,
eager to wound him; but first the boar struck him in onset,
just above on the knee, and with slanting rush of his tusk there
tore away much flesh, but he did not reach to the bone of the hero.
Yet Odysseus struck him, hitting him square on the right shoulder,
and right through passed the point of the bright-shining spear in its fury.
Down in the dust he fell with a grunt, and the spirit flew from him.
Then did the dear sons of Autolycus busy themselves round him,
and the wound of Odysseus, the noble and godlike fighter,
they bound up well with skill, and with charm-song stanched the black blood there,
and swiftly then they came again to the house of their own dear father.
Then Autolycus himself and the sons of Autolycus round him,
after they healed him well and bestowed on him splendid presents,
sent him off in all haste, rejoicing, back to his own land,
unto Ithaca. There his father and queenly mother
welcomed his homecoming gladly and asked him about every matter,
how he had gotten the scar; and he told them the whole tale clearly,
how while hunting the boar had struck him with white tusk in onset,
when he had gone to Parnassus with sons of Autolycus.
That scar the old woman, taking his leg in the palms of her two hands,
knew as she touched it and felt it, and let the foot fall from her holding.
Then in the basin the shin fell down, and the bronze gave a clanging;
over the other side it tipped, and the water poured on the ground there.
Joy and anguish together seized all the thoughts in her bosom;
tears filled both of her eyes, and her vigorous voice was arrested.
Then she touched Odysseus beneath the chin and spoke out to him, saying:
“Surely you are Odysseus, dear child; and I did not know you
before I had touched and handled my master all over, completely.”
So she spoke and cast her eyes toward Penelope, wishing
to make it known that her own dear husband was there in the hall now.
But she could not look straight back at her nor yet understand it;
for Athena had turned away the mind of Penelope from it.
Then Odysseus, feeling toward her throat, seized hold with his right hand,
With the other hand he drew her nearer and spoke out to her:
“Nurse, why are you willing to ruin me? You yourself reared me
there at your own breast; and now, after bearing much sorrow,
in the twentieth year I have come to the land of my fathers.
But since you have found me, and some god put it into your spirit,
keep still, lest any other man in the halls come to know it.
For thus I will tell you, and surely the thing will be finished:
if a god should subdue the proud suitors beneath my own hands here,
not even you, though you are my nurse, will I leave unpunished
when I kill the women, the serving-slaves, here in my hall.”
Then in reply spoke to him thoughtful Eurycleia:
“My child, what sort of word has escaped through the fence of your teeth there!
You know what manner of strength in me stands firm and unyielding;
I will endure like a stone, like iron that never is broken.
But another thing I will tell you; now lay it away in your spirit:
if a god should subdue the proud suitors beneath your own hands here,
then I will tell you the women in hall, every one of them,
which ones dishonor you, and which are free from all guilt there.”
Then resourceful Odysseus answered and said in reply to her:
“Nurse, why do you speak of them? There is no need for you to.
I myself will observe them well, and I will know every woman.
But keep this matter in silence, and leave the rest to the gods.”
So he spoke, and the old woman passed through the hall and departed,
going to bring the foot-water; for all of the first had been spilled out.
But when she had washed him and anointed him richly with olive,
back again Odysseus pulled the chair somewhat nearer the fire,
warming himself, and he covered the scar with his tattered garments.
Then among them thoughtful Penelope started her speaking:
“Stranger, I myself will ask you one little thing more only.
For soon will come the hour of delightful rest and of slumber
for whatever man sweet sleep may take, though burdened with sorrow.
But to me some god has allotted grief beyond all measure.
In the daytime indeed I find relief in weeping and wailing,
looking to my own tasks and the tasks of the maids in the household.
But whenever night comes on and sleep has taken all others,
then I lie in my bed, and around my throbbing heart there
keen and crowded anxieties goadingly trouble me, mourning.
As when Pandareus’ daughter, the nightingale green in the thicket,
sings out beautifully when spring is newly beginning,
sitting within the dense-leafed cover and foliage of trees there,
and often varying pours out her far-resounding song-notes,
mourning her child, beloved Itylus, whom once with the bronze blade
she killed in her witlessness, child of lord Zethus the ruler:
so also my own heart is divided and driven two ways here,
whether I stay by my son and guard all things in their order,
my possessions, my serving-women, and my high-roofed great dwelling,
honoring the marriage-bed of my husband and public opinion,
or at last follow with one of the Achaeans, whoever is finest,
one who courts me within these halls and brings me boundless bride-gifts.
While my son still was small, a mere child and childish in thinking,
he would not let me be wed and leave the house of my husband.
But now, when he is grown great and has come to manhood’s measure,
now he begs me again to go back from the house and depart from it,
angered over the livelihood that the Achaeans consume up.
But come now, hear this dream of mine and interpret it for me.
I have geese in my house, twenty of them, and they feed upon wheat
out of the water, and I take delight in watching them daily.
Then from the mountain there came a great eagle with hooked beak,
and he snapped all their necks and killed them, and they lay there
huddled in heaps through the halls, while he rose into bright upper ether.
Then I wept and wailed aloud, though indeed it was in a dream only;
and around me there gathered the fair-haired Achaean women,
while I lamented in pity because the eagle had killed my geese there.
But then he came back and sat on the jutting beam of the roof there,
and with the voice of a mortal he checked me and spoke out to me:
“Take courage, daughter of Icarius famous in far lands.
This is no dream, but a waking vision, a good one, accomplished.
The geese are the suitors, and I was before this the eagle,
but now I have come back again, and I am your own husband,
who upon all the suitors will bring an unsightly destruction.”
So he spoke, and the honey-sweet sleep released me and left me.
Looking about, I saw the geese there within the great hall-room,
pecking at wheat by the trough, where before they had always been feeding.”
Then resourceful Odysseus answered and said in reply to her:
“Lady, there is no way to interpret this dream by turning it
to some other meaning, since Odysseus himself has made plain to you
how it will be brought to fulfillment; and ruin appears for the suitors,
all of them utterly; not one will escape death and destruction.”
Then in reply spoke to him thoughtful Penelope:
“Stranger, dreams indeed are baffling things, and their sayings are tangled;
and not all things that they show find fulfillment among mortals.
For there are two gates, men say, for the insubstantial dream-shapes;
one pair is fashioned of horn, and the other is fashioned of ivory.
Those dreams, to be sure, that come by the way of sawn ivory,
these are deceptive and cheat us, bearing a message unfulfilled;
but the ones that come forth through the gates of polished horn there,
these bring truth to pass, whenever some mortal beholds them.
But as for me, I do not suppose that my terrible dream came
out from that place; how welcome a thing it would be for my child and me.
Yet another thing I will tell you, and lay it away in your spirit:
now indeed there comes this dawn of evil name, which from the house
of Odysseus will sever me; for now I shall set down a contest,
those axes which he in the hall of his house would set up in order,
all twelve of them, set in a row like timbers that prop up a vessel;
then standing far off he would shoot an arrow clear through them.
Now before all the suitors I shall proclaim this trial:
whoever most easily strings the bow with the strength of his hands,
and drives an arrow straight through all the twelve axes together,
with him then I shall go, leaving behind this house here,
my wedded home, very fair, and filled with the means of our living;
that house, I believe, I shall still remember even in dreaming.”
Then resourceful Odysseus answered and spoke in reply to her:
“Revered wife of Odysseus, son of Laertes in glory,
do not any longer delay this contest here in your dwelling;
for before they can do it, resourceful Odysseus will come here,
before these men, while handling this well-polished bow in their fingers,
string the cord on it tight and shoot the shaft clean through the iron.”
Then in turn wise-minded Penelope answered and spoke to him:
“Stranger, if you were willing to sit here beside me in hall
and delight me, then sleep would never be shed on my eyelids.
But there is no means for human beings to stay without sleeping
always; for on each thing the immortals have set down a portion
for us mortals who live on the grain-giving face of the ploughland.
Therefore now I shall go up into my upper chamber
and lie down upon that bed which has been made a bed of mourning,
always befouled by my tears, from the hour when Odysseus
went away to behold accursed Ilios, never named rightly.
There I shall lie; and you must lie down somewhere in this house here,
either by spreading a bed on the floor, or having them make one.”
So she spoke, and she went up to her silent upper chamber,
not alone, for other handmaids went there together beside her.
And when she had gone to the upper room with her serving women,
there she wept for Odysseus, her beloved husband, until
bright-eyed Athena cast sweet sleep down over her eyelids.
