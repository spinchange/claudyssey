And a portion beside Odysseus the servants set down there,
equal to what they themselves had obtained as allotted among them;

for so Telemachus ordered, dear son of godlike Odysseus.

Yet Athena did not let the haughty suitors at all then
hold back from the bitter abuse that gives pain to the spirit,
so that still deeper grief might sink in the heart of Odysseus.

There among those suitors was one man knowing unlawful behavior;

Ctesippus was his name, and in Same his dwelling was founded.

He, relying indeed on possessions exceeding in splendor,
wooed the wife of Odysseus, though long he had been gone from home there.

He then spoke out among the overbearing suitors and said thus:

“Hear me, lordly suitors, so I may speak out a word to you.

Long now the stranger has had his portion, equal to all men,
as is fitting;

for it is neither noble nor just to dishonor
any guest of Telemachus who may come to this house here.

But come, I also will give him a guest-gift, so that he likewise
may give some prize to the bath-attendant woman, or perhaps to
some other slave in the house of Odysseus, godlike in spirit.”
So he spoke and with his heavy hand he hurled an ox-hoof at him,
taking it up from the basket where it was lying;

Odysseus
dodged it by bending his head a little aside, and he smiled then
inwardly with a sardonic smile;

and it struck the strong wall there.

Then with words Telemachus sharply rebuked Ctesippus before them:

“Ctesippus, surely this turned out much better thus for your spirit:

you did not hit the stranger, for he himself dodged the missile.

Else with my sharp spear I would have struck you square in the middle,
and your father would busy himself with a tomb here, not wedding.

Therefore let no one show me outrage here in my own household.

For by now I perceive and I know every matter before me,
both the good and the worse;

but before this I still was childish.

Yet even so we endure these things as we look on and suffer:

sheep being slaughtered, and wine being drunk, and bread being consumed here;

for it is hard for one man to restrain and rebuke so many.

Come then, no longer work me these evils in hostile intention.

But if now you are eager to kill me myself with the bronze blade,
even that I would choose, and it would be far better for me then
to have died than forever to look on these shameful actions,
guests being brutally handled, and women servants as well being
dragged in disgrace through the beautiful rooms of the house there.”
Thus he spoke, and they all became wholly silent in stillness.

Then at the last Damastor's son, Agelaus, addressed them:

“Friends, no man could justly take offense at a fair-spoken saying
and answer back in contention, fastening on it with harsh words.

Do not be bullying this stranger at all, nor any other
of the household slaves in the halls of godlike Odysseus.

But to Telemachus I would speak, and also his mother,
speaking gently, if this might please the hearts of both of them.

So long as your spirit within your breasts still kept on hoping
wise Odysseus would return again to this house here,
so long there was no blame if she waited and held off
these suitors here in the house, since this indeed was the better,
if Odysseus returned and came back home to his dwelling.

But now this much is plain:

he is no longer returning.

Come then, sit beside your mother and tell her these matters:

let her wed whatever man is best and offers the most there,
so that you may rejoice and possess all your father's belongings,
eating and drinking, while she keeps the house of another.”
Then in reply to him spoke prudent Telemachus clearly:
