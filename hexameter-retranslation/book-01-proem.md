# Book 1: Proem (1.1–10)

## Reading text

1  Tell me, O Muse, of the versatile man, who was driven to wander
2  after he ravaged the god-hallowed fortress—the city of Trojans.
3  Many the cities of men that he saw, and he learned of their thinking;
4  many the griefs he endured on the sea, in the depths of his own heart,
5  striving to save his own life and to bring his companions back homeward.
6  Still he could never deliver his comrades, for all of his striving:
7  for it was they, by their own witless outrage, who brought on their ruin—
8  fools, who had eaten the cattle of Helios, high-going Sun-god.
9  Helios robbed them of homecoming's day—the return to their homeland.
10 Choose where to start it, O goddess, O daughter of Zeus, and now tell us.

## Notes on the test

- **1 — πολύτροπον:** “Versatile” keeps the word's range—resourceful,
  adaptable, capable of turning many ways—while supplying a natural dactyl.
  “Was driven to wander” retains the passivity of πλάγχθη: wandering is
  something inflicted on the man as well as something he does.
- **2 — ἱερὸν πτολίεθρον:** “God-hallowed fortress—the city” treats the two
  English nouns as appositional, not as two separate places.
- **3–4 — νόον / θυμόν:** “Thinking” is deliberately plainer than “mind,”
  while “his own heart” keeps the inward force of the second phrase.
- **5 and 9 — νόστος:** The repeated *home-* language is intentional. The
  companions' desired return in line 5 becomes the homecoming-day taken from
  them in line 9.
- **7 — ἀτασθαλίῃσιν:** “Witless outrage” tries to hold together the word's
  folly, recklessness, and moral violence.
- **8 — Ὑπερίονος Ἠελίοιο:** “Helios, high-going Sun-god” preserves both the
  name and the force of the traditional “Hyperion” epithet without forcing its
  English pronunciation against the meter.
- **10 — τῶν ἁμόθεν:** “Choose where to start” makes explicit the Muse's freedom
  to enter the story at any point, the principle on which the poem immediately
  begins *in medias res*.
