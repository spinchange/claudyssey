# The Odyssey — Book 10

*Aeolus and the bag of winds; the Laestrygonians; a year with Circe.*

1 Next we arrived at Aeolian island, where Aeolus made his
2 home, Hippotas’ son, who was dear to the deathless immortals.
3 Floating the island remained; all around it a wall had been fashioned,
4 bronze and unbreakable, while from the sea rose the cliff without foothold.
5 Twelve of his children were born and were living with him in the palace,
6 six were daughters and six were sons in the flower of manhood;
7 daughters he gave to his sons to possess as their wives in the household.
8 Always they feast at the side of their dear and beloved father
9 also their reverend mother, and good things uncounted are near them.
10 All day long is the house filled with savory fragrance, the courtyard
11 ringing with sound; but at nighttime they sleep with their reverend women,
12 lying in blankets together on well-corded bedsteads in chambers.
13 Into their city we came and the beautiful house of Aeolus.
14 One month long he attended me well and inquired about all things,
15 Ilium, Argive vessels, and homeward return of Achaeans;
16 all of the tale in appropriate order I told and related.
17 But when I asked in my turn for the road and entreated his sending,
18 nothing did he refuse, but prepared my return to the homeland.
19 And he presented a bag made out of the flayed hide of one great
20 ox nine years old, and inside he confined all the blustering windstorms.
21 Son of great Cronos had made him the steward who ruled over all winds,
22 either to halt or arouse any wind as his spirit desired it.
23 There in the hollow ship he secured it with shining and silver
24 cord, so that never a breath might blow past it, however little.
25 But he released for my passage the blowing breath of the West Wind,
26 carrying all of the vessels and men, but the purpose was never
27 fated to come to completion; our folly was destined to ruin us.
28 Nine days long we continued our sailing by daytime and nighttime;
29 then on the tenth day the fields of our fathers were rising before us,
30 close, and we saw human people attending the fires they had kindled.
31 Then sweet sleep came down on me, exhausted by ceaseless exertion;
32 always alone I continued to manage the sheet of the vessel,
33 giving it over to no other comrade, to hasten our homeward arrival.
34 Yet my companions began to converse with each other around me,
35 saying that silver and gold I was carrying into my homeland,
36 given as gifts by the great-hearted lord Aeolus, son of Hippotas.
37 So would a man glance aside at his neighbor and speak in this manner:
38 “See how greatly he always is loved and receives every honor
39 out of humanity, whatever the city or country he comes to.
40 See what abundance of beautiful treasure he carries from Troy-land,
41 all of his plunder, while we who have finished the selfsame long journey
42 come to our country with nothing at all in our hands from the labor.
43 Now lord Aeolus gave him these things out of friendship and favor.
44 Come, let us quickly examine the bag and discover its contents,
45 learning how much of the gold and the silver the bag was containing.”
46 So they spoke, and prevailed the wicked counsel of comrades.
47 Opening up the great bag, they released every wind from its binding;
48 straightway the storm winds seized them and swept them away over water,
49 weeping, afar from their fathers’ land; and I, suddenly waking,
50 pondered there in the depth of my own great and blameless spirit
51 whether to drop from the ship and be drowned in the salt-colored water,
52 or to endure it in silence and stay there among those still living.
53 Yet I endured it and stayed, and covered my own head over,
54 down in the ship I lay, while the vessels were carried by evil
55 blast of the storm to Aeolian island, my comrades lamenting.
56 There we set foot on the dry land and drew up an ample supply of
57 water, and quickly my men took their meal at the side of the swift ships.
58 Now when we tasted our measure of food and the drink we were given,
59 taking a herald beside me and one of the men as companion,
60 into the famous house of Aeolus I went, and I found him
61 feasting beside both his wife and the children who lived in his household.
62 Into the house we entered and sat by the jambs of the doorway,
63 there on the threshold; they marveled at heart and inquired of my journey:
64 “Why have you come here, Odysseus? What evil divinity harmed you?
65 Surely we sent you with every provision, so you might arrive in
66 your fatherland and your house or whatever location is dearest unto you.”
67 So they spoke, and I answered them while in my heart I was grieving:
68 “Evil companions have ruined me, they and a pitiless slumber.
69 Friends, bring a cure, since the power to do so is held in your own hands.”
70 So I spoke and entreated them, using words that were gentle and soothing;
71 they became silent, and then the old father addressed me in answer:
72 “Out of the island at once, most despicable creature now living!
73 It is against what is right that I welcome or send on his journey
74 anyone hated this way by the gods who are blessed and immortal.
75 Out, since you come to this island abhorred by the gods everlasting.”
76 So having spoken, he sent me from his halls, heavily groaning.
77 There we continued our sailing away with the heart full of sorrow,
78 while the strength of the men was destroyed by the wearisome rowing,
79 all our own fault, since no following wind would appear for our voyage.
80 Six days long we continued our sailing by daytime and nighttime;
81 then on the seventh day we came to the steep-built citadel Lamus,
82 Telepylus in the land of Laestrygonians, where one herdsman
83 driving the livestock in hails the herdsman who drives outward.
84 There an unsleeping man could have earned for himself double wages,
85 one wage tending the cattle and one for the silver-white sheep-flocks,
86 since very close are the paths that are followed by nighttime and daylight.
87 There we came into the glorious harbor, encircled by one cliff,
88 sheer and unbroken, extending around it on opposite margins;
89 two jutting headlands are standing across from each other and reach out
90 there at the mouth, and a narrow passage provides the sole entrance.
91 All of the others directed their curving vessels inside it,
92 mooring within the hollow harbor the ships in a close line;
93 never a wave was uplifted within it, whether enormous
94 or very small, but a shining calm spread outward around them.
95 Only myself held the black ship out of the mouth of the harbor,
96 there at its edge, and I fastened the cables secure to the cliff rock.
97 Up to a broken height I ascended and there took my station;
98 nowhere the labors of oxen or mortals appeared to our eyesight;
99 only we saw smoke rising aloft from the land in the distance.
100 Then I sent several companions ahead of the others to find out
101 what kind of men upon the earth were eating their bread-loaves.
102 Two men I chose for the journey and added a third as their herald.
103 Out on the land they went and followed a level road, where the wagons
104 carried the wood to the town from the towering crests of the mountains.
105 There before reaching the city they met a young woman who drew down
106 water, the strong daughter born to Laestrygonian Antiphates.
107 She had descended to Artacia’s beautifully flowing
108 spring, out of which all the people were carrying water townward.
109 Coming beside her, they spoke to the girl and inquired of the ruler,
110 who was the king of the people and who were the people he governed.
111 Straightway she pointed the high-roofed house of her father before them.
112 When they had entered the glorious house, they discovered the king’s wife,
113 huge as the peak of a mountain, and felt an abhorrence in seeing.
114 Straightway she called from the meeting place the renowned Antiphates,
115 husband who married her, and he devised their pitiful ruin.
116 Seizing one man from among my companions, he made him his dinner;
117 other two men leaped away and flew to the ships in their terror.
118 But through the town he raised up the cry of the war; when they heard it,
119 mighty Laestrygonian men came in throngs from all directions,
120 thousands, and not like humanity, rather resembling the Giants.
121 Down from the cliffs they were casting great stones with the weight of a strong man;
122 straightway there rose from the ships an evil and terrible clamor,
123 screaming of dying men and the crushing of vessels to pieces.
124 Spearing the men there like fish, they carried their hideous dinner.
125 While they destroyed all the people inside of the deep-sided harbor,
126 then I drew out the sharp-edged sword that I carried beside my
127 thigh, cutting all of the cables that held the dark-blue-prowed vessel.
128 Rapidly then I aroused my companions and gave them commandment,
129 telling them all to bend into oars and escape from the evil;
130 all of them tore up the sea at once in their terror of dying.
131 Gladly my own ship fled to the open sea-water and safety,
132 leaving the beetling cliffs; all the other vessels were ruined.
133 There we continued our sailing away with the heart full of sorrow,
134 glad to be free from death though our well-loved comrades had perished.
135 Unto Aeaea’s island we came, where beautiful-haired and
136 terrible Circe resided, a goddess with voice like a mortal,
137 sister by blood of Aeetes, the god with a dangerous spirit;
138 both had been born to the light-giving Helios, joined with their mother
139 Perse, herself a daughter whom Ocean brought into being.
140 There we brought in the vessel without any sound to the shoreline,
141 into a ship-holding harbor, and some god directed our passage.
142 There we disembarked from the ship, for two days and two nights together,
143 down we lay, consuming our hearts with exhaustion and sorrow.
144 Yet when the beautiful-haired Dawn brought the third day upon us,
145 then I took hold of the spear and the sharp-edged sword that I carried,
146 climbing away from the ship to a place for observing the country,
147 hoping to see works made by humanity, hoping for voices.
148 Up to a broken height I ascended and there took my station;
149 smoke I perceived as it rose from the earth with its widely traveled
150 roads, in the halls of the goddess through thick-growing brush and the timber.
151 Then in the depth of my mind and my heart I considered the question,
152 whether to go and examine the place where the fire-bright smoke rose.
153 Yet as I pondered, this seemed in my thinking the better decision:
154 first to return to the ship on the shore of the salt-colored water,
155 give my companions a meal and then send men to learn of the country.
156 Yet when I traveled the road and came near to the curve-sided vessel,
157 then some divinity pitied my loneliness there and provided
158 one tall-antlered and mighty stag in the road where I traveled.
159 Out of the forested pasture the stag was descending to river,
160 there for his drink, since the strength of the sun beat down on his body.
161 As he stepped forth from the woodland, I struck him full in the backbone;
162 clean through the stag did my bronze spear run and emerge on the far side.
163 Down in the dust with a cry he dropped, and his spirit departed.
164 Setting my foot on his body, I pulled out the bronze-headed spearpoint,
165 out of the wound, and just where I stood I laid it earthward.
166 But I pulled upward the brushwood and gathered the flexible willow,
167 plaiting a rope that was one fathom long and was thoroughly twisted,
168 binding together the feet of the beast with the mighty proportions.
169 Down to the black-hulled ship I returned with the stag on my shoulders,
170 leaning my weight on the spear, since never upon a single shoulder,
171 using one hand, could I carry the creature; the beast was enormous.
172 Down by the ship I cast it and roused all the men from their sorrow,
173 using the gentle words as beside every man I was standing:
174 “Friends, though we grieve, we shall not yet descend to the house of great Hades,
175 not till the day that is destined. Come then, while food and provisions
176 still are retained in the swift ship, let us attend to our eating,
177 lest being empty of food we are worn and destroyed by our hunger.”
178 So I spoke, and they rapidly gave their attention and answered;
179 taking the cloaks from their heads, by the shore of the sea that was barren,
180 all of them marveled to see the great stag, for the beast was enormous.
181 After their hearts had taken full pleasure in gazing upon it,
182 washing their hands, they prepared and made ready the glorious banquet.
183 So then all day long till the sun and the light were descending,
184 there we sat feasting on meat past counting and sweet wine beside it.
185 Then the sun entered the west and the darkness descended around us;
186 there we lay down to sleep on the surf-line there at the seashore.
187 When early Dawn had appeared with her beautiful rose-colored fingers,
188 then I called all my men in assembly and spoke to them openly:
189 “Hear now my words, my companions, although you are greatly afflicted.
190 Friends, we know not where darkness is found or the Dawn where she rises,
191 neither where Sun who gives light to humanity enters beneath the
192 earth nor where he arises. Come, let us consider, and quickly,
193 whether a plan is remaining for us; I do not believe it.
194 Up to a broken height I ascended and saw from its outlook
195 one island lying encircled by boundless expanses of ocean;
196 low is the island itself, and the smoke in the midst of the island
197 rose to my eyes from the brush that was thick and the timber around it.”
198 So I spoke, and the hearts in the depth of their bodies were broken,
199 holding in mind deeds done by Laestrygonian Antiphates,
200 also the violent deed of the man-eating mighty Cyclops.
201 Loudly they wept, and the great tears flowed from their eyes in abundance,
202 yet no advantage or use was accomplished by all their lamenting.
203 Now I divided the whole of my strong-greaved men who attended
204 into two groups, and provided a leader to each of the parties.
205 I led one band, and the other was led by the godlike Eurylochus.
206 Quickly we shook all the lots in a helmet constructed of bronze-work;
207 out leaped the lot that belonged to the great-hearted lord Eurylochus.
208 Off he departed, and two-and-twenty men with him traveled,
209 weeping, and leaving us weeping as well when they went on their journey.
210 There in a forested glen they discovered the palace of Circe,
211 built out of polished stones in a place that commanded the landscape.
212 Mountain-bred wolves were all around it and lions beside them,
213 beasts she herself had bewitched by the evil drugs she was giving.
214 Yet they attacked not the men or arose in a violent onslaught;
215 standing, they fawned all around them and waved their long tails in excitement.
216 Just as the dogs fawn around their master returning from banquet,
217 since he is always accustomed to carry some sweet-tasting morsels,
218 so did the strongly clawed wolves and the lions fawn all around them;
219 they at the sight of the huge and terrible creatures were frightened.
220 There in the gateway they stood of the beautiful-haired shining goddess;
221 inside they heard the voice of Circe, beautifully singing,
222 moving around at a great loom, deathless and never decaying,
223 weaving the fine-spun, beautiful, graceful, and shining work of a goddess.
224 Speaking among them, Polites began to address his companions,
225 leader of people and closest to me and the dearest companion:
226 “Friends, there is someone inside by the mighty loom who is moving,
227 beautifully singing, and all of the floor of the palace is humming,
228 whether a goddess or mortal; come, let us call to her loudly.”
229 So he spoke, and they lifted their voices and called to the woman.
230 Straightway she came from the palace and opened the beautiful doorway,
231 calling them inward; in innocence all of the men went behind her.
232 Only Eurylochus stayed at the back, for he feared a deception.
233 Into the palace she led them and seated them there upon benches;
234 cheese and barley meal and the pale-colored honey she stirred them,
235 mixed into Pramnian wine, while she blended within it a measure of
236 ruinous drugs, so the men should forget the beloved land of their fathers.
237 Then, when she gave it and they drank down what she offered completely,
238 striking them there with her wand, she confined them within the pigsties.
239 Heads of the pigs they possessed and the voice and the bristles and body;
240 minds were unaltered within them and stayed as they had been beforehand.
241 So they were penned while they wept; and Circe provided them acorns,
242 fruit of the oak and the ilex and cornel berries for feeding,
243 such food as pigs who lie on the ground are continually eating.
244 Straightway Eurylochus came running back to the swift black vessel,
245 bringing the news of our comrades and all of their bitter destruction.
246 Yet not a word could he bring himself forth, though he strained to be speaking,
247 stricken at heart by the anguish; his eyes were filled up with teardrops,
248 while his spirit was set upon nothing besides lamentation.
249 Yet when we all in amazement inquired about what had befallen,
250 then he related the ruin of all our companions who traveled:
251 “We went off through the brush as commanded by glorious lord Odysseus;
252 there in a forested glen we discovered a beautiful palace,
253 built out of polished stones in a place that commanded the landscape.
254 There by the side of a great loom someone was singing with clear voice,
255 whether a goddess or mortal; they lifted their voices and called her.
256 Straightway she came from the palace and opened the beautiful doorway,
257 calling them inward; in innocence all of the men went behind her.
258 Yet I remained at the back, since I feared there might be a deception.
259 All of the men disappeared there together, and none of them ever
260 came to my eyesight again, though long I was sitting and watching.”
261 So he spoke; and around my shoulders I slung the great silver-
262 studded bronze sword and the bow, and commanded the man to return and
263 lead me again by the selfsame road on which he had traveled.
264 Yet he embraced both my knees, taking hold with his two hands together;
265 wailing aloud, he addressed me, and these words took wing as he uttered:
266 “Zeus-born, do not now take me back there against my intention; leave me.
267 I know you shall not come back by yourself or return with another
268 one of the men you attend. With those who remain let us hurry,
269 fleeing, since even now we may still get away from the death-day.”
270 So he spoke, and I spoke to the man and provided an answer:
271 “Eurylochus, stay here yourself, remain right where you are standing,
272 eating and drinking beside the hollow and black-hulled vessel.
273 Yet I shall go, for a mighty necessity lies on my body.”
274 So having spoken, I went from the vessel and shore of the sea-way.
275 Just as I traveled the sacred glens and was nearly arriving
276 at the mighty palace of Circe, the mistress of manifold potions,
277 Hermes who carries the golden wand on my journey approached me,
278 taking the form of a man in his youth as I came to the palace,
279 first-growing down on his lip in the loveliest season of young life.
280 Taking my hand in his own, he spoke and addressed me directly:
281 “Where, sorrowful doomed man, over the hills are you wandering lonely,
282 ignorant of the country? Your men in the palace of Circe
283 now are confined like pigs in the thick-covered sties where she holds them.
284 Do you go there to release them? Yourself shall never return from
285 out of the palace, I tell you, but stay where the others are staying.
286 Come, I shall free you from evil myself and shall bring you to safety.
287 Here is a powerful drug you must carry inside Circe’s palace;
288 this shall avert from your head the arrival of pitiless death-day.
289 I shall relate all Circe’s designs that shall bring you destruction.
290 She shall prepare you a potion and mingle her drugs in your eating;
291 even so, she cannot enchant you at all, for the powerful
292 medicine I shall provide shall prevent her. I shall explain everything.
293 Just when the goddess Circe attacks with her long wand against you,
294 draw from beside your thigh the sharp-edged sword that you carry,
295 leap upon Circe as though you are truly intending to kill her.
296 She, overcome with terror, will urge you to come to her bedside.
297 Do not, from then on, refuse to lie in the bed of the goddess,
298 if you desire she release your companions and welcome you kindly;
299 yet you must bid her to swear the great oath of the blessed immortals
300 she is not planning to bring any further affliction upon you,
301 lest when you stand there naked she make you a coward and unman you.”
302 So having spoken, the slayer of Argus presented the medicine,
303 pulling it out of the earth, and he showed me the form it presented:
304 black at the root, but its flower appeared to my eyes to be milk-white.
305 Moly immortals call it, and hard is the labor for mortals
306 digging it up, but the gods have the power to do every action.
307 Then lord Hermes departed across the tree-covered island,
308 going to high Olympus; but I went onward to Circe’s
309 palace, and many dark thoughts moved through the heart in my body.
310 There at the doors of the beautiful-haired shining goddess I halted,
311 standing and calling aloud, and the goddess attended my outcry.
312 Straightway she came from the palace and opened the beautiful doorway,
313 calling me inward; I went at her side with the heart full of sorrow.
314 Into the palace she led me and seated me there on a silver-
315 studded chair beautifully and elaborately fashioned, with footstool.
316 She made ready a potion for me in a cup made of gold-work,
317 slipping a drug in the mixture, with evil intent in her spirit.
318 When she had given it, I drank, and the potion in no way bewitched me;
319 striking me there with her wand, she spoke and addressed me directly:
320 “Go to the pigsty now and lie down beside your companions.”
321 So she spoke; from beside my thigh I drew out the sharp sword,
322 leaping at Circe as though I were truly intending to kill her.
323 Shrieking, she ran underneath the great blade and embraced both my knees;
324 wailing aloud, she addressed me, and these words took wing as she uttered:
325 “Who are you, whence have you come, and where are your city and parents?
326 Wonder possesses me now that the drugs you have drunk have not changed you;
327 never another man has endured these drugs without changing,
328 once he had drunk and they passed the defensive enclosure of tooth-row.
329 Surely the mind in your breast must possess an enchantment-proof nature.
330 You must be lord Odysseus, the man who has manifold turnings,
331 whom the gold-wanded slayer of Argus repeatedly told me
332 would come here on his homeward return out of Troy in the swift ship.
333 Come now, place in its sheath the sword that you carry, and let us
334 two climb into my bed, so we, being mingled together
335 there in the labor of love, may learn to have trust in each other.”
336 So she spoke, and I spoke to the goddess and gave her an answer:
337 “Circe, how can you now command me to act with a kindness,
338 you who have made my companions into pigs in your palace,
339 keeping me now in this place and designing deceit when you call me
340 into your chamber and ask me to climb in the bed where you slumber,
341 so when I stand there naked you make me a coward and unman me?
342 Never shall I be willing to climb in the bed where you slumber,
343 not till you bring yourself, goddess, to swear me the great oath
344 you are not planning to bring any further affliction upon me.”
345 So I spoke, and she straightway swore as I asked and commanded.
346 But when she finished the oath and had sworn every word to completion,
347 I climbed into the beautiful bed of the goddess Circe.
348 Meanwhile, four serving women were working around in the palace,
349 women whose labor attended the house of the goddess in order.
350 They are the daughters of springs and the forests and beautiful rivers,
351 sacred, that flow in their course till they enter the salt-colored sea-way.
352 One on the chairs threw beautiful covers of purple above them,
353 first having spread smooth linen beneath all the beautiful fabric;
354 one drew the silver tables in front of the chairs for the feasting,
355 setting the baskets fashioned of gold on the tables in order;
356 one mixed up in a silver bowl honey-hearted and sweet wine,
357 then she distributed drinking cups that were fashioned of gold-work;
358 fourth brought water and kindled a powerful fire underneath the
359 mighty enormous cauldron, and water was heated within it.
360 After the water had boiled in the brightly shining bronze cauldron,
361 into the bathtub she set me and bathed from the mighty container,
362 tempering water to suit my desire as she poured it from head down to shoulders,
363 till she removed from my limbs all the weariness wasting my spirit.
364 Once she had bathed me and richly anointed my body with olive oil,
365 casting around me a beautiful cloak and a tunic for clothing,
366 into the palace she led me and seated me there on a silver-
367 studded chair beautifully and elaborately fashioned, with footstool.
368 Water for washing my hands an attendant brought in a pitcher,
369 beautiful, fashioned of gold, and she poured it above a great silver
370 basin for washing; beside me she drew up a table with polish.
371 Bread the respected housekeeper carried in with her and set it
372 there before me with manifold dishes, and gave of her stores freely,
373 urging my eating; my heart did not wish to partake of the banquet.
374 There I was sitting with thoughts turned elsewhere and evil foreboding.
375 Circe, when once she perceived me sitting and not putting forward
376 hands to the bread, and possessed by a terrible grief in my spirit,
377 close she approached and addressed me, and these words took wing as she uttered:
378 “Why do you sit there, Odysseus, a man who is stricken with silence,
379 eating the heart in your body and touching no food and no drinking?
380 Do you suspect yet another deception? You have no reason
381 now to be frightened, since I have already sworn the strong oath to you.”
382 So she spoke, and I spoke to the goddess and gave her an answer:
383 “Circe, what man who possesses a sense of appropriate conduct
384 ever could bring himself willingly food or a drink to be tasting,
385 not till he freed his companions and saw with his eyes they were living?
386 If in good faith you are urging that I now be drinking and eating,
387 free all the men, so my eyes may behold my companions, the faithful.”
388 So I spoke; and holding her wand, through the hall went Circe,
389 opening up all the doors of the pigsty and driving them outward,
390 still in the form of the pigs that are nine years old and well fattened.
391 There they were standing in front of the goddess, and moving among them,
392 Circe anointed each one of the men with a different potion.
393 All of the bristles the ruinous drug had made grow on their bodies,
394 that which the queenly Circe had given them, fell from their members.
395 Men they became once again, and younger than ever beforehand,
396 greatly more beautiful also and taller to look at in stature.
397 All recognized me, and each took hold of my hands in his own hands;
398 passionate weeping possessed every man, and the house all around us
399 rang with a terrible sound; even Circe was moved into pity.
400 Close she approached and addressed me, the radiant one among goddesses:
401 “Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus endlessly resourceful,
402 go to the swift-running ship and the shore of the sea without lingering.
403 First you must haul the vessel ashore on the dry land above it,
404 stowing your cargo and all of the ship’s equipment in caverns;
405 then come yourself once again, and bring your companions, the faithful.”
406 So she spoke, and the proud heart within me consented and yielded.
407 Down to the swift-running ship and the shore of the sea I proceeded;
408 there by the swift-running ship I discovered my faithful companions,
409 pitifully lamenting and letting the great tears fall from them.
410 Just as the calves of the farmyard will gather around all the cattle
411 coming back filled from their grazing into the yard of the dunghill,
412 frisking together to meet them—the fences no longer restrain them,
413 but with incessant lowing they run round the sides of their mothers—
414 so, when the men with their eyes had perceived me, around me they crowded,
415 weeping; and deep in their hearts it appeared they had come to their homeland,
416 come to the city itself and the soil of the country that bore them,
417 ruggedly coasted Ithaca, where they were nourished and nurtured.
418 Wailing aloud, they addressed me, and these words took wing as they uttered:
419 “Zeus-reared man, your return brings joy to us all in our sorrow,
420 even as though we had come to our homeland, the country of fathers.
421 Come now, tell of the death and destruction that took our companions.”
422 So they spoke, and I answered them all with compassionate language:
423 “First we must haul the vessel ashore on the dry land above it,
424 stowing our cargo and all of the ship’s equipment in caverns;
425 then bestir yourselves, every man, and come with me swiftly,
426 so you may look on your friends in the sacred palace of Circe,
427 eating and drinking their fill, for their stores are forever abundant.”
428 So I spoke, and the men gave heed to the word I had spoken.
429 Only Eurylochus tried to restrain all the other companions;
430 raising his voice, he addressed them, and these words took wing as he uttered:
431 “Pitiful men, where are we going? Why hunger for evils,
432 entering Circe’s palace, where she will transform us completely,
433 making us pigs, or perhaps into wolves, or perhaps into lions,
434 forced to be guards of her mighty house and remain there forever,
435 just as the Cyclops treated our friends when they entered his courtyard,
436 led by the reckless Odysseus, who went there among them,
437 since by this man’s blind folly those comrades also were slaughtered.”
438 So he spoke; and I pondered the matter within me, considering
439 whether to draw from beside my powerful thigh the long sword blade,
440 sever his head with a blow of the weapon, and send it earthward,
441 though he was closely related to me by the ties of a marriage.
442 This man and that one restrained me with honey-sweet words of persuasion:
443 “Zeus-born man, if you order, we gladly will leave him behind us,
444 here by the ship to remain and to keep the black vessel protected;
445 you lead the rest of us onward to Circe’s sacred enclosure.”
446 So having spoken, they climbed from the side of the ship and the seashore;
447 nor did Eurylochus stay by the hollow vessel behind them:
448 after us came he as well, for he feared my terrible censure.
449 Meanwhile Circe had bathed all the other companions within her
450 palace with tender attention, and rubbed them richly with olive
451 oil, and dressed them in fleecy cloaks and in tunics beneath them.
452 All of the men we discovered, well feasting, inside of the palace.
453 When they beheld one another and knew every face that confronted
454 theirs, they lamented and wept, and the whole house echoed around them.
455 Close she approached and addressed me, the radiant one among goddesses:
457 “Let no more of this swelling lamentation trouble the household.
458 I myself know well what you suffered upon the fish-teeming
459 sea, and the wrongs that malicious men have inflicted on dry land.
460 Come now, eat of your food and partake of the wine that is waiting,
461 till you recover again in your breasts that spirited courage
462 held when you first left behind you the soil of the country of fathers,
463 ruggedly coasted Ithaca. Now you are withered and spiritless,
464 always recalling the pain of the difficult wandering; never
465 does your heart turn to feasting, for truly your suffering was heavy.”
466 So she spoke, and our proud hearts consented and yielded within us.
467 There, as the days made their courses, the whole full circle of one year,
468 feasting we sat upon meat beyond counting and sweet wine beside it.
469 But when the year had passed, and the seasons had turned in their orbit,
470 months began waning away, and the long days came to completion;
471 then my companions, the faithful, called me aside and addressed me:
472 “Strange one, remember at last the land of your fathers; the season
473 now has arrived, if indeed it is fated that you shall be rescued,
474 coming again to your high-roofed house and the land of your fathers.”
475 So they spoke, and the proud heart within me consented and yielded.
476 So through the whole of the day till the light of the sun had descended,
477 feasting we sat upon meat beyond counting and sweet wine beside it.
478 But when the sun went down and the sacred darkness had gathered,
479 all of the men lay down in the shadowy halls of the palace.
480 I, going up to the bed of surpassing beauty of Circe,
481 clasped both her knees in my hands as a suppliant; the goddess attended.
482 Raising my voice, I addressed her, and these words took wing as I uttered:
483 “Circe, fulfill now the promise you gave me and send me to homeland;
484 already my spirit is eager to go, and the hearts of the others;
485 all of them wear out my heart as they gather around me in mourning,
486 whenever you are away and not in the hall to restrain them.”
487 So I spoke, and at once the radiant goddess responded:
488 “Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus endlessly resourceful,
489 you shall no longer remain in my house when your wishes oppose it.
490 Yet there is first one other journey that you must accomplish:
491 into the house of Hades and dread Persephone you must
492 go, to inquire of the spirit of Theban Tiresias, prophet
493 blind in his eyes, but whose understanding remains unimpaired still.
494 Even in death Persephone granted him thoughtful perception,
495 him alone; all the others are shadows that flutter about him.”
496 So she spoke, and the dear heart within me was broken completely.
497 There on the bed I sat and wept; and the spirit within me
498 wished no longer to live and to look on the brightness of sunlight.
499 But when I had grown weary of weeping and writhing in anguish,
500 then I addressed her in answer and spoke these words to the goddess:
501 “Circe, who will be guide on the course of this difficult journey?
502 No man yet in a black ship ever has sailed into Hades.”
503 So I spoke, and at once the radiant goddess responded:
504 “Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus endlessly resourceful,
505 let no concern for a pilot aboard your vessel distress you.
506 Set up the mast and extend on its yards the glistening white sails,
507 then take your seat; for the breath of the North Wind will carry her onward.
508 But when your ship has completed the passage across the Ocean,
509 there is a low shore standing, and Persephone’s sacred woodlands,
510 tall-growing poplars of darkness and willows that scatter their fruit young.
511 Beach your ship at the edge of the Ocean’s deep-running waters;
512 you must advance by yourself to the dank domain of the death-god.
513 There into Acheron flow Pyriphlegethon’s fire-bearing waters,
514 also Cocytus, whose stream from the water of Styx has its outflow;
515 there is a rock at the place where the thundering rivers are meeting.
516 Hero, draw close to the rock, and precisely obey my directions:
517 dig out a trench that extends for a cubit in each of its measures;
518 pour a libation around it for all of the people departed,
519 honey and milk being first, then sweet wine poured as the second,
520 water the third; and on these you must sprinkle the white flour of barley.
521 Earnestly pray to the strengthless heads of the dead and entreat them,
522 vowing that, after your coming to Ithaca, there in your palace
523 you will give them your best barren cow and heap treasures for burning;
524 then for Tiresias alone you will slaughter a black-fleeced
525 ram that excels all the others in strength and in worth in your sheepfold.
526 When you have prayed to the glorious nation of dead men and women,
527 slaughter a black ewe there with a ram, and bend both of their faces
528 downward to Erebus; turn yourself toward the streams of the river.
529 Then in their multitudes there will gather around you the spirits,
530 souls of the people who died and have passed from the world of the living.
531 Order your men and encourage them then to be skinning the carcasses,
532 sheep that lie slaughtered there by the pitiless bronze of the weapon;
533 let them be burned and prayers be offered before the immortals,
534 powerful Hades and dread Persephone, queen of the darkness.
535 Draw from beside your thigh the sharp-edged sword that you carry;
536 sit, and allow no strengthless head of the people departed
537 near to the blood until you have questioned Tiresias fully.
538 Swiftly the seer will come to your side, O leader of peoples;
539 he will reveal to you then the road and the measure of journey,
540 also the means of your homecoming over the fish-teeming sea-depths.”
541 So she spoke; and at once came Dawn on her gold-glimmering throne-seat.
542 Round me she cast for my clothing a cloak with a tunic beneath it;
543 she, the divine nymph, covered herself with a great shining silver
544 mantle, both finely and gracefully woven, and girded her waist with
545 beautiful gold, and above on her head she adjusted a long veil.
546 I made my way through the hall and aroused my companions from slumber,
547 standing beside every man and exhorting him gently in order:
548 “No more lying asleep in the sweetness of drowsing and slumber.
549 Let us depart; for the queenly Circe has shown me the pathway.”
550 So I spoke, and their proud hearts consented and yielded within them.
551 Yet not even from there did I lead every comrade uninjured.
552 One, Elpenor, the youngest among us, was sleeping above them,
553 neither a powerful warrior nor greatly sound in his judgment;
554 he had lain down far apart from his friends in the sacred enclosure,
555 heavy with wine and desiring the coolness, atop Circe’s palace.
556 Hearing the noise and commotion of all his companions departing,
557 suddenly up he leapt, and he did not remember, befuddled,
558 he must return by descending the long-built ladder below him;
559 headlong he fell from the roof, and the bones of his neck were completely
560 snapped from the spine, and his spirit descended to Hades’ dominion.
561 As the companions were starting to go, I addressed them in order:
562 “You are perhaps supposing that now you are going to your own
563 cherished country; but Circe has marked us a different pathway:
564 into the house of Hades and dread Persephone we must
565 go, to inquire of the spirit of Theban Tiresias, prophet.”
566 So I spoke, and their own hearts broke in their bodies completely.
567 Right where they stood, they sat on the earth, pulled hair from their temples,
568 weeping; but nothing of profit was born of their lamentation.
569 While we were going our way to the swift black ship and the seashore,
570 grieving deeply in heart and letting the great tears fall from us,
571 Circe meanwhile had gone in advance and beside the black vessel
572 tethered a dark ewe there with a ram for the rites of the dead men,
573 slipping with ease from our notice. For who with his eyes can behold one
574 god in despite of that god as the deathless one passes among us?
