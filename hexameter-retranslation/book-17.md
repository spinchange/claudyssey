# The Odyssey — Book 17

*The beggar comes to his own house; Argus; Antinous' footstool.*

1 When the Dawn had appeared, the early-born rose-fingered goddess,
2 then under his feet he bound on the beautiful sandals,
3 Telemachus, dear-loved son of the godlike Odysseus,
4 and he took up the stout spear, well fitted into his palm there,
5 eager to go to the town, and he spoke to his swineherd, saying:
6 “Old father, now I am going to town, so that my mother
7 may set eyes on me; for I do not think she will ever
8 cease from her grim lamentation and tear-laden mourning
9 till she has seen me myself. But to you I give this charge now.
10 Lead this unhappy stranger to town, so that once he is there
11 he may beg for his meal; and whoever is willing will give him
12 morsel of bread and a cup. It is no way possible for me
13 to put up with all men, when griefs are upon my own spirit.
14 If the stranger is deeply angered, the worse it will be for him.
15 For indeed it is dear to me to speak out truth without hiding.”
16 Then resourceful Odysseus in answer spoke back to him, saying:
17 “My dear friend, I myself have no wish to be held back either.
18 Better it is for a beggar to beg for his meal in the city
19 rather than out in the fields; and whoever is willing will give me.
20 For I am no longer so young that I should remain at the cattle-stalls
21 and in all things obey whatever an overseer tells me.
22 But go on; and this man here, the one whom you are commanding,
23 will lead me, once I have warmed by the fire and daylight has come there.
24 For these garments I have are miserably poor; do not let the
25 morning frost overcome me; and you say the town lies far off.”
26 So he spoke, and Telemachus went through the yard of the farmstead,
27 striding swiftly with his feet, and sowing evils for suitors.
28 But when he came to the house well built and fitly inhabited,
29 there he stood up his spear as he carried it by a tall pillar,
30 and he himself went within and crossed the threshold of stone there.
31 Him the first one by far to perceive was the nurse Eurycleia,
32 as she was spreading soft fleeces upon the elaborate chairs there.
33 Then with tears she came straight toward him, and around him the others,
34 women enslaved of Odysseus, enduring of spirit, were gathered,
35 and with affection they kissed his head and both of his shoulders.
36 Out from her chamber then came circumspect-minded Penelope,
37 like to Artemis, or like to golden Aphrodite,
38 and weeping she cast her arms all around her beloved son’s body,
39 and she kissed his head and both the bright beautiful eyes there,
40 and in lamentation she spoke to him winged words, saying:
41 “You have come, Telemachus, sweet light. I truly had never
42 thought I should look upon you again, when by ship you had journeyed
43 secretly off to Pylos, against my desire, for tidings
44 of your dear father. But come now, tell me whatever you witnessed.”
45 Then in turn clear-headed Telemachus answered her, saying:
46 “Mother, do not awaken my weeping, nor stir up my heart more
47 here in my breast, when I have but escaped a sheer destruction.
48 Rather, go bathe yourself, and take clean garments upon you,
49 then with your serving women go up to the upper chamber,
50 and pray to all the gods that you will offer complete hecatombs
51 if perhaps Zeus may ever accomplish requital in actions.
52 But I shall go to the place of assembly, so I may summon
53 the guest who came hither with me as I traveled back from that country.
54 Him I sent on ahead with my comrades equal to immortals,
55 and instructed Piraeus to take him along to his dwelling
56 and to treat him with kindness and honor until I have come there.”
57 So then he spoke, and for her his word became wingless and silent.
58 She then bathed herself, and took clean garments upon her body,
59 and she prayed to all the gods that she would offer complete hecatombs
60 if perhaps Zeus might ever accomplish requital in actions.
61 Then Telemachus afterward passed through the hall and went outward,
62 holding his spear; and together with him went two swift dogs also.
63 Wondrous grace then Athena poured out over him in abundance,
64 and all of the people gazed upon him in wonder as he came there.
65 Round him the haughty suitors assembled themselves in a circle,
66 speaking fair words, but devising dark evils deep in their spirits.
67 But he then turned aside from the thick press and tumult among them,
68 going where Mentor sat, and Antiphus, and Halitherses,
69 men who from the beginning had been his father’s companions.
70 There he went and sat down, and they questioned him over each matter.
71 Then to them came near Piraeus, renowned for the spear in battle,
72 leading the guest through the town to the place of assembly; nor long then
73 did Telemachus stay apart from the guest, but stood beside him.
74 And Piraeus was first to address him and speak out, saying:
75 “Telemachus, quickly now send some women along to my dwelling,
76 so I may send you the gifts that Menelaus gave to you.”
77 Then in turn clear-headed Telemachus answered him, saying:
78 “Piraeus, we do not yet know how these matters will issue.
79 If in my halls the haughty suitors should secretly kill me,
80 and divide all my father’s possessions among them in portions,
81 I would have you enjoy them yourself, rather than any of those men;
82 but if against these men I shall plant bloody death and destruction,
83 then bring them home to my house with gladness, and I shall be gladdened.”
84 So he spoke, and led the much-enduring stranger on to the household.
85 But when they had come to the halls where the family dwelt in comfort,
86 first they laid down their cloaks on the benches and carved chairs there,
87 then stepped into polished bathing-tubs and bathed their bodies.
88 And when the serving-women had bathed them and rubbed them with olive,
89 and had cast round them thick warm cloaks and tunics upon them,
90 out from the baths they stepped and seated themselves on the benches.
91 Then a maidservant brought them water and poured from a pitcher
92 over a silver basin, from a beautiful vessel of gold there,
93 so they might wash; and beside them she drew up a polished table.
94 Then the grave housekeeper brought in bread and set it before them,
95 placing many good things beside it, giving freely from what was there.
96 Over against them his mother sat down by the pillar of the hall there,
97 leaning back in her chair and twisting the fine-spun wool with her fingers.
98 Then they reached out their hands to the good things laid ready before them.
99 But when they had cast from them the desire for drink and for eating,
100 then prudent Penelope first began speaking among them:
101 “Telemachus, I for my part shall go up to my upper chamber
102 and lie down upon that bed which has been made for my grieving,
103 always befouled with the tears that are mine, from the hour when Odysseus
104 went with the sons of Atreus away to Ilios; yet you could not bear,
105 before the overbearing suitors came back into this house here,
106 to tell me clearly your father’s return, if perhaps you had heard it.”
107 Then wise Telemachus answered his mother and spoke in return thus:
108 “Therefore, my mother, to you I shall tell the whole truth of the matter.
109 We went to Pylos, to Nestor, the shepherd and ruler of peoples.
110 He received me there in his lofty halls with all loving-kindness,
111 and cherished me warmly, just as a father cherishes his own son
112 come after long absence from elsewhere back home in his youth there;
113 so he took loving care of me, with his sons renowned beside him.
114 Yet of Odysseus steadfast in heart he declared that never
115 had he heard any word, whether living or dead, from earth’s people.
116 But onward he sent me to Atreus’ son, to Menelaus spear-famed,
117 with horses to draw me, and with a close-jointed chariot also.
118 There I saw Argive Helen, because of whom many in hardship
119 Argives and Trojans alike had suffered, by will of the immortals.
120 Straightway then Menelaus, good in the battle-cry, questioned me,
121 asking for what need I had come to glorious Lacedaemon.
122 And I told him in turn the whole truth exactly and fully.
123 Then he answered me back, and spoke to me in these winged words:
124 “Out upon them, that they should desire to lie in the bed of
125 such a strong-hearted man, being themselves but weaklings and cowards.
126 As when amid a thicket the hind of a powerful lion
127 lays down her tender young fawns, newborn and still needing milk there,
128 then goes to graze over mountain-spurs and grassy hollows,
129 but he comes back again to the bed that is his in the thicket
130 and upon both alike lets loose an unspeakably shameful destruction,
131 so will Odysseus on them let loose an unspeakably shameful destruction.
132 If only, Father Zeus and Athena and lord Apollo,
133 he were the man he once was in well-built Lesbos among men,
134 when he rose at the challenge and wrestled with Philomeleides,
135 and threw him down with great force, and all of the Achaeans rejoiced there,
136 being such then, if Odysseus might mix with the suitors among them,
137 all of them soon would become short-lived and their marriages bitter.
138 Now as for all that you ask me and earnestly beg me to tell you,
139 I shall not speak beside the mark, nor turn you aside, nor deceive you.
140 Whatever the truthful old man of the sea declared and told me,
141 not one word of that will I hide from you, nor keep it in secret.
142 He said that he saw him upon an island, afflicted with anguish,
143 in the halls of the nymph Calypso, who keeps him there by compulsion.
144 He cannot make his way to the land of his fathers and country;
145 for there are no well-oared ships there beside him, no comrades
146 who might convey him over the broad back of the sea on his journey.”
147 So spoke Atreus’ son, Menelaus, renowned for the ash-spear.
148 When these things had been finished, I journeyed homeward; the immortals
149 gave me a following wind, and quickly they sent me to homeland.”
150 So he spoke, and the word stirred the heart in the breast within her.
151 Then in their midst godlike Theoclymenus also addressed them:
152 “Honored lady, wife of Odysseus, son of Laertes,
153 truly he does not know it for certain; but listen now to my saying.
154 For I shall prophesy to you exactly, and I shall hide nothing.
155 Let Zeus now know it first among gods, and the table of guest-right,
156 and the hearth of blameless Odysseus, to which I have now come,
157 that surely Odysseus is already here in his fatherland’s country,
158 sitting or moving abroad, and learning of these evil actions;
159 he is here, and for all of the suitors he plants an evil destruction.
160 Such was the bird-sign I marked as I sat on the well-benched vessel,
161 sitting there I discerned it and cried it aloud to Telemachus.”
162 Then in her turn wise-minded Penelope answered him:
163 “Ah stranger, if only this word might be brought to fulfillment;
164 then you would quickly know both my affection and many gifts from me,
165 given by me, so any man who met you would call you blessed.”
166 Thus they were speaking such things in turn to one another.
167 But the suitors before the great hall of Odysseus
168 took their delight in the discus and in casting of javelins,
169 there on the level-paved court, where earlier too they were reckless.
170 But when now it was time for the evening meal, and the sheep came
171 in from the fields on all sides, and the men drove them, as always,
172 then among them Medon spoke; for he was the one of the heralds
173 who pleased them the most, and attended them there at the feasting:
174 “Young men, since now all of you have delighted your spirits with contests,
175 come to the house, so there we may make ready the supper;
176 for there is nothing worse in taking a meal at due season.”
177 So he spoke, and they rose up and went, obeying his saying.
178 But when they had come to the house that was nobly inhabited,
179 then they laid down their cloaks on the benches and chairs in the hall there,
180 and they slaughtered great sheep and the fattened and flourishing goats,
181 and they slaughtered rich swine and a heifer brought in from the cattle,
182 making the feast ready. Meanwhile from the country toward townward
183 noble Odysseus and the godlike swineherd were hastening.
184 Then the swineherd began the discourse, that captain of people:
185 “Stranger, since now indeed you are eager to go to the city
186 today, as my master commanded me—though for my own part
187 I would prefer you remained here as warden to guard the farm buildings;
188 but I respect him and fear him, lest afterward he should reproach me;
189 harsh indeed are the scoldings that come from the mouths of our masters.
190 But come now, let us go; for the day has advanced the most part,
191 and soon toward the evening I think it will turn to more bitter cold for you.”
192 Then resourceful Odysseus in answer addressed him and said:
193 “I know it, I understand; you command one who thinks on these matters.
194 But come, let us go; and do you lead onward the whole way before me.
195 Give me a staff as well, if somewhere you have one already cut,
196 for support, since you say that the road is not easy for foot-travel.”
197 So he spoke, and about his shoulders he slung the unsightly beggar’s pouch,
198 thick-patched and tattered, and on it a twisted cord served as shoulder-strap.
199 Then Eumaeus placed in his hand a staff that well pleased him.
200 So the two of them went; and the dogs and the herdsmen remained there
201 staying behind by the stead, while he led his master townward,
202 like to a beggar in misery, like to an aged man also,
203 leaning upon his staff; and sorry garments were wrapped on his body.
204 But when as they strode on their way down the rugged footpath
205 they were now near the town and came to the spring of the people,
206 well-built and fair in its flow, where the townsmen drew their water,
207 which Ithacus made, and Neritus, and also Polyctor,
208 and around it there stood a grove of water-fed poplar trees there,
209 circling it round on all sides, and the cold water streamed down beneath them
210 from high on the rock; and above was fashioned an altar
211 for the nymphs, where all wayfaring men would offer libation.
212 There the son of Dolius, Melanthius, came up and met them,
213 driving the goats that surpassed all the flocks of the goatherds,
214 meant as a meal for the suitors; and two herdsmen followed beside him.
215 Seeing the two, he reviled them and spoke and called each by name there,
216 speaking outrageous and shameful words; and he stirred up Odysseus’ spirit:
217 “Now then indeed the utterly base is leading the base one before him;
218 always the god brings like to like and joins each fellow to fellow.
219 Where are you taking this belly-slave, most miserable swineherd,
220 this troublesome beggar, this scavenger haunting the banquets,
221 one who by many doorposts will stand and rub both his shoulders,
222 begging for scraps, not asking for swords nor asking for cauldrons?
223 If you would give him to me to become the keeper of sheepfolds,
224 and to sweep out the pens and carry green fodder to young kids,
225 then perhaps by drinking whey he might put some flesh on his thigh there.
226 But since indeed he has learned only evil and worthless behaviors,
227 he will not choose to go near to work, but cringing through the city
228 wants by begging to feed his insatiable belly forever.
229 But I will tell you this, and the thing will indeed be accomplished:
230 if he comes to the house of godlike Odysseus,
231 many footstools around his head from the hands of men in the hall there
232 will scrape all his ribs as he is struck and flung through the dwelling.”
233 So he spoke, and in passing, in witlessness, kicked with his foot at
234 Odysseus’ hip; but he did not knock him away from the pathway.
235 He stood firm and secure; and Odysseus pondered within him
236 whether rushing upon him he should with the staff take his spirit,
237 or lifting him high by the middle should smash his head on the earth there.
238 But he endured and held himself in with the strength of his spirit.
239 Then the swineherd, seeing him there, rebuked him and prayed with hands lifted:
240 “Nymphs of the spring, daughters of Zeus, if ever Odysseus
241 burned for you thigh-pieces, swathed over thickly with glistening fat there,
242 out of young lambs and kids, then grant me this prayer that I long for,
243 so that the man himself may return, and a god lead him homeward;
244 then he would scatter all of your splendors and swagger to nothing,
245 all that you flaunt in your insolence now as you wander forever
246 all through the town; and the flocks are destroyed by the wickedly faithless men.”
247 Then in reply spoke Melanthius, goatherd and master of she-goats:
248 “Dear me, what a thing the dog said, full of ruinous cunning!
249 Him on a black ship fitted with benches one day I shall carry
250 far away out of Ithaca, so he may earn me rich profit.
251 If only Apollo the archer with silver bow would strike Telemachus
252 down in the hall this day, or the suitors subdue and destroy him,
253 just as Odysseus has lost far away his day of returning.”
254 So he spoke, and he left them there as they slowly kept coming;
255 but he himself strode on, and swiftly he came to his master's dwelling.
256 Straightway he went inside, and sat down among all the suitors,
257 facing Eurymachus there, for he loved him most among all men.
258 Those who were serving set down by his side his portion of meat there,
259 and the revered housekeeper brought him bread and set it beside him
260 there for his eating. Then close by came noble Odysseus
261 and the divine swineherd and halted; around them there drifted
262 out the sound of the hollowed lyre, for Phemius started his singing.
263 Then Odysseus, taking the swineherd's hand, addressed him and said thus:
264 “Eumaeus, surely these are the beautiful halls of Odysseus,
265 easy indeed to distinguish and know when seen among many.
266 One part joins on to another, and all of the court has been finished
267 there with a wall and coping; the well-made doors are double;
268 no man alive could lightly break through and plunder the place here.
269 I can tell that within it a great host of men are feasting,
270 since the smell of roast meat mounts up, and the lyre in the house sounds,
271 that lyre which the gods have ordained as companion of banquets.”
272 Then in reply you spoke, Eumaeus, swineherd and loyal:
273 “You marked it with ease, since in other things too you are not witless.
274 Come then, let us consider how all of this business shall happen.
275 Either you go in first to the well-inhabited dwelling,
276 slipping among the suitors, while I remain here behind you;
277 or if you wish it, stay here, and I shall go onward before you.
278 Only do not delay very long, lest someone should see you
279 standing outside and should strike you or pelt you; on this I advise you.”
280 Then much-enduring noble Odysseus answered him, saying:
281 “I know it, I understand; you are urging a man who has thought it.
282 You go onward before me, and I shall remain here behind you.
283 I am not inexperienced in blows and in things men throw at me;
284 steadfast is the heart in me, since I have suffered so many
285 sorrows in waves and in war; let this too come after the others.
286 Yet there is no concealing the greedy insatiable belly,
287 cursed thing, which gives many troubles and evils to mortals;
288 because of it even the ships with good benches are fitted
289 out on the barren sea, bringing ruin and harm to opponents.”
290 So the two of them spoke such things to each other together.
291 Then a dog lying there raised up his head and his ears in attention,
292 Argus, the dog of long-suffering great-hearted King Odysseus,
293 whom once he himself had reared, but enjoyed him not, since before that
294 off he had gone to sacred Ilios. In the old days the young men
295 used to lead him to hunt wild goats and deer and the hares there.
296 Now he lay cast aside, with his master away and departed,
297 on a great heap of dung that was poured there before the outer doors, from
298 mules and from cattle in plenty, until the servants of Odysseus
299 carried it off to manure the broad field of their absent master.
300 There lay Argus the dog, all covered with swarming dog-ticks.
301 Then, when he noticed Odysseus standing there near him at last, he
302 wagged his tail and flattened both ears, but no longer had power
303 now to come any nearer and reach his own master before him.
304 But Odysseus, turning away, wiped off a tear that had risen,
305 easily hiding it from Eumaeus, and asked him a question:
306 “Eumaeus, this is a wonder indeed, this dog on the dung-heap.
307 Fine is the shape of his body, though this much I cannot determine,
308 whether he also was swift in his running, to match that appearance,
309 or was merely the sort of dogs that attend at men's tables,
310 such as their masters keep and take care of for ornament only.”
311 Then in reply you spoke, Eumaeus, swineherd and loyal:
312 “This is indeed the dog of a man who died far from his homeland.
313 If he were still such in his build and in all of his actions
314 as when Odysseus, departing to Troy, went away and left him,
315 quickly you would marvel to see his speed and his great strength.
316 Never did any beast that he chased in the deep of the woodland
317 get away from him; he was skillful beyond all others in tracking.
318 But now he is gripped by misery; and his lord in a foreign land perished,
319 far from his home, and the heedless women do not care for him.
320 Slaves, when no longer their masters maintain and uphold their dominion,
321 no longer then are they willing to labor as duty commands them.
322 Zeus of the thunderous voice takes away one half of the valor
323 out of a man, on the day when the yoke of his slavery takes him.”
324 So he spoke, and entered the house where people were dwelling in comfort,
325 and he went straight through the hall to the midst of the splendid suitors.
326 Then for Argus the fate of black death came over and seized him,
327 just when at last he had looked on Odysseus, now in the twentieth year.
328 Him then first of them all godlike Telemachus noticed,
329 seeing the swineherd come down through the house, and quickly thereafter
330 nodded and called him near; and he, glancing around him, took up a footstool
331 lying there, where the carver of meat used to sit in the banquet,
332 sharing out many portions of flesh to the suitors who feasted through the household.
333 This he carried and set by the table of young Telemachus,
334 facing it; and there he himself sat down. Then the herald
335 took up a portion and placed it before him, lifting bread from the basket.
336 Close behind him after that entered Odysseus the hero,
337 like to a beggar, a miserable old man and a graybeard,
338 leaning upon his staff; and foul clothes covered his body.
339 Then he sat on the threshold of ash wood, inside the doorway,
340 leaning against a cypress post, which long ago some craftsman
341 smoothed with skill in his work and had made it straight by the line-rule.
342 Then Telemachus called the swineherd close to him and addressed him,
343 taking a whole loaf out of the finely woven basket,
344 and some meat, as much as both of his hands could hold when he cupped them:
345 “Carry these things to the stranger, and tell him also
346 he should go among all of the suitors and beg from each one there;
347 shame is no help to a man when need has fallen upon him.”
348 So he spoke, and the swineherd went when he had heard that order,
349 and standing close beside him he spoke out winged words in answer:
350 “Telemachus gives you these, stranger, and bids you also
351 go among all of the suitors and beg from each one there;
352 shame, he says, is no good thing for a man who must live by his begging.”
353 Then resourceful Odysseus in turn made answer and said to him:
354 “Father Zeus, grant that Telemachus may be prosperous among men,
355 and may all come to pass that his heart in its longing desires for.”
356 So he spoke, and received them with both of his hands, and he set them
357 there before both his feet, on top of his tattered beggar’s wallet,
358 and he ate while the singer in that great hall was singing his measure.
359 But when he had finished his meal, then the noble singer fell silent.
360 Then the suitors made an uproar all through the hall; and Athena,
361 standing close by the side of Odysseus, Laertes’ offspring,
362 urged him on, so that he might collect broken crusts from the suitors
363 and might learn which men among them were righteous and which were unrighteous.
364 Yet not even so was she minded to ward off ruin from any.
365 Then he set out to beg, going rightward from man unto man there,
366 stretching his hand out everywhere, as though he had begged all his lifetime.
367 They in pity gave to him, and they marveled greatly to see him,
368 asking each other who he might be and from where he had come there.
369 Then among them Melanthius the goatherd spoke out and addressed them:
370 “Listen to me, you suitors who court our queen of great glory,
371 speaking of this stranger; for certainly I have seen him already.
372 Truly the swineherd was the one who conducted him hither;
373 but the man’s own stock I do not clearly know, where he claims it.”
374 So he spoke, and Antinous with harsh words rebuked the swineherd:
375 “You notorious swineherd, why was it you brought this fellow
376 here to the town? Do we not have enough other wandering beggars,
377 nuisances, miserable men who lick clean the leavings of banquets?
378 Is it a little thing in your eyes that they squander your master’s
379 livelihood here as they gather, and now you have invited this one too?”
380 Then you answered him, Eumaeus, swineherd faithful and noble:
381 “Antinous, though you are high-born, these are not words finely spoken.
382 For who ever goes out of his own accord and invites in a stranger,
383 one from abroad, unless he is one of the craftsmen who serve all:
384 some seer, or healer of sickness, or builder in timber,
385 or even a god-taught singer, whose song can delight those who hear him?
386 Such men indeed are invited over the boundless earth of mortals;
387 but no man would ask in a beggar to eat up his substance.
388 You are ever the harshest of all of the suitors assembled
389 toward the slaves of Odysseus, and especially harsh upon me; but I care not,
390 so long as prudent Penelope still lives here in the palace,
391 and so long as godlike Telemachus lives here beside her.”
392 Then sagacious Telemachus answered him face-to-face, saying:
393 “Be quiet; do not answer this fellow at length with your speeches.
394 Antinous always delights in provoking men viciously,
395 using hard words, and he even urges the others to do it.”
396 So he spoke, and then to Antinous he addressed winged words:
397 “Antinous, how nobly you care for me, just as a father
398 cares for his son, when you order me now to drive from my hall this
399 stranger by force of command; may a god never bring that to fulfillment.
400 Give to him, take it yourself; I do not begrudge it, I tell you.
401 So do not trouble my mother with this, nor trouble another
402 of the thralls in the halls of godlike Odysseus.
403 No, for that is not the intent that lives in your bosom;
404 rather you wish to eat it yourself than to give to another.”
405 Then in return Antinous answered him, saying:
406 “Telemachus, lofty in speech, ungovernable spirit, what said you?
407 If all of the suitors should offer him just so much bounty,
408 then for three months more this house might keep him far from its doorway.”
409 So he spoke, and lifting the footstool from under the table,
410 showed the one where he rested his shining feet at the banquet.
411 But all the rest gave too, and they filled up the beggar’s pouch there
412 full of bread and of meat; and now Odysseus was likely
413 back at the threshold to go and taste of Achaean almsgiving.
414 But he stopped beside Antinous, and spoke to him, saying:
415 “Give, my friend; you do not seem to me the worst of the Achaeans,
416 but the best; for truly you have the look of a ruler.
417 Therefore it is your part to give still more than the others
418 bread; and I in return will praise you over the broad earth.
419 For I too once dwelt in a house among men in abundance,
420 rich and prosperous, and often I gave to a wanderer,
421 whatever man he might be and whatever need had brought him.
422 Very many servants I had, and many possessions besides them,
423 all by which men live well and are called wealthy among people.
424 But Zeus son of Cronos destroyed it all, for surely he wished it,
425 he who sent me along with roving freebooting shipmen
426 on the long road to Egypt, that there I might perish.
427 There in the stream of Egypt I halted my curving ships there.
428 Then indeed I commanded my trusty companions in earnest
429 there by the ships to remain and to guard the beached vessels,
430 and I urged men to go as lookouts up to the watchpoints.
431 But they, yielding to outrage, and following their own force,
432 quickly began to ravage the beautiful fields of Egyptians;
433 women they carried away, and little children beside them,
434 and they slaughtered the men; and swiftly the cry reached the city.
435 They, hearing the shouting, came on with the dawn as it brightened;
436 all of the plain was filled with footmen and horses together,
437 and with the flash of bronze; and Zeus who delights in the thunder
438 cast foul panic among my companions, nor did any man dare
439 stand and face them in battle; for evils stood round us on all sides.
440 There many of us they killed with the edge of the bronze weapon,
441 and others they led away alive, to labor in bondage.
442 But me they handed to Cyprus, to one who met them there as a guest-friend,
443 Dmetor the son of Iasus, who ruled mightily in Cyprus.
444 From there I have now come here, enduring affliction.”
445 Then in return Antinous answered and shouted at him:
446 “What god has brought this pest to us, this nuisance at dinner?
447 Stand there in the middle, away from my table’s presence,
448 lest soon you come to a bitter Egypt and bitter Cyprus.
449 Such a bold and shameless beggar as you are before us!
450 One after another you stand beside all men, and they give you
451 lavishly, since there is no restraint and no scruple at all in
452 giving away what is other men’s, when plenty lies by each person.”
453 Then withdrawing, resourceful Odysseus spoke to him, saying:
454 “Ah me, then beauty and sense do not dwell together in you.
455 You would not give from your own house even salt to your suppliant,
456 you who now, seated here among goods that belong to another,
457 could not bring yourself to give me some bread, though so much lies before you.”
458 So he spoke, and Antinous grew still more angry in spirit;
459 glaring beneath his brows, he addressed him with winged words:
460 “Now I no longer suppose you will go from the hall here
461 back in good fashion, since now you have dared to utter insults.”
462 So he spoke, and lifting the footstool he struck his right shoulder,
463 low on the back by the spine; but he stood there fixed like a boulder,
464 steadfast, nor did the missile of proud Antinous shake him;
465 silently only he moved his head, deep plotting of evils.
466 Then back to the threshold he went and sat down, and beside him
467 set the well-filled pouch, and spoke out among the suitors:
468 “Hear me, you suitors of our far-famed queen and princess,
469 so I may speak what the heart in my breast bids me utter.
470 There is no pain in the mind, nor any grief worth mentioning,
471 when a man is struck while he fights for his own possessions,
472 whether for cattle or else for his white-fleeced sheep in the pasture.
473 But Antinous struck me because of my wretched belly,
474 ruinous thing, which gives so many sorrows to mortals.
475 Yet if indeed there are gods and avenging Furies for beggars,
476 may death overtake Antinous before he comes to his marriage.”
477 Then in return Antinous, Eupeithes’ son, answered him:
478 “Sit there in silence and eat, stranger, or else go elsewhere,
479 lest for such words as these the young men drag you through the hallways,
480 either by foot or by hand, and tear all the flesh from your body.”
481 So he spoke, and the rest were all deeply indignant against him.
482 Thus would a man among those overweening young suitors say:
483 “Antinous, that was not fair, to strike the poor wandering beggar,
484 reckless man, if perhaps indeed he is one of the gods from the heavens.
485 Even the gods, in the likeness of strangers from alien countries,
486 taking all kinds of forms, keep passing through cities of mortals,
487 watching the lawless violence there, and the good rule of people.”
488 So spoke the suitors; but he gave no heed at all to their saying.
489 But in his heart Telemachus fostered a mighty sorrow
490 for the blow; yet he did not let one tear fall down from his eyelids,
491 but in silence he shook his head, while pondering evils in darkness.
492 But when circumspect Penelope heard of the stranger’s affliction,
493 how he had been struck in the hall, then she said to her women:
494 “May famed Apollo the archer so strike you yourself, Antinous.”
495 Then the housekeeper Eurynome answered and spoke in response there:
496 “If only our prayers might come to fulfilment and ending,
497 none of these men would come to the fair-throned Dawn in the morning.”
498 Then circumspect Penelope answered and spoke to her again:
499 “Nurse, they are enemies all, since all of them scheme us destruction;
500 but Antinous most of all is like to black death in his spirit.
501 Some poor unhappy stranger goes wandering through the great house here,
502 begging from man after man, for hard need drives him onward;
503 all the others filled his wallet and each one gave him something,
504 but he with a footstool struck the base of the man’s right shoulder.”
505 Thus she spoke among all her serving women there in the chamber,
506 seated within; meanwhile noble Odysseus was eating his supper.
507 Then calling the noble swineherd to stand by, she spoke to him saying:
508 “Go now, noble Eumaeus, and bid the stranger come upward
509 here, so that I may address him and ask him some questions,
510 whether he has heard of stout-hearted Odysseus anywhere,
511 or saw him with his own eyes; he seems like one long storm-driven.”
512 Then answering her, you spoke, Eumaeus, swineherd and good man:
513 “If only, my queen, the Achaeans would keep themselves silent,
514 then what he tells would surely enchant your own dear spirit.
515 For three nights I had him with me, and three days I detained him
516 there in my hut, for he first came to me after fleeing a vessel.
517 Yet still he has not brought to an end all the woes he recounts there.
518 As when a man gazes entranced at a singer before him,
519 one who by gift of the gods has learned songs full of longing for mortals,
520 and men insatiably yearn to keep hearing, whenever he sings them,
521 so that man there enthralled me as he sat in my dwelling and told me.
522 He declares that he is a guest-friend linked to Odysseus’ father,
523 living in Crete, where the lineage and house of Minos are rooted.
524 Thence indeed he has now come here, after suffering many afflictions,
525 rolled and driven from place to place; and he says he has heard tell
526 that Odysseus is near, in the rich land of the Thesprotian people,
527 living, and bringing much treasure along with him home to this household.”
528 Then circumspect Penelope answered and spoke to him again:
529 “Go then, call him here, so he himself may tell me the story.
530 As for these men, let them sit at the doors and amuse themselves,
531 or else here through the house, since the heart in them is lighthearted.
532 Their own possessions lie there in their homes all unharmed and unravaged,
533 grain and sweet wine; their own house-servants consume these things there,
534 while they keep swarming daily into our house here without ceasing,
535 slaughtering cattle and sheep and the fat well-fattened she-goats,
536 feasting and drinking the flashing wine in extravagant folly.
537 Wastefully so, and much of our substance is all disappearing,
538 since there is no man such as Odysseus was, to defend this household.
539 But if Odysseus should come and attain to his fatherland’s country,
540 then with his son he would swiftly repay these men for their outrage.”
541 So she spoke, and Telemachus gave a great sneeze, and around him
542 terribly all the house rang out; and Penelope laughed then,
543 and quickly she spoke winged words to Eumaeus and addressed him:
544 “Go now for me, and summon the stranger to stand here before me.
545 Do you not see how my son has just sneezed upon all my saying?
546 Therefore the death of the suitors will not be left unaccomplished,
547 every one of them all; not one shall escape death and destruction.
548 And I will tell you something besides; do you store it within you:
549 if I perceive that he tells me the truth in all things exactly,
550 I will clothe him in cloak and tunic, fine garments to wear there.”
551 Thus she spoke, and the swineherd departed when he heard her commandment,
552 and standing near at his side he addressed him in winged words, saying:
553 “Stranger, old father, circumspect Penelope summons you inward,
554 mother of Telemachus; her heart bids her ask you some questions
555 about her husband, though she herself is afflicted with sorrows.
556 And if she finds that all you tell her is wholly truthful,
557 she will clothe you in cloak and tunic, which things above all things
558 you are in need of; but food you may beg through the people
559 and so sustain your belly; whoever is willing will give it.”
560 Then much-enduring noble Odysseus answered and said to him:
561 “Eumaeus, I could at once tell all things truly and clearly
562 to the daughter of Icarius, wary-minded Penelope;
563 for I know that man well; we have both borne one common affliction.
564 But I fear the throng of these bitter and violent suitors,
565 whose outrage and brutal force rise up to the iron heaven.
566 Even just now, when that man, as I passed through the chamber,
567 struck me and gave me pain, though I had done no harm whatever,
568 neither Telemachus checked him in that, nor any other.
569 Therefore bid Penelope stay within the halls and wait there,
570 though she is eager, until the time when the sun has gone under;
571 then let her ask me concerning her husband’s day of returning,
572 seating me nearer the fire; for the clothing upon me
573 is wretched; you know it yourself, since first I entreated you.”
574 So he spoke, and the swineherd went when he had heard the saying.
575 But when he had stepped past the threshold, Penelope called to him:
576 “You do not bring him, Eumaeus. What has the wanderer minded?
577 Has he perhaps taken fear of someone outrageous, or otherwise
578 feels shame here in the house? Yet a shamefaced beggar is worthless.”
579 Then in reply you answered her, Eumaeus the swineherd:
580 “What he has said is fitting, as any man else also
581 would think, while he seeks to avoid the outrage of haughty men.
582 He bids you remain till the time when the sun has gone under.
583 And for you also yourself it is better by far, my queen, thus:
584 to speak to the stranger alone, and alone to hear his answer.”
585 Then the wise-hearted Penelope answered him, saying:
586 “The stranger is no fool; he surmises how matters may happen.
587 For I think there are not such men among mortal people,
588 men so insolent, plotting deeds so reckless and wicked.”
589 So she spoke thus; and the noble swineherd then went on his way there
590 into the throng of the suitors, when all had been told and made clear.
591 Then at once he spoke winged words to Telemachus softly,
592 leaning his head in close, so the others might not overhear him:
593 “Dear friend, I am going away, to guard the swine and the matters
594 there, your livelihood and my own; let all here be your concern now.
595 First keep watch over yourself and preserve your own person;
596 see that nothing befalls you; for many Achaeans are plotting
597 evil; may Zeus destroy them before they bring ruin upon us.”
598 Then resourceful Telemachus answered him in return:
599 “It shall be so, old father; but go when you have supped at evening.
600 Then at dawn come again, and bring noble victims for slaughter;
601 all the rest shall be cared for by me and the deathless immortals.”
602 So he spoke, and the other sat down again on the polished seat there,
603 and when he had filled up his heart with eating and drinking,
604 he went on his way to the swine, and left the enclosure and hall there,
605 full of the banqueters; they were delighting in dance and in singing,
606 for now at last the day had already declined into evening.
