# The Odyssey — Book 2

*The assembly at Ithaca; Penelope's web; Telemachus sails by night.*

1  When Dawn in her earliness rose with her rose-colored fingers,
2  then from his bed rose up the dear son of steadfast Odysseus.
3  Dressing himself, he slung the keen sword at once on his shoulder,
4  under his radiant feet he bound on the beautiful sandals,
5  out of his chamber he strode, in his face like a godhead immortal.
6  Straightway he called to the clear-voiced heralds and firmly commanded
7  all of the long-haired Achaeans be summoned at once to assembly.
8  Loudly the heralds proclaimed it; in haste the people assembled.
9  When the men had assembled and all were collected together,
10 he made his way to the meeting, a bronze-pointed spear in his palm held,
11 not by himself, for two swift-footed hounds came along in attendance.
12 Over his person Athena diffused an ineffable splendor.
13 All of the people regarded his coming with eyes full of wonder;
14 down he sat in his father's seat, while the elders yielded.
15 First of them all to address the assembly was Aegyptius, hero,
16 bent under age, and in knowledge of numberless matters abundant.
17 One of his well-loved sons had departed with godlike Odysseus,
18 sailing in hollow ships to the country of Ilium's horses—
19 Antiphus, spearman; the savage and pitiless Cyclops had killed him
20 deep in his cavern and made him the last of the men for his supper.
21 Three other sons were alive: one mingled among the proud suitors,
22 named Eurynomus, while two always worked on the lands of their father.
23 Still he could never forget that lost son, but lamented and mourned him.
24 Weeping for him, he arose and addressed the assembled Achaeans:
25 “Hear me, you men who inhabit our Ithaca, hear what I tell you.
26 Never has one of our councils convened, nor assembly been seated,
27 since in the hollow ships our noble Odysseus departed.
28 Who has assembled us now? Upon whom has necessity fallen,
29 whether he stands with the young generation or those who are elder?
30 Has he received any word that the army is homeward returning,
31 something to tell us in public because he was first to discover?
32 Or does he bring some other affair of the people for counsel?
33 Surely he seems a good man and a blessed one. May Zeus accomplish
34 good for the man, whatsoever desire in his heart he is seeking.”
35 So he spoke, and the dear son of Odysseus took joy in the omen,
36 Not any longer he sat, for desire to speak had impelled him.
37 Into the midst he advanced, and a scepter was placed in his right hand
38 there by Peisenor the herald, a man of considerate judgment.
39 First to the elder he turned, and he spoke to him openly, saying:
40 “Elder, the man is not distant; your own eyes shall presently know him.
41 I have assembled the people, for sorrow has fallen upon me.
42 No word has come to my ears that the army is homeward returning,
43 something to tell you in public because I was first to discover,
44 nor do I bring some other affair of the people for counsel.
45 Mine is the need, for two great evils have fallen upon my household.
46 First I have lost my excellent father, the king who before this
47 governed among you, and gentle he was, as a father is gentle.
48 Now an affliction far greater will presently shatter my household,
49 utterly wasting my property, ruining all my subsistence.
50 Suitors besiege my unwilling mother with harsh importunity,
51 cherished sons of the men who are here accounted the noblest.
52 Yet they are fearful of going straight to the house of her father,
53 Icarius, who could himself set the bridal gifts for his daughter,
54 giving her hand as he wished to the man who delighted her also.
55 Rather, they enter our dwelling and haunt it on every occasion,
56 slaughtering cattle and sheep and the goats that are heavy with fatness,
57 feasting away and consuming our wine with its fire-colored brightness,
58 never accounting the cost; and the greater part all goes to ruin.
59 No man is here such as once was Odysseus, guarding the household.
60 We have no power to drive this away; from this day thereafter
61 pitiable men shall we be, and unpracticed in valor and fighting.
62 I would repel it myself if the strength to repel it were in me!
63 Deeds past endurance are done here, deeds not decently ordered;
64 shamefully all of my household is ruined. Be moved to resentment!
65 Show some respect for yourselves and for neighboring peoples around you,
66 those who inhabit beside you; and fear the immortals' displeasure,
67 lest they be angered by evil and turn retribution upon you.
68 Hear my entreaty by Zeus on Olympus and also by Themis,
69 goddess who gathers assemblies of mortals and brings their dismissal:
70 hold yourselves back, my friends, and abandon me here to my sorrow,
71 wasting away—unless perhaps my father, the noble Odysseus,
72 once with malicious intent harmed all of the strong-greaved Achaeans,
73 and in return you maliciously injure me, taking requital,
74 urging these men. It would surely be better for me and more welcome
75 if it were you yourselves who consumed all my cattle and treasures.
76 Were you consuming them, someday repayment might come from the city:
77 we would pursue you with words through every street of the city,
78 claiming our property back until everything taken was rendered.
79 Now you are laying incurable griefs in the depths of my spirit.”
80 So he spoke in his wrath, dashed the scepter to earth, and was weeping,
81 Pity took hold of the people and spread through the whole of assembly.
82 All of the others were silent; and none had the courage to answer
83 young Telemachus then with an arrogant word of aggression.
84 Only Antinous answered him there and addressed him directly:
85 “High-spoken young Telemachus, ungoverned in anger, what language!
86 Shaming us here, you would gladly attach every fault to the suitors.
87 None of the suitors, the sons of Achaeans, has caused your affliction;
88 no, but your own dear mother, incomparable mistress of cunning.
89 Three whole years have gone past, and the fourth will be soon arriving,
90 while she deceives the intelligence lodged in Achaean bosoms.
91 Hope she bestows upon all and to every man offers a promise,
92 sending her messages out, while her inward intention is other.
93 This is another deception her heart has contrived and accomplished:
94 setting a mighty loom in the hall, she began there to weave it,
95 delicate, wide beyond measure, and straightway addressed us as follows:
96 ‘Young men who court me, because great Odysseus now has been taken,
97 wait, though you urge on the marriage, until I have finished this weaving;
98 let not the threads I have spun be abandoned and scattered for nothing.
99 This is a shroud for the hero Laertes, against that dark morning
100 when the destructive allotment of death, laying mortals low, takes him.
101 lest any Achaean woman among this people should blame me
102 if such a man who amassed great wealth should be laid without grave-cloth.’
103 So she spoke, and once more our imperious spirits were yielding.
104 Then in the daytime she wove at the loom and accomplished her great web;
105 yet in the nighttime, under the torches, again she unraveled.
106 Three years long she concealed her deception and fooled the Achaeans.
107 Yet when the fourth year came, as the seasons revolved in their order,
108 one of her women revealed it, a servant aware of the secret;
109 and we discovered her loosening all of the radiant fabric.
110 So she completed the work by compulsion, although she resisted.
111 Now hear the suitors' response, so that you in your heart may perceive it,
112 and so the whole of Achaea may likewise possess the assurance:
113 send your own mother away and command her to enter a marriage,
114 taking the man whom her father appoints and who also delights her.
115 But if she continues distressing the sons of Achaeans much longer,
116 keeping in mind those gifts above women Athena bestowed her—
117 knowledge of beautiful handiwork, excellence, generous judgment,
118 cleverness such as we never have heard of in women before her,
119 even the fair-haired daughters of old in Achaea's tradition,
120 Tyro, Alcmene, and Mycene wearing the beautiful headband—
121 none of those women possessed an intelligence equal to Penelope's.
122 Yet in devising this purpose, her judgment has not been becoming.
123 Just for as long shall they ravenously eat your possessions
124 while she maintains this intent which the gods are now placing within her.
125 Great is the glory she wins for herself by her cunning invention,
126 yet for your house she creates only grief for abundant resources.
127 We shall not go to our lands, nor depart for another location,
128 till she selects an Achaean at last and accomplishes marriage.”
129 And in reply spoke the thoughtful and steadfast young Telemachus:
130 “How, Antinous, could I expel her unwillingly homeward,
131 she who has borne me and raised me, while somewhere my father is living,
132 whether he's living or dead? And a heavy repayment awaits me,
133 owed to Icarius, should I by my own will send off my mother.
134 Evil would come from her father, and heaven would add to my sorrows,
135 since as she left this house, she would call the detestable Furies,
136 praying against me; and mortals would likewise reproach and revile me.
137 Therefore the word that you ask me to say shall be spoken out never.
138 But if your own hearts find any shame in the deeds you accomplish,
139 go from my halls and prepare other feasts for yourselves and your fellows,
140 eating your own possessions and visiting house after household.
141 Yet if you think it is better for you and more profitable also
142 one man's abundance be wasted away with no restitution,
143 ravage it all. I shall call on the gods who are living forever,
144 praying that Zeus may at last bring deeds of requital upon you;
145 then you shall die in the halls with no blood-price paid in requital.”
146 So he spoke, Telemachus. Zeus of the wide voice sent forth two eagles,
147 down from a towering mountain's summit releasing their flight-path.
148 First, for a while, they flew with the gusting and turbulent wind-stream,
149 keeping together and stretching their pinions alongside each other.
150 But when they came to the midst of the many-voiced Ithacan council,
151 wheeling around they beat their wings with a thickening clamor,
152 glaring on all of the heads underneath, with destruction in eyesight.
153 Raking each other with talons around the cheeks and the neckbones,
154 rightward away they darted across all the houses and city.
155 People were filled with astonishment, seeing the birds with their own eyes,
156 pondering deep in their hearts what fulfillment the omen portended.
157 Old Halitherses, the hero, arose in the midst to address them,
158 Mastor's son, who surpassed all the men of his own generation,
159 reading the flight of the birds and explaining the purposes fated.
160 Wishing them well, he arose and addressed the assembled Achaeans:
161 “Hear me, you men who inhabit our Ithaca, hear what I tell you—
162 most of all to the suitors I speak and declare what is coming.
163 Over their heads an enormous destruction is rolling. Odysseus
164 shall not remain any longer removed from the land and his loved ones.
165 Already near us, he plants the beginnings of murder and black death,
166 meant for these men, every one; and a sorrow shall come upon many
167 others who live here on Ithaca under the brightness of heaven.
168 Long before that, let us now take counsel on how to restrain them—
169 better, let them restrain their own deeds, and the sooner the better.
170 No inexperienced prophet am I, but accomplished in bird-lore.
171 All is approaching for him in accordance with what I foretold him,
172 back when the Argives embarked for the pastures of horses at Ilium,
173 while in their company went the resourceful and wise Odysseus.
174 I said, after many afflictions, with all his companions perished,
175 known to no man, in the twentieth year he would come to his homeland.
176 All I foretold him before is now reaching its destined fulfillment.”
177 But in reply spoke Eurymachus, excellent son born of Polybus:
178 “Old man, go home and announce your prophecies there to your children,
179 lest an affliction befall them at some time in days that are coming.
180 I am a prophet far better than you on the matter before us.
181 Many the birds that are flying about underneath the sun's brightness;
182 not every bird is an omen. As for the absent Odysseus,
183 far off he died, and I wish you had likewise been lost with Odysseus.
184 You would not be delivering prophecies here in abundance,
185 nor would you sharpen the anger of young Telemachus further,
186 hoping that he may bestow on your household a gift in repayment.
187 Now I shall tell you a thing, and I promise its sure execution:
188 if, with the knowledge of age and the many events you remember,
189 you should beguile a much younger man and encourage his anger,
190 first it shall make the result more painful by far for the young man,
191 though he shall never accomplish a thing on account of your speaking;
192 while on yourself, old man, we shall fasten a fine whose repayment
193 galls you in spirit, and bitterly hard shall its punishment hurt you.
194 Here is the counsel I give Telemachus here in the sight of the people:
195 let him command his mother to go to the house of her father.
196 There they can furnish a wedding and give all the bride-gifts appropriate,
197 many and splendid, the gifts that should follow a cherished daughter.
198 Not before then do I think that Achaea's sons shall abandon
199 arduous courtship. No man makes us tremble with fear or anxiety,
200 not Telemachus, no, notwithstanding his flood of oration;
201 nor do we value the prophecy, old man, which you are proclaiming,
202 all unfulfilled, for it only makes you the more to be hated.
203 Just as before shall his property all be consumed without payment,
204 while she continues to put the Achaeans off from her marriage.
205 We shall continue to wait, in contentious pursuit of the woman,
206 rivals because of her excellence, never pursuing the others,
207 women whom any of us might appropriately take into marriage.”
208 And in reply spoke the thoughtful and steadfast young Telemachus:
209 “You, Eurymachus, all of you other imperious suitors,
210 I shall no longer entreat you or talk of the matter before you.
211 Now the immortals are perfectly aware, and all the Achaeans.
212 Come, but provide me a ship that is swift and a crew of companions,
213 twenty in number, to make my voyage there and return me.
214 I shall be traveling first to the sands of Pylos, then Sparta,
215 seeking a word of the homeward return of my long-absent father.
216 Maybe a mortal can tell me, or I may discover the Rumor
217 born out of Zeus, which beyond other messengers carries men's tidings.
218 If I am told that my father is living and making his homeward
219 journey, although I am ground down by grief, I can bear it a year more.
220 Yet if I hear he is dead and no longer exists among mortals,
221 then I shall come once again to the land of my fathers that loves me,
222 heap up a tomb for my father and furnish the rites that are proper,
223 many and splendid, and afterward give my mother in marriage.”
224 So having spoken, he sat down; then Mentor arose in the council,
225 Mentor who long had been comrade and friend to the blameless Odysseus.
226 Sailing away in the ships, Odysseus left him the household,
227 bidding them all obey the old man, who should safeguard possessions.
228 Wishing them well, he arose and addressed the assembled Achaeans:
229 “Hear me, you men who inhabit our Ithaca, hear what I tell you.
230 Never again let a king with a scepter be gentle and gracious,
231 kind in his heart, or consider the right and appropriate measure;
232 rather, forever be cruel in mind and accomplish injustice,
233 since nobody any longer remembers the godlike Odysseus,
234 none of the people he governed, although as a father he loved them.
235 Yet I am less indignant against the imperious suitors,
236 even for violent deeds that they weave in their ill-meaning spirits.
237 They set their own living heads as a stake while violently eating
238 all of Odysseus' house, and they say he shall never come homeward.
239 No, I am angry at all of the rest of the people—at all you,
240 sitting in silence and never attacking the suitors with language,
241 never restraining the few, though the people are many in number.”
242 But Leocritus, Evenor's son, in reply spoke against him:
243 “Mentor, destructive and crazed in your wits, what a word you have spoken,
244 urging the people to stop us! A struggle is hard to accomplish,
245 fighting with men over feasting, especially men who outnumber.
246 Even suppose that Odysseus, Ithacan-born, should come homeward,
247 finding the lordly suitors at feasting within his own household,
248 and in his spirit determine to drive all the men from his dwelling:
249 no joy at all would his wife have then in the man she was longing
250 ever to welcome; an ugly death he would meet on the threshold,
251 fighting so many. Your speech has not found an appropriate measure.
252 Come now, people, disperse, and let each man return to his labor.
253 Mentor and old Halitherses can hasten the young man's departure,
254 since from the first they were both the companions and friends of his father.
255 Yet he will sit for a long time here, I imagine, collecting
256 news upon Ithaca; never will he bring this journey to action.”
257 So he spoke, and dissolved in its swiftness the people's assembly.
258 So they scattered away, and each man went home to his household;
259 all of the suitors went into the house of the godlike Odysseus.
260 But by himself to the sands of the seashore went Telemachus,
261 washing his hands in the gray-colored sea, and he prayed to Athena:
262 “Hear me, divinity, you who came yesterday into our household,
263 bidding me sail in a ship on the face of the mist-covered sea-way,
264 seeking a word of the homeward return of my long-absent father.
265 All my designs the Achaeans obstruct and delay from fulfillment,
266 most of them all these suitors, abusive and swollen with outrage.”
267 So he spoke as he prayed, and Athena approached and drew near him,
268 having assumed Mentor's body, resembling also his speech-voice.
269 Raising her voice she addressed him at once with these words that had feathers:
270 “Young Telemachus, never again shall you prove weak or witless,
271 if in your spirit is planted the excellent strength of your father—
272 such was the man in accomplishing action and spoken intention.
273 Then shall your journey be neither delayed nor deprived of fulfillment.
274 Yet if you are not truly the son of himself and Penelope,
275 no hope have I that you ever shall bring your intention to action.
276 Few are the sons, it is true, who arise to the stature of fathers;
277 most men are worse than their fathers, and few are the better.
278 Since you shall never again prove a coward or witless in counsel,
279 since the intelligence once in Odysseus has not wholly failed you,
280 hope remains that you surely shall bring this endeavor to action.
281 Therefore dismiss from your mind all the plans and designs of the suitors,
282 men without sense, for no sound understanding or justice is in them.
283 Nothing they know of the death and the darkening doom that is near them,
284 standing beside them, to sweep them away on a single day's dawning.
285 Not for much longer shall this, the journey you yearn for, be distant.
286 Such an ancestral companion am I, and prepared to assist you:
287 I shall equip you a fast-running ship and myself shall go with you.
288 Go to your house and again move among all the arrogant suitors;
289 gather provisions and pack every portion in fitting containers,
290 wine in the two-handled jars, barley meal, mortal men's very marrow,
291 stored in the close-fitted skins. I shall go through the town and assemble
292 willing companions at once. Many ships in sea-circled Ithaca,
293 newly constructed and old, are possessed by the island's inhabitants.
294 I shall inspect them for you and select from among them the finest;
295 swiftly we then shall equip her and launch on the width of the sea-way.”
296 So she spoke, Athena, the daughter of Zeus; Telemachus lingered
297 not any longer, once he had heard the voice of the goddess.
298 Homeward he went on his way, while his heart in his breast was afflicted.
299 There he discovered the lordly suitors inside of the palace,
300 singeing the hogs in the courtyard and stripping the hides from the goat-flesh.
301 Straight at Telemachus came Antinous, smiling with laughter,
302 took the young man by the hand, and spoke a word, and addressed him:
303 “High-spoken young Telemachus, ungoverned in anger, no longer
304 trouble your spirit with evil designs or with angry expressions.
305 Come now, eat and drink at my side in the way you did formerly.
306 All of these needs the Achaeans shall gladly supply and accomplish,
307 giving a ship and selected companions, that sooner you come to
308 sacred Pylos and gather a word of your excellent father.”
309 And in reply spoke the thoughtful and steadfast young Telemachus:
310 “How, Antinous, could I among you insolent suitors
311 eat at my ease and in silence enjoy the repose of a banquet?
312 Was it not plenty that all this while, you suitors, were cutting
313 all my possessions apart, many excellent things, while a child still?
314 Now I am grown, and by hearing the speeches of others have learned things,
315 while in the depths of my body my courage is rising and swelling.
316 I shall endeavor to fasten a doom of destruction upon you,
317 whether I travel to Pylos or stay in the land of my fathers.
318 Go I shall, and the journey I speak of shall not be an empty
319 errand—a passenger, since I possess neither ship nor companions,
320 just as your lordships no doubt have decided was more to your profit.”
321 He spoke, and he easily wrenched his hand from Antinous' clutches.
322 Through the house, all of the suitors attended the work of the banquet,
323 mocking the young man and throwing abusive expressions upon him.
324 So would a man among those arrogant young men be speaking:
325 “Clearly young Telemachus plans the destruction and death of all of us!
326 Either from sandy Pylos he shall bring back men to avenge him,
327 or out of Sparta, because of the terrible zeal that impels him.
328 Maybe he means to go into the rich-soiled country of Ephyra,
329 bringing back poisons that waste human life, to be cast in the wine bowl,
330 killing us all when the wine has been mixed and distributed to us.”
331 So would another among those arrogant young men be speaking:
332 “Who can be certain? Perhaps, having boarded the hollow-built vessel,
333 far from his people he too shall be lost in his wandering, like Odysseus.
334 So our labor and trouble would surely be doubled in measure:
335 all his possessions would have to be shared and divided among us;
336 while we should give his mother the house, and the man who shall wed her.”
337 So they spoke, but he entered the lofty room of his father,
338 spacious and high in the roof, where bronze lay heaped up with treasure,
339 gold and clothing in chests, and abundance of fragrant olive oil.
340 There great vessels were standing that held old wine in their bellies,
341 sweet to be drunk, with the pure and divine-tasting drink sealed within them,
342 set in a row by the wall for the day when Odysseus, suffering
343 many afflictions, might nevertheless come again to his household.
344 Closely constructed doors had been fitted and locked on the chamber,
345 double in thickness; and night and day an intelligent woman
346 guarded it all with the deep understanding contained in her spirit,
347 old Eurycleia, the daughter of Ops, who was born from Peisenor.
348 Calling her into the storeroom, young Telemachus then said:
349 “Nurse, come now, and draw some wine for me into the two-handled vessels,
350 sweet and the finest in flavor, except for the portion you're guarding,
351 thinking of that ill-fated man, if Odysseus somehow,
352 Zeus-born, comes home after slipping away from destruction and death-doom.
353 Fill up twelve for me now and provide every vessel a cover.
354 Pour, too, barley meal for me into the close-fitted leather,
355 twenty whole measures of barley grain pulverized under millstones.
356 Keep this alone in your knowledge and leave it collected together.
357 Evening shall see me come for it all when the hour has arrived and
358 Mother has gone to her upper apartment, preparing for slumber.
359 I shall be traveling first to the sands of Pylos, then Sparta,
360 seeking a word of my dear father's homecoming, if I can hear one.”
361 So he spoke, and Eurycleia, the nurse he had loved, was lamenting,
362 grieving aloud and addressing him straightway with words that had feathers:
363 “Why has this thought, dear child, arisen inside of your spirit?
364 Where do you wish to travel across the earth's measureless spaces,
365 only and well-beloved son? Far from home he has perished,
366 Zeus-born great Odysseus, somewhere in lands of the strangers.
367 Those men, as soon as you leave, will fashion behind you their evils,
368 killing you under deception and sharing your property with them.
369 Stay with possessions that here are your own and remain in your household.
370 No need is laid upon you to be hurt on the barren sea, wandering.”
371 And in reply spoke the thoughtful and steadfast young Telemachus:
372 “Courage, my nurse; for the purpose is made in alliance with godhead.
373 Swear to me now that you shall not tell my dear mother about it,
374 not till the eleventh day has arrived, or the twelfth in its order,
375 or till she misses me first and discovers herself my departure,
376 lest she should damage her beautiful skin with lamenting and weeping.”
377 So he spoke, and the old woman swore the great oath of immortals.
378 When the oath had been sworn and its words brought at last to completion,
379 straightway she drew off the wine for his journey in two-handled vessels,
380 poured out the barley meal for him into the close-fitted leather.
381 Young Telemachus went to the hall and again joined the suitors.
382 Once more the gray-eyed goddess Athena conceived a new purpose.
383 Taking the form of Telemachus, throughout the town she went roaming,
384 standing beside every man and addressing him clearly with orders,
385 bidding him come in the evening and gather beside the swift vessel.
386 Next from Noemon, the splendid son of the wise man Phronius,
387 she asked a swift-running ship, which he willingly promised to lend her.
388 Down went the sun at the last, and the roadways were shadowed in darkness.
389 Then she drew down the swift ship and set in its well-fashioned body
390 all of the gear that is commonly carried by ships with good benches.
391 Close to the harbor's edge she moored her; courageous companions around her
392 gathered as one, while the goddess awakened the heart of each sailor.
393 Once more the gray-eyed goddess Athena conceived a new purpose.
394 Off she departed and entered the house of the godlike Odysseus.
395 There she diffused sweet sleep on the suitors and clouded their senses,
396 striking the cups from their hands while they drank and bewildering all of them.
397 Through the town they staggered to sleep, no longer remaining
398 seated at feasting, for slumber was falling across their eyelids.
399 But to Telemachus spoke the gray-eyed goddess Athena,
400 calling him out to the front of the well-built halls of his household,
401 having assumed Mentor's body, resembling also his speech-voice:
402 “Young Telemachus, already your strong-greaved comrades are seated,
403 holding the oars in their hands and awaiting your word of departure.
404 Come, let us go, and no longer delay the beginning of travel.”
405 So having spoken, Athena, the goddess, proceeded before him,
406 rapidly, while Telemachus followed the steps of the goddess.
407 When the two had come down to the ship and the shore of the sea-way,
408 there they discovered the long-haired companions upon the sand-beach,
409 and the sacred strength of Telemachus spoke and addressed them:
410 “Come now, companions, and let us bring down our provisions; already
411 all are collected together inside of the hall. Of our purpose
412 Mother knows nothing, nor any of our women, excepting one only.”
413 So having spoken, he led them away, and the others attended.
414 Carrying everything down, they stored it inside the well-benched vessel,
415 just as the well-loved son of Odysseus firmly commanded.
416 Young Telemachus boarded the vessel, Athena before him;
417 down in the stern of the vessel she sat, and alongside the goddess
418 young Telemachus sat. The companions released the stern-cables,
419 boarded the ship in their turn, and took up their seats at the oarlocks.
420 Gray-eyed Athena provided a wind that would follow behind them,
421 fresh-blowing West Wind, singing aloud on the wine-dark sea-way.
422 Young Telemachus called to his comrades and firmly commanded
423 all lay hold on the tackle; and gladly they heard and obeyed him.
424 Raising the mast made of pine, they set it inside of the mast-box,
425 stood it erect and secured it with forestays fastened before it,
426 up by the well-twisted ox-hide ropes they hauled the white sail.
427 Wind in the sail's middle bellied it out, while the dark-colored wave-stream
428 loudly around the keel hissed as the ship made her passage upon it.
429 Running along on the face of the waves, she accomplished her pathway.
430 When they had fastened the tackle throughout the swift black-hulled vessel,
431 high they erected the wine bowls, filled to the brim and then crowned them,
432 pouring libations to gods everlasting, who live without dying,
433 most of them all to the gray-eyed daughter of Zeus they made offering.
434 All the night through and beyond into Dawn the ship clove the sea-way.
