# The Odyssey — Book XXIV

*The second Nekyia; Laertes and the trees; the peace of Ithaca.*

1 Hermes the Cyllenian summoned the souls and called them,
2 souls of the suitor men; and he held in his hands the fair wand,
3 lovely and golden, the wand with which he bewitches men's vision,
4 whom he will, and again wakes others asleep from their slumber;
5 with it he stirred them and led, and they followed behind with thin squeaking.
6 As when bats in the depths of a marvellous cavern are flitting,
7 squeaking as ever they wheel, when one has dropped from the cluster
8 hanging there from the rock, and they cling to one another,
9 so they went with shrill crying together; and Hermes before them,
10 Hermes the kindly, led down by the mouldering shadowy pathways.
11 Past the streams of Ocean they went, and the White Rock beside them,
12 past the gates of the Sun and past the land of the Dream-folk,
13 then in haste they arrived at the meadow of pale asphodel there,
14 where the souls make their home, the phantoms of mortals outwearied.
15 There they found the soul of Achilles, scion of Peleus,
16 and of Patroclus, and blameless Antilochus also,
17 and of Ajax, who was best in beauty and stature
18 of all the rest of the Danaans after the noble son of Peleus.
19 These stood there about Achilles; and close beside them there also
20 came the soul of Agamemnon, son of Atreus, in sorrow,
21 grieving; around him the rest were assembled, all those who beside him
22 died in the house of Aegisthus and met their destiny with him.
23 First then the soul of the grandson of Aeacus addressed him:
24 “Son of Atreus, we used to declare that Zeus of the bright bolt
25 loved you beyond all hero men all through the days of your lifetime,
26 seeing you ruled over many and mighty men as their master
27 there in the land of the Trojans, where we Achaeans bore anguish.
28 Yet even for you it seems the destructive fate was appointed
29 early to stand near by, that fate no mortal escapes from.
30 If only, receiving the honor of which you were lord and possessor,
31 there in the land of the Trojans you had met death and your doom then;
32 then all the Panchaeans would have built up a tomb to your glory,
33 and for your son thereafter you would have won mighty honor.
34 But now it was fated that death most pitiful should ensnare you.”
35 Then in turn the soul of the son of Atreus addressed him:
36 “Fortunate son of Peleus, Achilles, peer of immortals,
37 you who died there in Troy, far off from Argos; around you
38 other men fell, the best of the sons of Trojans and Argives,
39 battling over your body; while you in the whirling dust eddies
40 lay there immense in your might, forgetful now of horsemanship.
41 We for our part fought on through the whole day long, nor ever
42 would we have ceased from the war, had not Zeus with a storm-wind checked us.
43 But when we had brought you out of the battle and back to the shipping,
44 then on a bier we laid you, and washed your beautiful body
45 with warm water and oil; and around you many the Danaans
46 poured out hot tears and sheared from their heads the hair in their grieving.
47 Then from the sea came your mother with the immortal sea women,
48 hearing the message; and over the sea there rose an amazing
49 cry, and beneath it a shudder seized hold of all the Achaeans.
50 Then they would have leaped up and gone to the hollow ships in panic,
51 had not a man held them back, one knowing much from old seasons,
52 Nestor, whose counsel before had always appeared the best to us.
53 He with kind thought for them stood forth in assembly and told them:
54 “Stay, Argives; do not flee, young sons of the Achaean people.
55 This is his mother who comes from the sea with immortal sea women,
56 coming to look on the face of her son now stricken by death.”
57 So he spoke, and the great-hearted Achaeans checked their terror.
58 Then about you there stood the daughters of the Ancient of Ocean,
59 mourning in piteous grief, and they put on you deathless raiment.
60 Then the nine Muses all in succession with beautiful voices
61 sang the lament; and no man of the Argives then would you notice
62 free of his tears, for so piercing the clear-voiced Muse had awakened.
63 Seventeen nights and days alike we mourned you together,
64 both we immortal gods and mortals that die upon earth here;
65 but on the eighteenth day we consigned you to fire, and around you
66 many fat sheep we slaughtered, and shambling cattle with curved horns.
67 You were burned in the garments of gods, with abundant ointment,
68 and with sweet honey; and many Achaean heroes around you
69 marched in their armor about the pyre while it burned all around you,
70 footmen and horsemen alike; and a great uproar arose there.
71 But when at last the flame of Hephaestus had wholly consumed you,
72 then at dawn, Achilles, we gathered your white bones together
73 into unmixed wine and oil; and your mother had given
74 a golden two-handled urn, a gift, she said, of Dionysus,
75 and a work of Hephaestus, the god renowned far and widely.
76 Therein lie now your white bones, illustrious great Achilles,
77 mingled with dead Patroclus, the son of Menoetius,
78 and apart those of Antilochus, whom beyond all companions
79 most you honored of all after dead Patroclus had left you.
80 Over them then we heaped a great and a blameless tomb mound
81 we, the holy host of the Argive men skilled with the war-spear,
82 there on a jutting headland above the broad Hellespont,
83 so from the sea it might be visible far off to mortals,
84 men who are living now and the men who hereafter shall be.
85 Then your mother, petitioning the gods, sought prizes of splendor,
86 set them there in the midst of the games for the best of Achaeans.
87 Truly by now you have stood at the funeral contests of many
88 heroes, whenever a king has died and the young men about him
89 gird themselves and make themselves ready to strive for the prizes;
90 yet your heart would have marveled most if you had looked on those contests,
91 what surpassingly splendid prizes the goddess set out there
92 in your honor, silver-footed Thetis; for dear were you to the gods all.
93 Thus not even in death did you lose your name, but forever
94 noble your glory shall be among all mankind, Achilles.
95 But for myself, what delight was there for me, after I finished the war-web?
96 For on my homeward way Zeus planned a pitiful ruin,
97 death at the hands of Aegisthus and my own accursed wife.”
98 Thus they spoke such things in turn, addressing each other,
99 when near to them came the guide, the slayer of Argus,
100 leading the souls of the suitors whom great Odysseus had mastered.
101 And in amazement the pair came straight toward them when I saw them.
102 Then the soul of Agamemnon, son of Atreus, knew him,
103 the dear son of Melaneus, renowned Amphimedon;
104 for in Ithaca once he had lived as his guest-friend in his household.
105 To him first spoke out the soul of the son of Atreus:
106 “Amphimedon, what befell you, that under the dark earth you entered,
107 all of you chosen men and all in the same bloom of your manhood?
108 A man would not choose better throughout a whole city of people.
109 Was it Poseidon who broke you aboard your ships on the seaways,
110 stirring against you harsh winds and the long heave of billows?
111 Or did hostile men destroy you somewhere ashore on the mainland
112 while you were driving off cattle, or beautiful flocks of sheep there,
113 or while in battle for city and women you fought to the finish?
114 Tell me, for I am asking; for I declare I was your guest-friend.
115 Do you not remember when I came there into your dwelling,
116 urging Odysseus with godlike Menelaus beside me
117 to go with us to Ilium in the well-benched vessels?
118 A full month passed by while we traversed the wide sea altogether,
119 laboring hard to persuade Odysseus, sacker of cities.”
120 Then in return the soul of Amphimedon answered him:
121 “Most glorious son of Atreus, lord of men Agamemnon,
122 all these things I remember, Zeus-nurtured king, as you tell them;
123 and to you I shall tell very clearly and truly the whole tale,
124 the evil end of our death, and how it came to completion.
125 We were courting the wife of Odysseus, long absent;
126 and she neither refused outright the hateful marriage nor ended it,
127 all the while preparing for us death and black destruction,
128 but in her mind she devised this other stratagem also:
129 setting a great loom up in the halls, she wove at her weaving,
130 delicate-threaded and wide beyond measure; and quickly she told us:
131 “Young men, my suitors, since noble Odysseus has perished,
132 wait, though eager to wed me, until I finish this fabric,
133 lest the threads of my spinning should perish and come to no purpose,
134 as a burial shroud for the hero Laertes, against the hour
135 when the deadly fate of long-stretching death shall overtake him,
136 lest some Achaean woman somewhere among all the people
137 blame me if one who won much wealth should lie there without a death-shroud.”
138 So she spoke, and our proud heart within us was won to persuasion.
139 Then by day she would weave at the great loom through all the daylight,
140 but by night she unraveled it, once she had set by the torches.
141 Thus for three years she concealed it by guile and persuaded Achaeans;
142 but when the fourth year came on and the seasons arrived with it,
143 as the months waned away and many long days were accomplished,
144 then indeed one of her women spoke out, who knew of it clearly,
145 and we found her unraveling there the splendid weaving.
146 Thus she finished it then, though unwilling, compelled by necessity.
147 But when she showed us the cloth, after weaving the great web,
148 and after washing it, shining like sunlight or like the moonlight,
149 then at that very moment some evil divinity brought great Odysseus
150 from somewhere to the edge of the land where the swineherd was dwelling.
151 There too came the dear son of godlike Odysseus,
152 coming from sandy Pylos aboard his black ship returning.
153 And the two of them, devising evil death for the suitors,
154 came to the famous town; and truly Odysseus came later,
155 while Telemachus went first before him leading the way there.
156 Then the swineherd led him, with poor clothes wrapped on his body,
157 like to a beggar in misery, and like to an old man,
158 leaning upon a staff, and mean clothes hung on his body.
159 None of us could discern him for the man that he truly was there,
160 when he appeared among us so suddenly, not even the elder men.
161 No, we kept on assailing him, hurling foul words and missiles.
162 Yet for a time in his own great halls he endured and was steadfast,
163 pelted with blows and taunts, with a heart that was patient in suffering.
164 But when the mind of Zeus of the aegis lifted and roused him,
165 then with Telemachus he took up the beautiful armor,
166 carried it into the chamber, and fastened the doors with the bolts there.
167 Then by the craft in his heart he instructed his own dear wife there
168 to set for the suitors the bow and the gray iron axes,
169 for us doomed men a contest of pain and beginning of slaughter.
170 Not a man there among us could draw on the string of that great bow,
171 no, we fell very far from the mark; we were wholly deficient.
172 But when at last to the hands of Odysseus the great bow had come there,
173 then all of us shouted together and cried out against it,
174 not to hand him the bow, not even if much he should ask for it;
175 only Telemachus there kept urging him on and commanding.
176 Then in his hand he received it, resourceful enduring Odysseus,
177 and with ease he strung the great bow, and shot through the iron.
178 Then to the threshold he strode, and poured out the swift-flying arrows,
179 glaring around him in dread, and he struck down lordly Antinous.
180 After him then at the rest he kept shooting his arrows of anguish,
181 aiming straight before him; and they kept falling in heaps all around us.
182 It was plain that one of the gods was standing beside them in battle;
183 for then with fury they swept through the hall and struck all before them,
184 killing to right and to left, and hideous groaning arose there
185 as heads were battered; the whole floor steamed with the blood of the fallen.
186 So we perished, Agamemnon; and even now in this moment
187 our bodies lie without care in the halls of Odysseus.
188 For our dear ones at home in the house of each man do not know yet,
189 those who would wash from our wounds the black clotting blood that is on us
190 and lay us out with their mourning; for that is the due of the dead men.”
191 Then in reply the spirit of Atreus' son spoke and addressed him:
192 “Blessed son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
193 surely with great excellence you won for yourself such a woman.
194 How good was the mind in blameless Penelope's bosom,
195 daughter of Icarius, how well she remembered Odysseus,
196 her own wedded husband; therefore her glory shall never perish,
197 no, for her excellence the immortals shall fashion a lovely
198 song upon earth among men for circumspect Penelope.
199 Not like the daughter of Tyndareus, who devised evil actions,
200 slaying her own wedded lord; and hateful song shall attend her
201 out among humankind, and she will bring grievous reproach down
202 upon all womankind, even upon one who acts with goodness.”
203 So the two of them spoke such words to one another, conversing,
204 standing there in the house of Hades, beneath the earth's hidden places.
205 But the others, when down from the city they came, very quickly
206 reached the fair well-fashioned farm of Laertes, which once he
207 gained for himself after long toil and labor in plenty.
208 There was his house, and around it on every side were the shelters
209 where the serving men ate and sat and also took sleep there,
210 thralls of necessity, who worked at the things that he loved there.
211 In the house there was also an old Sicilian serving-woman;
212 carefully she tended the old man there on the farmland,
213 far from the city. There Odysseus spoke to the thralls and his dear son:
214 “Now you others go into the well-built house all together,
215 and for our supper at once sacrifice the best of the swine there.
216 I for my part will make trial now of my father and test him,
217 whether he will know me and mark me well with his own eyes,
218 or fail to know me, his son, after so long a time in absence.”
219 So he spoke, and into the hands of the thralls he gave over the war-gear.
220 Then they went quickly on toward the house; but noble Odysseus
221 moved more near to the orchard rich in fruit, with testing in mind there.
222 Yet descending into the great orchard he found no Dolius,
223 neither any of the thralls nor sons; for they all had gone outward
224 gathering stones for a fence to wall in the orchard and vineyard,
225 and the old man himself was leading the way for the others.
226 So he found his father alone in the well-worked orchard there standing,
227 digging around a young plant; and he wore a befouled tunic,
228 patched and shabby; and round his shins he had fastened of oxhide
229 sewn leggings, to keep from the scratches; and over his hands too
230 he had gloves because of the thorns; and over his head he was wearing
231 a cap of goatskin, feeding the grief that swelled in his spirit.
232 But when enduring noble Odysseus had marked him and known him,
233 worn by old age, with the weight of heavy grief in his spirit,
234 standing beneath a tall pear tree he let the tears fall downward.
235 Then he pondered this way and that in his mind and his great heart,
236 whether to kiss his father and clasp him close in his arms there,
237 and to tell him all, how he had come and reached his own country,
238 or to inquire into all and test him in every particular.
239 As he turned it in mind, this course appeared to him better:
240 first with words of mocking edge to make full trial and test him.
241 Thinking on this, noble Odysseus went straight up before him.
242 There he was, head bowed down, digging around a young tree-root;
243 standing close beside him, his glorious son then addressed him:
244 “Old sir, no want of skill is on you in tending an orchard;
245 truly your care is good; there is nothing at all here neglected,
246 neither a plant nor fig tree, nor vine, nor olive either,
247 neither a pear tree nor any bed in the garden untended.
248 Yet I will tell you one thing more, and do not take it in anger:
249 you yourself are not well cared for, but with your old age upon you
250 wretchedly you are begrimed, and your clothing is mean and dishonored.
251 It is not surely for sloth that your master neglects you;
252 neither does anything slavish appear when one looks at you,
253 neither in shape nor stature; you seem like a man who is kingly.
254 Such indeed you would seem, if you bathed and partook of your supper,
255 and then lay down upon softness; for this is the due of the old ones.
256 But come now, tell me this, and recount it exactly and truly:
257 whose slave are you among men, and whose orchard are you tending?
258 And tell me this as well in truth, so that I may know clearly,
259 whether in fact we have come to this Ithaca, as was told me
260 by a man whom I met just now as I came here upon the roadway,
261 not very sound in his wits, since he had not the patience in him
262 either to tell me all things or listen through to my question
263 touching a guest-friend of mine, whether still he is living and present
264 or by now he is dead and gone to the house of Hades below.
265 For I will tell you this; now attend and listen unto me:
266 once in my own dear fatherland country I welcomed a certain
267 man who had come to my house, and never yet any other
268 stranger from far away came as dear to my dwelling as he did.
269 He declared that his race was from Ithaca, and he told me
270 Laertes son of Arcesius was his father by birthright.
271 Him I led to my house, and I gave him a generous welcome,
272 loving him warmly and well, for there was abundance within doors,
273 and I bestowed on him gifts of friendship, as was fitting:
274 seven whole talents I gave him of gold well wrought by craftsmen,
275 and I gave him a mixing bowl all silver, flowered with ornament,
276 twelve plain cloaks, and as many rugs and spread-coverings with them,
277 just so many fair mantles besides, and tunics upon them,
278 and apart from these, women, four, who knew blameless handiwork,
279 shapely and skilled, and of these he might choose whichever he wished to.”
280 Then his father replied to him, shedding tears as he answered:
281 “Stranger, indeed you have reached the land for which you are asking,
282 but over it now violent men and reckless ones hold sway.
283 Therefore the gifts that you gave him, bestowing ten thousand favors,
284 all were in vain. If only you had found him here in Ithaca
285 living, then surely with gifts in return he would have dismissed you
286 well, and with noble guest-right; for so it is right with the giver.
287 But come now, tell me this, and recount it exactly and truly:
288 what year is this from the time when you welcomed that stranger,
289 your guest-friend, that unhappy man, my son, if ever I had one,
290 luckless child, whom somewhere far from his friends and his fatherland country
291 either fish in the sea have devoured, or else upon dry land
292 he became spoil for beasts and birds; and neither his mother
293 wrapped and lamented him, nor his father, we who begot him;
294 neither his richly dowered wife, wise-hearted Penelope,
295 wailed for her own dear husband upon his bed, as was fitting,
296 drawing his eyes shut down; for this is the due of the departed.
297 And tell me this as well in truth, so that I may know clearly:
298 who and from where are you among men? Where is your city and parentage?
299 Where does the swift ship stand that conveyed you hitherward
300 and your godlike companions? Or did you come as a passenger
301 on another man’s ship, and they put you ashore and departed?”
302 Then resourceful Odysseus in turn made answer and told him:
303 “Then indeed I will tell you all things exactly and truly.
304 I am from Alybas, where my renowned house has its dwelling;
305 I am son of Apheidas, of lordly Polypemon descended;
306 and my own name is Eperitus. Yet some spirit or other
307 drove me astray from Sicania to come here against my wishing.
308 My ship stands yonder away from the town by the edge of the farmland.
309 But as for Odysseus, this now is the fifth turning year-sum
310 since he went away from there and departed out of my country,
311 luckless man. Yet indeed there were favorable birds for his journey,
312 birds on the right, and I rejoiced in them as I sent him onward,
313 and he too was glad as he went; and the heart in us both was still hoping
314 we would meet again in guest-friendship and exchange splendid presents.”
315 So he spoke, and a black cloud of sorrow enveloped the old man.
316 Then with both of his hands he took up the dark sooty dust there
317 and poured it over his white head, groaning heavily, deeply.
318 Then the spirit within Odysseus was stirred; through his nostrils
319 sharp pain struck as he looked on his father beloved before him.
320 Then he sprang and embraced him and kissed him, and thus he addressed him:
321 “Father, why, I am this man myself, whom you have been seeking,
322 home in the twentieth year I have come to the land of my fathers.
323 But hold back now from weeping and tearful lamentation.
324 For I will tell you the truth; yet even so we must hurry:
325 I have slain the suitors inside our own halls and chambers,
326 making them pay for their soul-wounding shame and their evil.”
327 Then in return wise Laertes answered and spoke to him:
328 “If indeed you are Odysseus, my son, and have come here,
329 tell me a sign now, plain to be known, so I may believe you.”
330 Then resourceful Odysseus answered him, saying:
331 “First of all, mark this scar here clearly with your own eyesight,
332 where on Parnassus a boar drove in on me with his white tusk
333 when I had gone there; for you had sent me, and my honored
334 mother as well, to Autolycus, dear father of my mother,
335 so I might receive the gifts he promised me here and assented.
336 But come, let me also recount the trees in the thriving orchard
337 which once you gave to me, when I kept begging for each one,
338 being a child and following you through the garden behind you;
339 there we passed among them, and you named and told them all to me.
340 Thirteen pear trees you gave me, and ten apple trees also,
341 and forty fig trees; and of the vine rows you named me
342 fifty to give, and each one had its season of vintage;
343 there on the vines all manner of clusters were hanging,
344 whenever the seasons of Zeus came pressing them down from above.”
345 So he spoke, and the old man's knees and dear heart were loosened,
346 for he knew the sure signs Odysseus had clearly recounted.
347 Round his beloved son he flung both arms in embrace there;
348 and great-enduring noble Odysseus caught him while swooning.
349 But when again he drew breath and his spirit came back to his bosom,
350 then once more in reply he addressed him and answered:
351 “Father Zeus, then surely the gods still dwell on Olympus,
352 if in truth the suitors have paid for their reckless outrage.
353 Yet now in my heart I terribly fear that at any moment
354 all the Ithacans here will come against us, and they will
355 send out tidings abroad to the towns of the Cephallenians.”
356 Then resourceful Odysseus answered and said to him:
357 “Take heart; do not let these things be matters of care in your spirit.
358 Come, let us go to the house, which lies close here by the orchard.
359 There I sent on ahead Telemachus, and the cowherd,
360 and the swineherd, so they might prepare us supper with all speed.”
361 So after speaking thus, they went to the beautiful dwelling.
362 And when now they arrived at the well-inhabited homestead,
363 there they found Telemachus, and the cowherd, and swineherd
364 carving abundant meat and mixing the gleaming dark wine.
365 Meanwhile in his own house great-hearted Laertes was tended:
366 a Sicilian handmaid bathed him and rubbed him with olive,
367 and then around him she cast a beautiful cloak; and Athena
368 standing close by made strong the limbs of the shepherd of people,
369 and she made him greater to see, and more stalwart than formerly.
370 Out from the bath he stepped, and his dear son marveled at seeing
371 face to face how like to the immortal gods he had become there;
372 then calling to him he addressed him with winged words, saying:
373 “Father, surely one of the gods who are everlasting
374 made you better to look on in stature and beauty of body.”
375 Then in return wise Laertes answered and spoke to him:
376 “If only, father Zeus, and Athena, and lord Apollo,
377 such as I was when I took Nericus, strongly founded,
378 there on the cape of the mainland, as lord of Cephallenians,
379 such a man I had been but yesterday in our own halls,
380 with armor upon my shoulders, to stand by you and defend you
381 against the suitor men; then many of them I would have
382 loosened the knees in the house, and your own heart would have rejoiced there.”
383 Thus then the two of them spoke such things to one another.
384 But when the others had ceased from labor and made ready the banquet,
385 then they sat down in order on benches and chairs in a row there.
386 And just as they were setting their hands to the meal before them,
387 there came old Dolius near, and with him the sons of the old man,
388 wearied out from the field work, because his mother had called them,
389 the old Sicilian woman who raised them and lovingly tended
390 him, the old man, ever since crushing old age overtook him.
391 And when they saw Odysseus and marked him there in their spirits,
392 they stood in the hall astonished; but then Odysseus
393 greeted them kindly and spoke to them, fastening speech on them:
394 “Old sir, sit down to supper, and let your amazement be ended.
395 Long have we waited here in the hall, desiring our supper,
396 holding back from the meal and expecting you always to enter.”
397 So he spoke, and Dolius came straight on, with both hands extended;
398 and Odysseus grasped his hand and kissed him there at the wrist joint.
399 Then calling to him he addressed him with winged words, saying:
400 “Dear friend, since you have returned, long hoped for among us servants,
401 though we had given up hope; yet the gods themselves brought you homeward.
402 Hail, and rejoice exceedingly; may the gods grant you blessings.
403 But tell me this thing truly, so that I may know it clearly:
404 does circumspect Penelope already know of it,
405 that you have come home here, or shall we send her a herald?”
406 Then resourceful Odysseus answered and said to him:
407 “Old man, she knows already. Why should you trouble with this matter?”
408 So he spoke, and again he sat on the well-polished stool there.
409 So likewise the sons of Dolius gathered round noble Odysseus,
410 greeting him warmly with words, and clasping his hands in their own hands,
411 then in due order sat down beside Dolius, their father.
412 Thus they in the halls were intent on the meal before them.
413 But Rumor the messenger quickly went through the whole city,
414 telling abroad the suitors’ hateful death and destruction.
415 And they all heard together, and came from this side and that side,
416 groaning and lamenting before the house of Odysseus.
417 Out from the hall each household bore its dead and buried them;
418 but the others, from outlying towns, they sent to his own home,
419 setting each man on the fishers’ swift and seafaring vessels;
420 and they themselves went massed to the place of assembly, hearts grieving.
421 But when they had gathered and all were together assembled,
422 then among them Eupeithes rose up and addressed them:
423 for unbearable grief for his son lay deep in his spirit,
424 for Antinous, whom glorious Odysseus first had slaughtered.
425 For him shedding tears he spoke in the gathering and said this:
426 “Friends, this man has devised a great outrage against the Achaeans.
427 Some with his ships he led away, many and excellent fighters,
428 and he lost the curved ships, and he destroyed all the people.
429 Then coming back he killed the best by far of the Cephallenians.
430 Come then, before he can quickly get clear to sandy Pylos,
431 or even to shining Elis, where the Epeians are rulers,
432 let us go; else afterward we shall be bowed down forever.
433 For this will be shame for men of later ages to hear of,
434 if we do not avenge the slayers of sons and of brothers.
435 For me there would not be sweetness at all in the life left before me,
436 but I would die with all speed and join those already perished.
437 But let us go, before they cross the strait and escape to the mainland.”
438 So he spoke, shedding tears, and pity seized all the Achaeans.
439 Then Medon came near to them, and the godlike singer with him,
440 out of the halls of Odysseus, since sleep had now released them.
441 In their midst they stood; and astonishment seized every hearer.
442 Then Medon, understanding wise thoughts, spoke out among them:
443 “Hear me now, men of Ithaca; for not without the gods’ will
444 did Odysseus devise and accomplish these deeds in the palace.
445 I myself saw an immortal god, who there beside Odysseus
446 stood very near, and in all things seemed to be Mentor.
447 Now this immortal god would appear before Odysseus,
448 giving him courage, and now through the hall would drive the suitors,
449 rushing among them; and close-packed they tumbled and fell there.”
450 So he spoke, and beneath pale fear all of them were afflicted.
451 Then the old hero Halitherses spoke forth among them,
452 son of Mastor; for he alone saw before and behind them.
453 He with good will toward them addressed the assembly and said this:
454 “Hear me now, men of Ithaca, and heed what I tell you.
455 By your own wickedness, friends, these things have come into being.
456 You would not listen to me, nor yet to Mentor, people’s shepherd,
457 when we said you must make your sons desist from their folly,
458 they who committed a monstrous deed in their evil presumption,

467 But when they had put upon their flesh the glittering bronze there,
468 they all assembled in front of the broad-wayed city together.
469 And among them Eupeithes led on, in his own great folly;
470 for he thought he would avenge the killing done to his son, yet never
471 was he destined to return, but there to encounter his death-spirit.
472 Then Athena addressed Zeus, the son of Cronos, and said this:
473 “Father of us all, son of Cronos, highest of all the mighty,
474 tell me, as I ask you, what plan in your heart are you hiding?
475 Will you stir up again evil war and terrible battle,
476 or instead will you establish friendship between both parties?”
477 Then Zeus the cloud-gatherer answered her and said to her:
478 “My child, why do you ask me of this and inquire into it?
479 Was it not you yourself who shaped this counsel in your thinking,
480 that Odysseus on coming home should exact payment from those men?
481 Do as ever you please; but I shall tell you what is the fitting.
482 Now when godlike Odysseus has punished the suitors,
483 let them cut binding oaths, and let him be king forever;
484 and let us cast a forgetting on slaughter of children
485 and of brothers; and let them keep loving each other
486 just as before, and let there be wealth and peace in abundance.”
487 So he spoke, and he roused Athena, already eager before this;
488 down she darted away from the peaks of Olympus.
489 Now when they had put from them desire for honey-sweet eating,
490 then among them godlike Odysseus, long-suffering, started:
491 “Let some man go outside and see if they now are approaching.”
492 So he spoke, and the son of Dolius went, as he bade him;
493 going he stood on the threshold, and saw that they all were close by;
494 quickly then he addressed Odysseus with winged words, saying:
495 “Here they are now, close at hand; but let us arm all the faster.”
496 So he spoke, and they sprang to their feet and put on their armor,
497 four men around Odysseus, and six of the sons of Dolius;
498 and with them Laertes and Dolius also armed themselves,
499 though they were gray-haired men, yet still by necessity fighters.
500 But when they had clothed all their flesh in the gleaming bronze armor,
501 then they opened the doors and went out, and Odysseus led them.
502 Then there came close by them Athena, the daughter of Zeus, there,
503 like to Mentor in build and in voice altogether.
504 Seeing her, then godlike Odysseus, long-suffering, rejoiced there,
505 and at once to Telemachus, his own dear son, he addressed him:
506 “Telemachus, now you yourself will know this, entering battle,
507 where men fight face to face, and the best men are sifted and chosen:
508 never disgrace the line of your fathers, who long before this time
509 have surpassed all the earth in valor and manly courage.”
510 Then in turn wise Telemachus answered his father and said this:
511 “You will see, if you wish it, dear father, with this heart within me,
512 I shall not bring disgrace on your line, as you tell me.”
513 So he spoke, and Laertes rejoiced and spoke out a saying:
514 “What a day this indeed is for me, dear gods; I am joyful.
515 Son and son's son are contending together concerning prowess.”
516 Then standing beside him gray-eyed Athena addressed him:
517 “Son of Arceisius, dearest by far of all my companions,
518 pray to the gray-eyed maiden and Zeus who is father above her,
519 then with a great swing hurl forth your spear that casts a long shadow.”
520 So she spoke, and Pallas Athena breathed great strength within him.
521 Then he prayed to the daughter of Zeus the mighty in heaven,
522 and with a great swing hurled forth his spear that casts a long shadow,
523 and he struck Eupeithes straight through his bronze-cheeked helmet.
524 Yet the spear was not held by the helm, but the bronze point drove through it;
525 down he crashed as he fell, and his armor clattered upon him.
526 Then on the front fighters Odysseus fell, and his glorious son too,
527 striking with swords and with spears that were sharpened at both ends.
528 Then indeed they would have destroyed them all and deprived them of homecoming,
529 had not Athena, the child of Zeus who bears the aegis,
530 cried out with her voice and held back all the people together:
531 “Hold from the painful war, men of Ithaca, hold now,
532 so that with no more bloodshed you may be parted most quickly.”
533 So spoke Athena, and upon them all pale fear seized hold then;
534 from their hands, in their terror, their weapons all flew and were scattered,
535 and they all fell down on the ground when the goddess had uttered her outcry;
536 then they turned toward the city, desiring the safety of living.
537 Terribly shouted aloud then godlike Odysseus, long-suffering,
538 and with a rush he swooped like an eagle that flies in the high air.
539 Then indeed the son of Cronos let fly his smoky thunderbolt,
540 and it fell there in front of the gray-eyed daughter of the mighty sire.
541 Then to Odysseus spoke again gray-eyed Athena:
542 “Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
543 hold now, and cease from the strife of the war that makes equals of all men,
544 lest in some way Zeus son of Cronos, wide-thundering, be angered.”
545 So spoke Athena, and he obeyed, and his heart was made glad there.
546 Then thereafter she set a sworn peace between both of the parties,
547 Pallas Athena, the child of Zeus who bears the aegis,
548 like to Mentor in build and in voice altogether.
