# The Odyssey — Book 5

*Calypso’s island: the raft, Poseidon’s storm, the landing on Scheria.*

1 Dawn from the bed where she rested beside the lordly Tithonus
2 rose, bearing light to the gods everlasting and mortal humanity.
3 Gods were assembling and taking their seats in the place of their council,
4 Zeus high-thundering among them, whose power is greatest of all gods.
5 There gray-eyed Athena recounted the many cares of Odysseus,
6 thinking of him, for it grieved her he stayed in the house of the sea nymph:
7 “Father Zeus, and you blessed immortals who live without dying,
8 never again let a scepter-bearing king be gentle and kindly,
9 never let one in his heart understand the appropriate justice;
10 let him be harsh in his dealings instead and commit every outrage,
11 since not one of the people remembers the godlike Odysseus,
12 subjects he governed as king, though he was as gentle as father.
13 Now on an island he lies and endures overpowering sorrows,
14 there in the halls of Calypso the nymph, who continues to hold him
15 forcibly there, and he cannot return to the land of his fathers,
16 since no ships with their oars are beside him and no companions
17 able to carry him over the broad and enormous back of the sea-way.
18 Now there are men who intend to murder his well-beloved son
19 during his homeward journey; he sailed out in search of his father,
20 going to sacred Pylos and shining Lacedaemon.”
21 Then cloud-gathering Zeus made answer and spoke to Athena:
22 “Child of my blood, what word has escaped from the fence of your tooth-row?
23 Did you yourself not frame this counsel and plan it beforehand,
24 how on returning Odysseus should punish the men for their outrage?
25 Guide young Telemachus now with your skill, for you have the power,
26 so he returns to the land of his fathers untouched by affliction,
27 while in their vessel the suitors return with no deed accomplished.”
28 Thus having spoken, he turned to the well-loved son Hermes and told him:
29 “Hermes, since you are messenger also in all other matters,
30 tell to the nymph with the beautiful hair our unalterable counsel:
31 steadfast Odysseus now shall return from his long deprivation,
32 having no escort of gods everlasting or mortal humanity.
33 Suffering pain on a raft made of timbers securely united,
34 he on the twentieth day shall arrive at the rich land Scheria,
35 country of godlike Phaeacians, whose lineage is close to immortals.
36 They in the depth of their hearts shall honor the man like a god there,
37 send him away in a ship to the well-loved land of his fathers,
38 giving him clothing abundant and bronze and gold for his treasure,
39 more than Odysseus ever had carried away from the Trojan
40 country if safe he had come with the fair share allotted in plunder.
41 So it is fated for him to behold his friends and arrive at
42 home with its high-built roof and the country and land of his fathers.”
43 Thus did he speak, and the guide who had slaughtered Argus obeyed him.
44 Straightway under his feet he fastened the beautiful sandals,
45 golden and deathless, that carry him over the breadth of the water,
46 also the measureless land, with the rushing and breath of the windstorm.
47 Then he took hold of the wand with which he enchants human eyelids,
48 those he desires, and awakens again other men from their sleeping.
49 Holding it firmly, the powerful slaughterer of Argus was flying.
50 Down on Pieria first he alighted, then dropped to the sea-way,
51 skimming along on the surface of waves in the form of a shearwater,
52 one that across the tremendous troughs of the desolate sea-way
53 hunts for the fish and repeatedly wets its wings in the salt brine.
54 Such was the manner that Hermes rode over crowding wave-crests.
55 Yet when he reached that faraway island and came to its shore there,
56 out from the violet-colored water he stepped onto dry land,
57 onward he went till he came to the mighty cave where the goddess
58 nymph with the beautiful hair made her dwelling, and found her within it.
59 There on the hearth an enormous fire was blazing, and fragrance
60 carried afar from the burning and split cedar wood and the citron,
61 filling the island. Within, the goddess sang beautifully, moving
62 back and forth by the loom as she wove with a shuttle of gold-work.
63 High and luxuriant forest had grown all around the great cavern:
64 alder and black-colored poplar and sweet-scented cypress together.
65 There the long-winged birds of the air were accustomed to roosting,
66 little horned owls and hawks and the crows of the sea with long tongues there,
67 all who continually take their employment and food from the water.
68 Over the hollow cave a flourishing vine had been trained there,
69 grown to its prime and abundantly heavy with clusters of grapes now.
70 Four flowing springs in a row poured out their crystalline water,
71 close to each other, though each was directed along its own pathway.
72 Soft-colored meadows of violets and wild celery round them
73 blossomed; and even a god everlasting arriving among them
74 would look on in astonishment there and be gladdened in spirit.
75 There stood the guide who had slaughtered Argus and gazed in amazement.
76 Then, when his heart had been filled with delight in the whole of the vision,
77 straightway into the broad cave he entered; and radiant Calypso,
78 shining among the goddesses, knew who he was when she saw him.
79 Gods everlasting are never unknown to each other in meeting,
80 even if one makes his dwelling afar from the rest of immortals.
81 Yet he did not discover the great-hearted lord Odysseus
82 there, for the man sat weeping as always he sat on the seashore,
83 rending the heart in his bosom with tears and with groaning and sorrow,
84 gazing across the desolate sea while the tears still fell downward.
85 Radiant Calypso, the goddess, inquired of Hermes and asked him,
86 seating him there in a chair that was beautifully bright in its shining:
87 “Why have you come to me, Hermes who carries the gold wand, my honored
88 guest and beloved? You never before were accustomed to visit.
89 Tell what you hold in your mind, for my heart is determined to do it,
90 if I have power to do it, and if it is able to be done.
91 Yet come inside in the first place and let me set food there before you.”
92 Thus having spoken, the goddess drew up a table beside him,
93 heaping ambrosia over it, mixing the red-colored nectar.
94 Then the guide and the slaughterer of Argus was drinking and eating.
95 After the god had partaken of food and encouraged his spirit,
96 then he replied to the goddess in words and gave answer directly:
97 “Goddess, you ask me, a god, why I come, and I shall not conceal it;
98 all I shall tell you in truth, since you have commanded the answer.
99 Zeus ordered me to come here, though I had no desire for the journey.
100 Who by his choice would cross such a measureless distance of salt sea,
101 past all description, where no mortal city is near us to offer
102 either the victims that please all the gods or appropriate hecatombs?
103 Yet no other god can evade or bring to frustration
104 any intention of Zeus who possesses and carries the aegis.
105 He says a man lives beside you, the most wretched warrior
106 out of the heroes who fought nine years round Priam’s city,
107 sacked it at last in the tenth and departed to sail for his homeland.
108 Yet on the homeward journey they sinned against goddess Athena,
109 who stirred a terrible wind and enormous waves of the sea-way.
110 There all the rest of his excellent traveling comrades were ruined,
111 while to this island the wind and the wave carried only Odysseus.
112 Zeus now commands you to send that man on his journey, and quickly.
113 It is not fated for him to die here afar from his people;
114 still it is fated for him to behold his friends and arrive at
115 home with its high-built roof and the country and land of his fathers.”
116 Thus did he speak, and the radiant goddess Calypso was trembling.
117 Raising her voice, she addressed him, and these words took wing as she uttered:
118 “Pitiless gods, more jealous than every being beside you!
119 You feel resentment when goddesses openly sleep beside mortal
120 men, if a goddess should take one to be her beloved husband.
121 So when the rose-fingered Dawn took Orion to be her companion,
122 you who are gods living free of affliction resented her pleasure,
123 till in Ortygia the chaste gold-throned goddess Artemis found him,
124 coming against him with gentle arrows and killing him swiftly.
125 So when Demeter the goddess with beautiful hair obeyed her
126 heart and in love lay down by Iasion there in the furrow,
127 fallow ground plowed three times, it was not long hidden from great Zeus,
128 who with a cast of the glittering lightning destroyed him completely.
129 So now, gods everlasting, you grudge me a mortal companion.
130 I was the one who delivered him, riding astride of the keel there,
131 after the lightning of Zeus had struck his swift-running vessel,
132 splitting it there in the midst of the sea with its wine-colored water.
133 There all the rest of his excellent traveling comrades were ruined,
134 while to this island the wind and the wave carried only Odysseus.
135 I gave him love and nourishment there, and I promised to make him
136 deathless and free from the withering touch of old age all his lifetime.
137 Yet no other god can evade or bring to frustration
138 any intention of Zeus who possesses and carries the aegis.
139 Let the man go, if Zeus now urges and gives the commandment,
140 over the desolate sea; but I shall not be his conveyor.
141 No ships with their oars are beside me and no companions
142 able to carry him over the broad and enormous back of the sea-way.
143 Yet with a willing heart I shall counsel him, hiding no knowledge,
144 so he returns to the land of his fathers untouched by affliction.”
145 Then the guide and the slaughterer of Argus replied to the goddess:
146 “Send him away in this manner, and guard against angering great Zeus,
147 lest he should turn afterward in his wrath and bring punishment on you.”
148 Thus having spoken, the powerful slaughterer of Argus departed.
149 Then the queenly nymph went seeking the great-hearted lord Odysseus,
150 once she had heard and attended the messenger sent her from great Zeus.
151 There on the shore she found him; his eyes were continually tearful,
152 never were dry, while the sweetness of life kept draining away in
153 grief for the homeward return, since the nymph no longer could please him.
154 True, in the nighttime he slept by her side, for she forced him to do so,
155 there in the hollow caves, an unwilling man by a willing goddess;
156 yet in the daytime he sat on the rocks and the beaches around them,
157 rending the heart in his bosom with tears and with groaning and sorrow,
158 gazing across the desolate sea while the tears still fell downward.
159 Standing beside him, the radiant goddess addressed him and told him:
160 “Sorrowful man, do not grieve here longer or let your existence
161 waste itself further away; I am ready entirely to send you.
162 Come now, cut long timbers and fasten with bronze the broad structure
163 of a raft, and construct on its surface a high-rising half-deck,
164 so it may carry you over the mist-covered face of the sea-way.
165 Bread I shall set in the vessel and water and red-colored wine there,
166 all to your heart’s satisfaction, to keep off the pain of your hunger.
167 Clothing I give you and send an appropriate wind to your sternward,
168 so you return to the land of your fathers untouched by affliction,
169 if this is willed by the gods everlasting who govern broad heaven,
170 they who in purpose and action are stronger and mightier than I.”
171 Thus did she speak, and the steadfast and noble Odysseus trembled.
172 Raising his voice, he addressed her, and these words took wing as he uttered:
173 “Goddess, you plan something else in this place, not my safe expedition,
174 telling me I should cross on a raft the tremendous gulf of the sea-way,
175 terrible, painful, which even the swift and beautifully fitted ships do not
176 cross, though they take great delight in a favoring wind sent by great Zeus.
177 Never shall I set foot on a raft if you are unwilling,
178 not till you bring yourself, goddess, to swear the tremendous oath to me
179 you do not plan any other affliction or evil against me.”
180 Thus did he speak, and the radiant goddess Calypso was smiling.
181 Stroking him there with her hand, she addressed him aloud by his name there:
182 “Truly you are a deceiver and never unpracticed in cunning,
183 since you considered and spoke such a careful expression before me.
184 Witness it now, O Earth and the measureless Heaven above us,
185 also the downward-dripping water of Styx, the most solemn
186 oath and the most to be dreaded by gods everlasting in swearing:
187 I do not plan any other affliction or evil against you.
188 No, I am thinking and giving the counsel that I would determine
189 there for myself if a similar need should arise and compel me.
190 Honest and righteous the mind in my bosom, and never a heart made
191 out of implacable iron is mine; it understands pity.”
192 Thus having spoken, the radiant goddess proceeded before him
193 rapidly, while in the steps of the goddess he followed her onward.
194 Both then entered the hollow cave, both goddess and mortal.
195 He sat down on the chair where Hermes had lately been sitting,
196 while every kind of the food that mortal humanity eats there
197 also the drink was brought by the nymph and positioned before him.
198 She took a seat directly across from the godlike Odysseus;
199 servants set nectar and heavenly food at the side of the goddess.
200 Both reached their hands to the excellent food that was ready before them.
201 Then, when desire for food and for drinking was put from their bodies,
202 radiant Calypso the goddess began to address lord Odysseus:
203 “Zeus-born Laertes’ son, resourceful and noble Odysseus,
204 so you now wish to depart for the well-loved land of your fathers,
205 straightway, this moment? Depart, and may joy go beside you regardless.
206 Yet if you knew in the depth of your heart what abundance of sorrow
207 fate has appointed for you ere you come to the land of your fathers,
208 you would remain here with me and would keep this house, being deathless,
209 even although you long every day to behold your own consort,
210 she for whose presence you ache with a constant desire in your lifetime.
211 Yet I declare I am neither inferior to her in appearance
212 nor in the stature I carry, since never is it appropriate
213 mortal women compete with immortals in bodily form or in beauty.”
214 Then the resourceful Odysseus answered the goddess and told her:
215 “Goddess and queen, do not now be angry with me; I understand it.
216 Well do I know that the thoughtful and steadfast Penelope does not
217 equal your stature or beauty to look on, but is far beneath you,
218 since she is mortal and you are immortal and free of all aging.
219 Still I desire and I long every day of my whole life to travel
220 homeward and finally look on the day of my homeward returning.
221 If some god on the wine-colored sea should again bring me shipwreck,
222 still I shall bear it, the heart in my bosom experienced in sorrow.
223 Much I have suffered already and labored in waves and in battle;
224 let this affliction be added to all the afflictions that came first.”
225 Thus he spoke, while the sun sank down and the twilight descended.
226 Into the innermost hollow of cavern the two then proceeded,
227 taking delight in the pleasure of love and remaining together.
228 When Dawn in her earliness rose with her rose-colored fingers,
229 straightway the lord Odysseus clothed himself in mantle and tunic.
230 She, the divine nymph, put on a silvery mantle around her,
231 finely and gracefully woven, and fastened around her a beautiful
232 girdle of gold, then covered the crown of her head with a veil there.
233 Then she considered the sending away of the great Odysseus.
234 Into his palms she fitted a massive ax for his labor,
235 fashioned of bronze and sharpened alike on each side of the ax head,
236 fitted with beautiful handle of olive wood, solid and well made.
237 Then she presented a smoothly polished adze and conducted
238 him to the island’s furthest extreme, where the tallest trees flourished:
239 alder and black-colored poplar and fir trees touching the heavens,
240 long ago dried and seasoned, and light for his sailing on water.
241 After she showed him the place where all of the tallest trees flourished,
242 radiant Calypso returned to her home and abandoned him working.
243 He began cutting the timber, and work went swiftly before him.
244 Twenty trees altogether he felled, then trimmed with the bronze blade,
245 skillfully smoothed them all and made straight by the line of the builder.
246 Meanwhile radiant Calypso the goddess brought augers before him;
247 through every timber he bored and fitted one closely to other,
248 fastening all with the mortising craft and the pegs of the builder.
249 Wide as the curving hull of a broad and burdened merchantman
250 measured by one who is thoroughly skilled in the craft of construction,
251 so broadly did Odysseus make and construct the raft body.
252 Half-decks he set in their place and attached to the close-fitted cross ribs,
253 finishing all with the long-running planks that would serve as the gunwales.
254 Therein he fashioned a mast and a yard that was fitted upon it;
255 also he fashioned a steering oar that would hold to the course there.
256 All from the stern to the bow he surrounded with braided willow
257 barriers warding the wave and abundant brush used for ballast.
258 Meanwhile radiant Calypso brought cloth for the making of sailcloth,
259 which he constructed as well with the skill and the craft he possessed there,
260 fastening braces and halyards and sheets in their places securely.
261 Then with his levers he hauled the raft to the glittering salt sea.
262 This was the fourth day, and all of the work had been brought to completion.
263 There on the fifth day radiant Calypso dispatched him,
264 bathing his body and clothing him there in sweet-smelling garments.
265 One skin dark with the wine the goddess provided aboard it,
266 also another and greater for water, provisions in leather
267 sack, and abundant comforting meats for the wearisome journey.
268 Then she provided a wind blowing harmless and warm from behind him.
269 Glad in the wind, the noble Odysseus spread out the sailcloth,
270 skillfully guiding the raft with the steering oar in his two hands,
271 seated; and never did sleep come down on his eyes for the nighttime,
272 watching the Pleiades, watching Boötes late in his setting,
273 watching the Bear that is known to humanity also as Wagon,
274 turning in place and continually watching the hunter Orion,
275 only the starry formation that never bathes in the ocean.
276 Radiant Calypso the goddess had given him careful instruction
277 he should keep this on the left of his hand while he traveled the sea-way.
278 Seventeen days he continued to sail, making way over water.
279 Then on the eighteenth day the shadowy mountains appeared there,
280 mountains of the Phaeacian country, the part that was closest;
281 like a round shield they appeared to him over the mist-covered sea-way.
282 Yet the strong shaker of earth, returning from Ethiopians,
283 saw him from far off out of the mountains of Solyma; plainly
284 visible there he sailed, and the god grew angrier in spirit.
285 Shaking his head, he addressed his own heart and considered the matter:
286 “Curse it! The gods have surely revised their intentions concerning
287 lord Odysseus while I remained with the Ethiopians.
288 Here he is close to the Phaeacian country where fate has appointed
289 he shall escape from the final noose of the pain that has bound him.
290 Still I declare I shall drive him deep enough into affliction.”
291 Thus having spoken, he gathered the clouds and stirred up the sea-way,
292 grasping his trident; and every squall of the winds he awakened,
293 every direction, and covered both land and the sea with the storm cloud;
294 night rose suddenly upward and outward from heaven’s expanses.
295 East Wind and South Wind fell on him together and hard-blowing West Wind,
296 also the clear-born North Wind rolling a mountainous wave there.
297 Then were Odysseus’ knees unstrung and the heart in his body;
298 deeply disturbed, he addressed his own great-hearted spirit and told it:
299 “Sorrowful man that I am! What happens to me at the ending?
300 Now I am frightened the goddess revealed everything without error,
301 saying that I on the sea, ere I came to the land of my fathers,
302 must fill my measure of pains, and it all is coming to fullness.
303 See how Zeus crowns all of the breadth of the heaven with storm cloud,
304 stirring the sea, while the squalls of the whole of the winds are assailing,
305 every direction; and now my destruction is altogether certain.
306 Three times blessed were the Danaans, four times blessed, who were slaughtered
307 in those years in broad Troy, doing kindness to Atreus’ two sons!
308 Would that I too had died there and met my allotted destruction,
309 there on the day when innumerable Trojans were casting
310 bronze-headed spears at my body above the dead offspring of Peleus.
311 Then I had won full rites for my death and Achaeans had carried my glory;
312 now it is fated I die by a miserable form of destruction.”
313 Just as he spoke, an enormous wave came rushing from high up,
314 terribly driving upon him and whirling the raft in a circle.
315 Far from the raft he was thrown, and the steering oar from his fingers
316 flew, while the mast was snapped in the middle by terrible tempest,
317 winds from every direction converging and striking together;
318 far off into the water the sail and the yard were carried.
319 Long underneath the water the wave held the suffering hero;
320 swiftly he could not arise from beneath the enormous wave’s downrush,
321 since he was burdened with clothes that the radiant Calypso had given.
322 Yet in the end he rose and spat from his mouth bitter salt brine,
323 streaming abundantly down from the crown of his head in great torrents.
324 Yet even so, though exhausted, he never forgot the raft body;
325 lunging amid the waves, he caught hold of its timbers and mounted,
326 sitting himself in the midst and evading the ending of death there.
327 Great waves carried it here and there in the force of the current.
328 Just as the northern wind in the autumn bears thistledown over
329 open plains, while the clusters are clinging securely together,
330 so the winds carried it here and there over open seawater.
331 Now the South Wind tossed it across to the North Wind to carry,
332 now in his turn East Wind surrendered the raft to the West Wind.
333 Then she perceived him, the beautiful-ankled daughter of Cadmus,
334 Ino the White Goddess, once a mortal with mortal expression,
335 now in the depths of the salt sea sharing the honor of godhead.
336 She took pity on lord Odysseus, driven and worn by affliction;
337 taking the form of a shearwater, up from the sea she came flying,
338 perched on the close-fitted raft and addressed him and gave him instruction:
339 “Sorrowful man, why has earth-shaking lord Poseidon become so
340 terribly hostile to you, that he plants such a harvest of evil?
341 Still he shall not destroy you, although his fury is raging.
342 Do as I tell you exactly, for you do not seem without wisdom.
343 Strip off the clothing you wear and abandon the raft to the windstorm;
344 strike out swimming by force of your hands on the homeward return to
345 land of Phaeacians, where it is fated you flee from destruction.
346 Here, spread this deathless veil underneath the breadth of your chest now.
347 It is immortal; you need not fear any pain or destruction.
348 Yet at the moment your hands have taken a hold upon dry land,
349 loosen it once more and cast it afar in the wine-colored sea-way,
350 far from the land, and turn your face in the opposite quarter.”
351 Thus having spoken, the goddess presented the veil to Odysseus;
352 then she herself sank back underneath the turbulent sea swell,
353 taking the form of a shearwater; dark-colored waves closed above her.
354 Then the steadfast and noble Odysseus thought of the matter;
355 deeply disturbed, he addressed his own great-hearted spirit and told it:
356 “Sorrow! I fear some immortal is weaving another deception,
357 telling me I must leave my raft and surrender it wholly.
358 No, I shall not yet obey, for afar with my own eyes
359 I saw the land where the goddess declared that my refuge awaited.
360 This I shall do, since it seems in my mind to be greatly the best thing:
361 while all the timbers continue to hold in the fastenings firmly,
362 so long I stay on the raft and endure the afflictions upon it;
363 yet at the moment the wave has broken the raft into pieces,
364 then I shall swim, since no better counsel appears to my thinking.”
365 While in the depth of his mind and his spirit he turned these reflections,
366 earth-shaking lord Poseidon uplifted a mountainous wave there,
367 terrible, savage, and arching above him, and hurled it upon him.
368 Just as a turbulent wind tosses up a dry pile of chaff there,
369 scattering all of the wisps in one direction and other,
370 so it scattered the long raft timbers; but lord Odysseus
371 mounted astride of a single beam like a man on a racehorse,
372 stripping away the clothes that the radiant Calypso had given.
373 Straightway under his chest he spread out the veil of the goddess,
374 plunged headfirst in the sea with his arms spread wide for the swimming,
375 eager to swim; and the powerful shaker of earth then perceived him.
376 Shaking his head, he addressed his own heart and considered the matter:
377 “So now suffer abundant afflictions and drift in the sea-way,
378 till you arrive at the people whom Zeus has nurtured and favored.
379 Still I imagine you shall not make little of all your affliction.”
380 Thus having spoken, he lashed the beautiful-maned team of horses,
381 coming to Aegae, the place where his glorious dwelling is founded.
382 Yet the gray-eyed goddess Athena devised a new counsel.
383 All of the pathways of all other winds she bound fast and securely,
384 ordering every wind to lie down and to slumber in stillness.
385 Only the rapidly blowing North she aroused and broke wave-crests,
386 so that illustrious Zeus-born Odysseus might reach the oar-loving
387 people Phaeacian, fleeing the doom that is death and destruction.
388 Two nights long and two whole days on the densely packed sea swell
389 there he was driven, and often his heart looked straight at destruction.
390 Yet when the beautiful-haired Dawn brought the third day upon him,
391 then at last the wind lay down, and a calm without wind was
392 spread; and he suddenly saw the land very near to his body,
393 sharply observing ahead as a mountainous wave raised him upward.
394 Just as the life of a father is welcome and dear to his children,
395 one who in sickness lies and endures overpowering sorrows,
396 wasting away for a long time, a hateful divinity haunting,
397 till to their joy the gods free him at last from the evil affliction,
398 so did the land and the trees show welcome and dear to Odysseus.
399 Hard he swam onward, desiring to set both his feet on the dry land.
400 Yet when as far from the shore as a voice crying loudly can carry,
401 then he could hear the sea booming against all the teeth of the rock face.
402 Great waves thundered and broke on the outermost edge of the dry land,
403 terribly belching, while all of the coast was surrounded with salt spray.
404 No ship-holding harbor was there and no roadstead afforded;
405 only projecting headlands and rock teeth and high-rising cliff walls.
406 Then were Odysseus’ knees unstrung and the heart in his body;
407 deeply disturbed, he addressed his own great-hearted spirit and told it:
408 “Sorrow! Now Zeus lets me see land beyond expectation,
409 after I finally crossed the whole of this gulf of the sea-way,
410 yet no escape from the gray-colored water is anywhere shown me.
411 Sharp-running crags lie offshore, and around them a wave is continually
412 roaring and churning, while steeply the rock rises upward behind them.
413 Deep is the sea at the shoreline, and nowhere am I able to stand on
414 both of my feet and escape from the evil that comes to destroy me.
415 If I attempt to climb outward, a mountainous wave may seize me,
416 hurl me against the solid stone, and my struggle be fruitless.
417 Yet if I swim still further along in the hope of discovering
418 gently shelving beaches or harbors protected from ocean,
419 then I am frightened the squall of the storm shall again seize me upward,
420 carry me heavily groaning across the fish-thronging sea-way;
421 or some divinity sends a monster against me from salt brine,
422 one from the swarm that the famous Amphitrite feeds in the sea depth,
423 since I know how the famous shaker of earth has become hostile to me.”
424 While in the depth of his mind and his spirit he turned these reflections,
425 then an enormous wave brought him straight to the jagged-edged shoreline.
426 There would the skin have been stripped and his bones have been broken asunder,
427 had not the gray-eyed goddess Athena suggested a counsel.
428 Lunging ahead, with both of his hands he took hold of the rock face,
429 clinging and groaning until the enormous wave finally passed him.
430 Thus he escaped it; but backward the water came leaping against him,
431 striking and throwing him far from the land in the midst of the sea-way.
432 Just as an octopus dragged from the depth of its den in the seafloor
433 has many pebbles adhering to cups on the ends of its tentacles,
434 so from the bold hands of the hero the skin was stripped on the rock face;
435 over his head the enormous wave closed and covered Odysseus.
436 There before destiny wretched Odysseus surely had perished,
437 had not the gray-eyed goddess Athena provided him clear sense.
438 Up from the surf where the water came belching against the dry shoreline,
439 swimming outside it, he looked to the coast in the hope of discovering
440 gently shelving beaches or harbors protected from ocean.
441 Yet when in swimming he came to the mouth of a beautiful river,
442 there at last did the ground seem best for the man in his judgment,
443 free of all stones and provided with shelter from wind and the tempest.
444 Feeling the current that flowed, in the depth of his heart he petitioned:
445 “Hear me, O lord, whoever you are; I come much imploring,
446 fleeing the sea and the threatening anger of lord Poseidon.
447 Even to gods everlasting a wandering man is held sacred
448 when he arrives as a fugitive, just as I now have arrived at
449 both your stream and your knees after suffering many afflictions.
450 Pity me, lord, for I call and declare myself now your suppliant.”
451 Thus did he speak, and the god stayed the current and held back the wave there,
452 calming the water before him and bringing him safe to the river’s
453 mouth. He bent both his knees and let both of his powerful hands fall;
454 utterly crushed by the sea was the heart in the depth of his bosom.
455 All of his flesh was swollen, and seawater streamed from his body,
456 running in torrents from mouth and nostrils; without breath or speech there
457 down he lay, barely living, and terrible weariness seized him.
458 Yet when at last he recovered his breath and his spirit was gathered,
459 then from his body he loosened the veil of the goddess and cast it
460 into the seaward-flowing river; an enormous wave bore it
461 backward and down on the current, and Ino immediately took it
462 into her well-loved hands. From the river Odysseus turned then,
463 sank in the reeds, and he kissed the grain-giving breadth of the plowland.
464 Deeply disturbed, he addressed his own great-hearted spirit and told it:
465 “Sorrow! What happens to me? What shall come at the end of my struggle?
466 If I keep anxious watch through the night by the side of the river,
467 cold that is evil together with dew that is soaking may finish
468 me, since I have no strength and my spirit is gasping and weary;
469 while toward the dawn cold wind blows out from the stream of the river.
470 Yet if I climb this slope to the shade of the forest and settle
471 down in the densely grown brush, if the cold and the weariness leave me,
472 letting the sweetness of sleep come over my body and hold me,
473 then I am frightened wild beasts shall make me their spoil and their carrion.”
474 Yet as he weighed it, this seemed in his mind to be greatly the better.
475 Up to the forest he went and discovered it close to the water,
476 there on a rise in the clearing; he crept underneath double thicket,
477 two trees sprung from a single stock, one wild and one cultivated.
478 Neither the force of the moist-blowing winds could penetrate through them,
479 nor could the shining sun strike through with the light of its sunbeams,
480 nor could the rain soak through, for the two trees were densely entangled,
481 each interlaced with the other. Beneath them the lord Odysseus
482 crept and immediately heaped up a broad bed there with his strong hands,
483 since an abundant drift of the leaves that had fallen was lying,
484 ample to cover and shelter two men, or even three mortals,
485 there in the winter season, although the weather were bitter.
486 Seeing the bed, the steadfast and noble Odysseus rejoiced there,
487 lay in the midst, and poured the abundance of fallen leaves over him.
488 Just as a man in a pile of the dark-colored ashes will bury
489 one burning log at the furthest edge of his fields, with no neighbors,
490 saving the seed of the fire so he need not seek it from elsewhere,
491 so in the leaves did Odysseus bury himself; and Athena
492 poured sleep over his eyes, to release him as quickly as might be
493 out of his toil-worn weariness, closing his well-loved eyelids.
