# The Odyssey — Book 19

*The interview by the fire; the scar; the contest announced.*

1 But in the hall there stayed behind noble Odysseus,
2 plotting the suitors’ slaughter together with Pallas Athena.
3 Quickly he spoke then out to Telemachus winged words:
4 “Telemachus, you must lay up the war-gear away in the inner room,
5 every piece; and the suitors, if longing they ask you about them,
6 you must put them aside with gentle and soft-spoken wording:
7 “Out of the smoke I removed them, because they no longer resembled
8 what kind of arms Odysseus left here when he journeyed to Troy-land;
9 no, they are blackened all over, wherever the breath of the fire came.
10 And besides, some god put even this greater thought in my spirit:
11 lest somehow, drunken with wine, when you kindle a quarrel among you,
12 you should wound one another and bring disgrace on the feasting
13 and on the courtship; for iron itself draws a man onward.’”
14 So he spoke, and Telemachus obeyed his beloved father.
15 Out he called to the nurse and spoke to Eurycleia:
16 “Nurse, come now, shut up the women apart in their quarters for me,
17 while I put in a chamber the gear and the armor of my father,
18 beautiful things, which all through the house uncared-for the smoke dims,
19 since my father went away; and I was still then but a small child.
20 Now I desire to lay them aside where the breath of the fire will not reach.”
21 Back to him spoke in return the dear nurse Eurycleia:
22 “If only, my child, you would truly take thought and good prudence at last,
23 caring now for the house and guarding all of its substance.
24 But come, who then will carry a light as he goes there before you,
25 since you do not allow the maidservants to go out, who would light you?”
26 Once more wise Telemachus spoke aloud and answered her:
27 “This stranger here; for I will not keep any man in idleness
28 who touches my meal-bin, even if he has come from afar off.”
29 So he spoke, and for her the word he had uttered was wingless.
30 She locked the doors of the strong-built and well-inhabited hall.
31 Up sprang the two of them, Odysseus and his glorious son,
32 carrying inward the helmets and bossed shields together
33 and the sharp-pointed spears; and before them Pallas Athena,
34 holding a golden lamp in her hand, made wonderful brightness.
35 Then Telemachus quickly addressed his father and said this:
36 “Father, indeed I behold with my eyes here a marvel full great.
37 Surely the walls of the hall, and the handsome cross-beams within it,
38 and the fir-wood rafters, and the pillars towering upward,
39 shine in my eyes as though from the blaze of a burning fire.
40 Truly some god is within, one of those who possess wide heaven.”
41 Him resourceful Odysseus answered, saying in turn:
42 “Silence now, keep it within your mind, and do not be asking.
43 Such indeed is the custom of gods who hold high Olympus.
44 But you go now to bed, and I shall remain here behind you,
45 so I may still provoke the maidservants a while, and your mother;
46 and she in her grief will inquire of me closely on every matter.”
47 So he spoke, and Telemachus went through the hall and departed
48 onward into his chamber beneath the bright glare of the torches,
49 where indeed he would sleep in the past whenever sweet sleep came on.
50 There then he lay down also and waited for Dawn on her godly throne.
51 But in the hall there stayed behind noble Odysseus,
52 plotting the suitors’ slaughter together with Pallas Athena.
53 Forth from her chamber came circumspect and wise Penelope,
54 like to Artemis truly, or golden Aphrodite.
55 Beside the hearth they placed for her the chair where she used to be seated,
56 inlaid and turned with ivory and silver, which once a craftsman,
57 Icmalius, made, and beneath it a stool for the feet he had fitted,
58 joined to the chair itself, and over it broad fleece was thrown.
59 There then sat down circumspect and wise Penelope.
60 In from the hall there came the white-armed women attendants.
61 Much bread they took away from the boards, and the tables together,
62 and the cups from which those overweening men had been drinking.
63 Down from the braziers they cast the fire onto the hearth-floor,
64 and heaped much wood upon them afresh, for light and for warming.
65 But Melantho upbraided Odysseus a second time, saying:
66 “Stranger, will you still even now annoy us all through the night here,
67 wandering round through the house, and spying upon all the women?
68 Out through the doors with you, wretch, and be glad for the meal you have eaten,
69 or soon enough you will go when struck with a blazing torchbrand.”
70 Glowering darkly upon her, wise Odysseus answered and said:
71 “Strange woman, why do you set on me with a spirit so angered?
72 Is it because I am filthy, and wear poor clothes on my body,
73 and because I beg through the land? For necessity drives me to do it.
74 Such indeed are the beggars and men who wander in sorrow.
75 I too once dwelt in a house among men in the old days,
76 blessed and wealthy, and many a time I would give to a wanderer,
77 such as he might be, and whatever the need was that brought him.
78 Slaves I had beyond number, and many other possessions,
79 all by which men live well and are called prosperous and wealthy.
80 But Zeus son of Cronos destroyed it, since somehow it pleased him.
81 Therefore see to it, woman, lest you too lose all the splendor
82 by which now among all the maidservants you are distinguished;
83 lest at some time your mistress, angered, deal harshly against you,
84 or Odysseus return; for still there remains some hope of him.
85 But if indeed he has perished that way and no homecoming waits him,
86 still by Apollo's favor his son is such already,
87 Telemachus; and no woman within these halls can escape him
88 doing some reckless wrong, since he is no longer so childish.”
89 So he spoke, and circumspect Penelope listened and heard him,
90 and she rebuked the handmaid and spoke out a word and addressed her:
91 “Certainly, shameless and bold one, bitch without fear, I perceive you
92 doing this great deed, which you will wipe from your own head surely.
93 For you knew everything well, since from my own mouth you had heard it,
94 how in my halls I intended to question the stranger
95 about my husband, since I am afflicted with careful grief for him.”
96 So she spoke, and then to the steward Eurynome said this:
97 “Eurynome, bring here a chair, and spread fleeces upon it,
98 so that the stranger may sit there and speak out his word and may hear me;
99 for I desire to question him closely and learn all about him.”
100 So she spoke, and the woman came quickly, eager in service,
101 brought a well-polished chair and threw on top of it fleeces;
102 then there sat down at last long-enduring noble Odysseus.
103 First to speak among them was circumspect Penelope:
104 “Stranger, this first thing now I myself shall ask you directly:
105 who among men are you, and from where? Where are city and parents?”
106 Answering her, resourceful Odysseus spoke thus before her:
107 “Lady, no mortal anywhere over the boundless earth could
108 find any fault with you; for your fame reaches heaven's wide spaces,
109 like the fame of some blameless king, who in fear of the immortals
110 rules over many men, both numerous and mighty in valor,
111 and upholds justice; and under him the black earth is yielding
112 wheat and barley, and the trees are burdened and heavy with fruitage;
113 the flocks bear steady increase, and the sea provides men with fishes
114 through his good government, and the people flourish beneath him.
115 Therefore ask me now in your halls of all the other matters,
116 but do not question my lineage and fatherland any further,
117 lest you fill my spirit still more full of painful afflictions
118 as I remember; for I am indeed most burdened with sorrows.
119 It is not fitting that I in another man's house should be sitting
120 weeping and mourning; for grieving without cease is the worse thing.
121 Else some maidservant may take offense at me, or you as well may,
122 and may say that with wine I am making my mind swim in teardrops.”
123 Him circumspect Penelope in answer addressed and told him:
124 “Stranger, indeed my beauty and form and comeliness wholly
125 were destroyed by the deathless gods when the Argives departed
126 sailing to Ilium, and with them went my husband Odysseus.
127 If only he might return and care for the life that is in me,
128 then would my fame be greater and thus would my beauty be fairer.
129 But now I am consumed with grief, for the god has driven so many
130 evils upon me. For all the best men who hold sway in the islands,
131 both in Dulichium and in Same and wooded Zacynthus,
132 and those who inhabit clear-seen Ithaca here all around me,
133 woo me against my will, and they are devouring the household.
134 Therefore I give no thought to strangers and suppliants coming,
135 nor even to heralds, who are workers in service of the people;
136 rather, in longing for dear Odysseus I waste away inwardly.
137 They are urgent for marriage, while I keep twisting out cunning.
138 First of all for me a god breathed this design in my spirit:
139 having set up in my halls a great loom, to weave there a garment,
140 fine-spun and very wide; and immediately I said to them:
141 “Young men, my suitors, since noble Odysseus has perished,
142 wait, though eager to marry me, until I finish this fabric,
143 lest the threads that I spin should perish and come to nothing utterly,
144 as a burial shroud for the hero Laertes, against the time when
145 deadly fate of destructive death shall at last overtake him;
146 lest some Achaean woman throughout the land should reproach me,
147 if a man of great wealth should lie there without any winding-sheet on.”
148 So I spoke, and their proud hearts in their breasts were persuaded.
149 Then by day I would weave at the great loom all through the daytime,
150 but by night I unraveled it, once I had set there the torches.
151 Thus for three years I escaped their notice and kept them persuaded;
152 but when the fourth year came and the seasons were passing upon us,
153 with the months wearing down and many long days completed,
154 then at last through my women, those bitches who cared for nothing,
155 they came upon me and caught me and chided me sharply with outcries.
156 So I completed it then, though unwillingly, under compulsion.
157 Now I can neither evade this marriage nor find some other
158 counsel; and my own parents are urgently pressing me to wed him,
159 while my son is distressed at the men who consume all his livelihood,
160 knowing it now; for already he is enough of a grown man
161 to care for his house, and to him Zeus grants honor and glory.
162 But even so, tell me your lineage, and from what place you come from;
163 for you are not sprung from the oak of old tale, nor from the rock.”
164 Making answer, resourceful Odysseus spoke to her, saying:
165 “Revered wife of Odysseus, son of Laertes,
166 surely you will not cease from asking and probing my kindred?
167 Yet I will tell you indeed; though truly you give me over
168 to sorrows more than I bear; for this is the lot of a mortal,
169 when from the land of his fathers a man has long been absent,
170 wandering over the cities of men and suffering anguish.
171 Even so, I will tell what you ask me and press me to answer.
172 There is a certain land, Crete, in the midst of the wine-dark ocean,
173 fair and fertile, encircled by waters; and men are within it
174 many, past all numbering, and there are ninety cities.
175 Tongue is mingled with tongue; and among them are Achaeans,
176 and Eteocretans great-hearted, with Cydonian peoples beside them,
177 Dorians in their three tribes, and splendid Pelasgians.
178 There among them is Knossos, a mighty city, where Minos
179 ruled for nine-year seasons, companion of great Zeus in counsel,
180 father of my own father, great-hearted Deucalion.
181 Deucalion begot me and lordly Idomeneus with me;
182 but he in the curved ships had gone on his way unto Ilion,
183 going with Atreus’ sons; but my glorious name is Aethon,
184 younger by birth; but he was the elder and better man by far.
185 There I saw Odysseus, and I gave him the gifts of a stranger;
186 for the might of the wind had driven him down into Crete,
187 as he was making for Troy, blown off his course past Malea.
188 He brought his ship to a halt at Amnisos, where is the cavern
189 of Eileithyia, in difficult harbors, and barely escaped the storm-blasts.
190 Straightway he went up into the town and inquired for Idomeneus;
191 for he said that he was his cherished and honored guest-friend.
192 But for him already had come the dawn, the tenth or eleventh,
193 since he had gone with the curved ships onward inside unto Ilion.
194 I took him then to my house and gave him generous welcome,
195 warmly cherishing him, since abundant stores were within it;
196 and for the rest, for the comrades who followed along beside him,
197 from the public stores I provided the glowing wine and the barley,
198 and cattle to sacrifice, so that their hearts might be satisfied.
199 There for the space of twelve days remained the noble Achaeans;
200 for the great North Wind penned them in, nor would let them on dry land
201 even stand; some hostile divinity had stirred it against them.
202 But on the thirteenth day the wind fell, and they launched on the seaway.”
203 So he spoke many falsehoods, and gave them the semblance of true things.
204 And as she listened, her tears kept flowing, her fair flesh was melting,
205 even as snow melts away on the peaks of the towering mountains,
206 snow that the East Wind has melted, when West Wind has heaped it upon them;
207 and as the snow is dissolving, the rivers are filled in their flowing:
208 so her beautiful cheeks were melting with the tears she was pouring,
209 weeping for her own husband who sat there close beside her. Odysseus
210 in his heart had pity to see his own wife in her weeping,
211 but his eyes in his head stood fixed as if they were horn or iron,
212 motionless there beneath his lids; and in cunning he hid back his tear-drops.
213 But when she had fully her fill of tear-laden lamentation,
214 then once more she answered him, speaking aloud these words to him:
215 “Now, stranger, I think that I shall make trial of you in this matter,
216 whether in truth in your halls you received and gave welcome to my husband,
217 there with his godlike companions, as now you declare that you did.
218 Tell me what sort of garments he wore about his body,
219 and what manner of man he was, and the comrades who went on beside him.”
220 Making answer, resourceful Odysseus spoke to her, saying:
221 “Lady, it is a hard thing, after so long an interval,
222 to tell it; for this is already the twentieth year fully
223 since from that place he departed and left my own country behind him.
224 Yet even so, I will tell you as my heart pictures him inwardly.
225 Noble Odysseus wore a mantle of wool, sea-purple,
226 doubled; and on it a brooch had been fashioned entirely of gold-work,
227 fitted with twin tubes for the pin; and before it a wonder was fashioned:
228 in its forepaws a hound held a dappled fawn, and was gripping it,
229 while it writhed; and all who beheld it kept marveling at it,
230 how, though made all of gold, the one gripped the fawn and was throttling it,
231 while the other, eager to flee, kept struggling hard with its forefeet.
232 I noticed too the tunic around his body, bright-shining,
233 like the skin that is over a dried-up onion in thinness;
234 so soft it was to the touch, and bright as the blaze of the sunlight.
235 Truly many women gazed upon him there and marveled.
236 And another thing I will tell you, and lay it away in your spirit:
237 I do not know if Odysseus wore these things when he left home,
238 or whether one of his comrades gave them aboard the swift vessel,
239 or perhaps some guest-friend gave them, since to many Odysseus
240 was beloved; for few among all the Achaeans could match him.
241 I too gave him a bronze sword there and a twofold mantle,
242 handsome and sea-dark purple, and a tunic with tassels,
243 and with all due honor I sent him aboard his good benched vessel.
244 And indeed a herald, a little the elder than he was,
245 went with him too; and I will tell you plainly what sort he was.
246 Rounded across the shoulders, dark of skin, and curly-headed,
247 Eurybates was his name; and Odysseus honored him greatly
248 over the rest of his comrades, because he knew his mind truly.
249 So he spoke, and in her he aroused still greater desire for weeping,
250 when she had recognized all the signs Odysseus described surely.
251 But when at last she had had her fill of the tearful lamenting,
252 then in reply she addressed him and spoke these winged words:
253 “Now then, stranger, though earlier you stirred my pity deeply,
254 in my halls from this time onward you shall be dear and respected.
255 For I myself gave him these garments, the very ones you mention,
256 folding them out from the chamber, and fastened the shining brooch-pin
257 for him to be a delight; but never again shall I greet him,
258 coming back to his home and his own dear fatherland's country.
259 By an evil fate in a hollow ship went great Odysseus
260 to look upon evil Ilios, not to be named among mortals.”
261 Speaking in answer, resourceful Odysseus addressed her and said this:
262 “Revered wife of Laertes' son, of noble Odysseus,
263 do not now any longer consume your fair skin with your grieving,
264 neither melt your heart away as you mourn for your husband. I blame you
265 not in the least; for any woman laments for her husband,
266 lawfully wed, with whom in love she has borne her children,
267 even if he be less than Odysseus, whom they liken to godheads.
268 But now cease from your weeping, and listen closely to my story;
269 for I will tell you the truth exactly and will not deceive you:
270 I have already heard of Odysseus and his homecoming,
271 nearby, in the rich-settled land of the Thesprotian people,
272 living; and he is bringing abundant and splendid possessions,
273 begging his way through the land. Yet his trusty companions
274 he lost, and his shapely ship, on the wine-dark sea water,
275 coming away from the island Thrinacia; for against him
276 Zeus and Helios were angered, because his companions killed cattle.
277 They all perished together in the loud-resounding sea-surge;
278 but as for him, on the keel of the ship the wave cast him shoreward,
279 onto the land of the Phaeacians, close akin to the immortals.
280 They in the depth of their hearts did him honor as though to a godhead,
281 and they gave him many possessions, and wished of themselves too
282 to convey him home unharmed. And Odysseus long since
283 would have been here, but this seemed more gainful to his own spirit:
284 to gather wealth while passing over the face of much country.
285 So beyond all mortal men in the matter of profits and prudence
286 Odysseus knows many ways; no other man could contend with him.
287 So to me spoke Pheidon, the king of the Thesprotian people;
288 and he swore this oath to me there, pouring libation at homeward:
289 that the ship had been hauled down and the comrades stood ready,
290 those who would send him away to his own dear fatherland's country.
291 But me he dispatched before him; for there came there a vessel
292 of Thesprotian men, sailing to wheat-rich Dulichium.
293 And he showed me the treasure, all that Odysseus had gathered;
294 it would indeed maintain yet another man to the tenth generation,
295 so many were the lordly possessions stored in his palace.
296 But he said that he had gone to Dodona, there to listen
297 for the design of Zeus, from the oak with its high crown of leafage,
298 how he might come again to his own dear fatherland's country,
299 either openly now, or in secret, after so long a time absent.
300 So then he is safe, and indeed he will very soon be arriving,
301 very near, no longer far from his friends and his fatherland's country;
302 yet even so, I will give you my solemn oath on the matter.
303 Let Zeus now know it first, highest and best of the immortals,
304 and the hearth of the blameless Odysseus, to which I am coming:
305 surely all these things will be brought to pass as I tell you.
306 Within this very circuit of time will Odysseus come hither,
307 as this month wanes away and the next one rises upon us.”
308 Once again thoughtful Penelope answered him, saying:
309 “Ah if only, stranger, this word might indeed be accomplished.
310 So would you swiftly know my friendship and many gifts from me,
311 so that whoever might meet you would count you among the blessed.
312 But in my heart I foresee it thus, and thus it shall happen:
313 neither will Odysseus come home, nor will you get an escort,
314 since there are no masters such as that in this household,
315 such as Odysseus was among men, if ever indeed he was one,
316 to receive honored strangers and send them on their journey.
317 But wash him now, handmaids, and prepare him a bedstead,
318 with bedding and cloaks and bright-glossed blankets upon it,
319 so that being warmly covered he may await Dawn's golden throne.
320 And at earliest morning bathe him and anoint him with olive oil,
321 so that indoors by Telemachus there he may mind his own supper,
322 seated within the great hall; and more grievous for any among them,
323 whoever afflicts him with soul-destroying taunting and malice:
324 here he will get nothing done hereafter, however fiercely enraged he.
325 For how, my guest, will you learn whether I surpass the other women
326 in good sense and in the thoughtful counsel alive in my spirit,
327 if in my halls you feast there unwashed and clad in foul raiment?
328 Men are but creatures that flourish a little while only.
329 One who is harsh in himself and who harbors harsh understanding,
330 on him all mortals call down pains yet to come while he liveth,
331 while he lives, and when he is dead all men make mock of him after.
332 But if a man is blameless himself and knows what is blameless,
333 then strangers abroad bear wide the fame of that man among all men,
334 carrying it over mankind, and many declare he is noble.”
335 Her resourceful Odysseus answered, saying in turn:
336 “Revered wife of Odysseus, son of Laertes, hear me:
337 truly to me cloaks now and shining blankets have grown hateful
338 from the time when I first left behind me the snowy hillsides of Crete,
339 setting forth in the long-oared ship on the course of my journey;
340 and I will lie down now as before through many unsleeping nighttimes.
341 For indeed many nights on an unlovely couch I have suffered,
342 lying awake and awaiting divine Dawn seated on her fair throne.
343 Nor at all does the washing of feet bring delight to my spirit,
344 neither shall any woman lay hold of a foot of my body,
345 none of the women who serve as attendants throughout your household,
346 unless there be some old woman, long-tried, who knoweth what is fitting,
347 one who has borne in her heart as many afflictions as I have;
348 such a woman I would not begrudge to lay hold of my two feet.”
349 Him in turn thoughtful Penelope answered, speaking:
350 “Dear guest, never yet has a man of far-wandering strangers,
351 one so wise in discernment, come on to my house as a loved one,
352 for with such clear understanding you speak every word that is prudent.
353 There is an old woman with me who has shrewdness stored in her spirit,
354 she who nursed that unhappy man well and cherished and reared him,
355 taking him in her hands when first his mother had borne him.
356 She shall wash your feet, though age has made her feeble and weakened.
357 But come now, arise, prudent Eurycleia, and hear me:
358 wash a man of your master’s own age; for perhaps now Odysseus
359 has by this time such feet, and such hands, as belong to this stranger;
360 for in misfortune mortals grow old very quickly indeed.”
361 So she spoke, and the old woman covered her face with both of her two hands,
362 and she shed warm tears, and uttered a mournful saying:
363 “Ah me, my child, I am helpless for you; in truth Zeus above all men
364 hated you, though you held in your breast a god-fearing spirit.
365 For no other mortal ever burned for Zeus who delighteth in thunder
366 so many rich thigh-pieces, or offered such splendid hecatombs,
367 as you gave unto him, praying that you might come to a season
368 of sleek old age, and might rear your glorious son to manhood.
369 But now from you alone has he utterly taken the day of returning.
370 So, I suppose, women in foreign houses mocked also at that other,
371 women of far-off strangers, whenever he reached some glorious dwelling,
372 just as all these hounds here are mocking at you, every one of them.
373 Therefore avoiding the insult and many disgraceful affrontings
374 you did not let them wash you; but me, and I am not unwilling,
375 the daughter of Icarius, prudent Penelope, has ordered.
376 Therefore your feet I shall wash, for Penelope’s sake and your own too,
377 and because for your sake my heart is stirred deep within me
378 by griefs. But come now, attend to the word that now I shall tell you:
379 many much-suffering strangers indeed have arrived here before us,
380 yet never so far do I say I have looked on one so resembling
381 Odysseus in body and voice and feet as you now resemble him.”
382 And resourceful Odysseus answered her, speaking in turn:
383 “Old woman, so say all those who have looked with their own eyes upon us,
384 both of us together, that we are exceedingly like one another,
385 even as you yourself in your careful thought have spoken and judged it.”
386 So he spoke, and the old woman took up a beautiful shining basin,
387 that all-shining vessel wherein she washed feet, and she poured in
388 much cold water, and after that added the hot for the washing.
389 Meanwhile Odysseus sat down close by the hearth and quickly thereafter
390 turned himself toward the dark; for straightway he thought in his spirit
391 she might feel and perceive the scar, and all would become manifest.
392 She came near and began to wash her lord, and at once she knew it,
393 knew the scar that a boar once gave him by striking with white tusk,
394 when he had gone to Parnassus to Autolycus and his sons,
395 to the noble father of his own mother, who far surpassed all mortals
396 both in thievery and in the oath; and a god himself gave these to him,
397 Hermes, since he would burn to him well-pleasing thigh-pieces of victims,
398 lambs and kids; and the god went gladly beside him and helped him.
399 Once Autolycus came to the fertile land and the people of Ithaca,
400 and he found there the son newly born of his own dear daughter.
401 So Eurycleia placed the child on the knees of his loved one,
402 when he had ceased from supper, and spoke a word and addressed him:
403 “Autolycus, you yourself must now find what name you will give him,
404 your own dear daughter’s child; for he has been prayed for by many.”
405 Her in return Autolycus answered, and spoke out, saying:
406 “Son-in-law dear and my daughter, then give him the name that I tell you.
407 Since I have come here burdened with wrath against many people,
408 both against men and against women throughout the grain-giving wide earth,
409 therefore let Odysseus be the name that he shall be given.
410 And for my part, whenever he comes to the vigor of manhood
411 and to Parnassus, the great house of his mother’s people,
412 there I will give him gifts and send him homeward rejoicing.”
413 For the sake of those things Odysseus came, to receive noble presents.
414 Both Autolycus himself and the sons of Autolycus round him
415 clasped his hands in welcome and greeted him with gentle sayings;
416 and Amphithea his grandmother, mother of his own mother,
417 embraced Odysseus and kissed his head and both eyes in their beauty.
418 And Autolycus called aloud to his sons in their glory,
419 bidding them furnish the feast; and they listened and heard his urging.
420 Straightway then they led in a bull, a male in his fifth year.
421 This one they flayed and dressed, and they carved up all of his body,
422 skillfully jointing the meat, they threaded the pieces on skewers,
423 roasted them well with care, and divided to each man his portion.
424 So then all through the day until the sun went down in the evening
425 they feasted, and no heart lacked anything of the equal banquet.
426 But when the sun went down and the darkness came on in the twilight,
427 then indeed they lay down and took for themselves sleep’s gift.
428 But when Dawn had appeared, the young one, rose-fingered Eos,
429 forth they went to the hunt, both the dogs and the men themselves also,
430 even the sons of Autolycus; and noble Odysseus
431 went there among them. They climbed the steep mountain all mantled
432 deep in forest, Parnassus, and quickly they reached windy foldings.
433 Then did the Sun strike fresh on the tilled fields of the mortals,
434 rising from out of the deep-swirling stream of Ocean,
435 and they, the beaters, came to a glen; and the hounds, out before them,
436 searching the tracks, went on; and behind them followed in order
437 all of the sons of Autolycus; and noble Odysseus
438 went with them close by the hounds, brandishing his long-shadowing spear.
439 There in a dense thicket a huge wild boar lay couched and hidden.
440 Into that place no force of the wet-blowing winds ever entered,
441 neither did shining Sun with his beams ever strike through upon it,
442 neither could rain pass through from one side straight on to the other,
443 so exceedingly dense it was, and leaves lay fallen there thickly.
444 Round the boar there came the clatter of feet of the hunters,
445 both of men and of dogs, as they pressed on; and out of the thicket
446 facing them rose he, his ridge-bristles lifting in terror,
447 fire in his eyes; and he stood close beside them. And foremost
448 rushed in Odysseus, lifting on high in his stout hand the long spear,
449 eager to wound him; but first the boar struck him in onset,
450 just above on the knee, and with his tusk’s swift slanting onrush,
451 tore away much flesh, but he did not reach to the bone of the hero.
452 Yet Odysseus struck him, hitting him square on the right shoulder,
453 and right through passed the point of the bright-shining spear in its fury.
454 Down in the dust he fell with a grunt, and the spirit flew from him.
455 Him the dear sons of Autolycus tended, busying round him,
456 and the wound of Odysseus, the noble and godlike fighter,
457 they bound up well with skill, and with charm-song stanched the dark bleeding,
458 and swiftly then they came again to the house of their own dear father.
459 Both Autolycus himself and the sons of Autolycus round him,
460 after they healed him well and bestowed on him splendid presents,
461 sent him off in all haste, rejoicing, back to his own land,
462 unto Ithaca. There his father and queenly mother
463 welcomed his homecoming gladly and asked him about every matter,
464 how he had gotten the scar; and he told them the whole tale clearly,
465 how while hunting the boar had struck him with white tusk in onset,
466 when he had gone to Parnassus with sons of Autolycus.
467 That scar the old woman, taking his leg in the palms of her two hands,
468 knew as she touched it and felt it, and let the foot fall from her holding.
469 Down in the basin the shin fell, and the bronze gave a clanging;
470 over it tipped to the other side, and the water poured groundward.
471 Joy and anguish together seized all the thoughts in her bosom;
472 tears filled both of her eyes, and her vigorous voice was arrested.
473 Touching Odysseus beneath the chin, she spoke out to him, saying:
474 “Surely you are Odysseus, dear child; and I did not know you
475 before I had touched and handled my master all over, completely.”
476 So she spoke and cast her eyes toward Penelope, wishing
477 to make it known to her that within was her own dear husband.
478 But she could not look straight back at her nor yet understand it;
479 for Athena had turned away the mind of Penelope from it.
480 Feeling toward her throat, Odysseus seized hold with his right hand,
481 With the other hand he drew her nearer and spoke out to her:
482 “Nurse, why are you willing to ruin me? You yourself reared me
483 there at your own breast; and now, after bearing much sorrow,
484 in the twentieth year I have come to the land of my fathers.
485 But since you have found me, and some god put it into your spirit,
486 keep still, lest any other man in the halls come to know it.
487 For thus I will tell you, and surely the thing will be finished:
488 if beneath my own hands a god should subdue the proud suitors,
489 not even you, though you are my nurse, will I leave unpunished
490 when I kill the women, the serving-slaves, here in my hall.”
491 And in reply spoke to him thoughtful Eurycleia:
492 “My child, out past the fence of your teeth what a word has escaped you!
493 You know what manner of strength in me stands firm and unyielding;
494 I will endure like a stone, like iron that never is broken.
495 But another thing I will tell you; now lay it away in your spirit:
496 if beneath your own hands a god should subdue the proud suitors,
497 then I will tell you the women in hall, every one of them,
498 which ones dishonor you, and which of them all are guiltless.”
499 Making reply, resourceful Odysseus answered her, saying:
500 “Nurse, why do you speak of them? There is no need for you to.
501 I myself will observe them well, and I will know every woman.
502 But keep this matter in silence, and leave the rest to the gods.”
503 So he spoke, and the old woman passed through the hall and departed,
504 going to bring the foot-water; for all of the first had been spilled out.
505 But when she had washed him and anointed him richly with olive,
506 back again Odysseus pulled the chair somewhat nearer the fire,
507 warming himself, and he covered the scar with his tattered garments.
508 First to speak among them was thoughtful Penelope:
509 “Stranger, I myself will ask you one little thing more only.
510 For soon will come the hour of delightful rest and of slumber
511 for whatever man sweet sleep may take, though burdened with sorrow.
512 But to me some god has allotted grief beyond all measure.
513 In the daytime indeed I find relief in weeping and wailing,
514 looking to my own tasks and the tasks of the maids in the household.
515 But whenever night comes on and sleep has taken all others,
516 then I lie in my bed, and around my heart in its throbbing
517 keen and crowded anxieties goadingly trouble me, mourning.
518 As when Pandareus’ daughter, the nightingale green in the thicket,
519 sings out beautifully when spring is newly beginning,
520 sitting among the trees, in their thick and crowded foliage,
521 and often varying pours out her far-resounding song-notes,
522 mourning her child, beloved Itylus, whom once with the bronze blade
523 she killed in her witlessness, child of lord Zethus the ruler:
524 so also my own heart is divided and driven both ways over,
525 whether I stay by my son and guard all things in their order,
526 my possessions, my serving-women, and my high-roofed great dwelling,
527 honoring the marriage-bed of my husband and public opinion,
528 or at last follow with one of the Achaeans, whoever is finest,
529 one who courts me within these halls and brings me boundless bride-gifts.
530 While my son still was small, a mere child and childish in thinking,
531 he would not let me be wed and leave the house of my husband.
532 But now, when he is grown great and has come to manhood’s measure,
533 now he begs me again to go back from the house and depart from it,
534 angered over the livelihood that the Achaeans consume up.
535 But come now, hear this dream of mine and interpret it for me.
536 I have geese in my house, twenty of them, and they feed upon wheat
537 out of the water, and I take delight in watching them daily.
538 Out from the mountain there came a great eagle with hooked beak,
539 and he snapped all their necks and killed them, and they lay tumbled,
540 huddled in heaps through the halls, while he rose into bright upper ether.
541 But I wept and wailed aloud, though indeed it was in a dream only;
542 and around me there gathered the fair-haired Achaean women,
543 while in pity I lamented that my geese had all been killed by the eagle.
544 But coming back, on the jutting beam of the roof he alighted,
545 and with the voice of a mortal he checked me and spoke out to me:
546 “Take courage, daughter of Icarius famous in far lands.
547 This is no dream, but a waking vision, a good one, accomplished.
548 The geese are the suitors, and I was before this the eagle,
549 but now I have come back again, and I am your own husband,
550 who upon all the suitors will bring an unsightly destruction.”
551 So he spoke, and the honey-sweet sleep released me and left me.
552 Looking about, I saw the geese there within the great hall-room,
553 pecking at wheat by the trough, where before they had always been feeding.”
554 Making reply, resourceful Odysseus answered her, saying:
555 “Lady, there is no way to interpret this dream by turning it
556 to some other meaning, since Odysseus himself has made plain to you
557 how it will be brought to fulfillment; and ruin appears for the suitors,
558 all of them utterly; not one will escape death and destruction.”
559 Once more in reply spoke to him thoughtful Penelope:
560 “Stranger, dreams indeed are baffling things, and their sayings are tangled;
561 and not all things that they show find fulfillment among mortals.
562 For there are two gates, men say, for the insubstantial dream-shapes;
563 one pair is fashioned of horn, and the other is fashioned of ivory.
564 Those dreams, to be sure, that come by the way of sawn ivory,
565 these are deceptive and cheat us, bearing a message unfulfilled;
566 but the ones that come forth through the gates of horn, smoothly polished,
567 these bring truth to pass, whenever some mortal beholds them.
568 But as for me, I do not suppose that my terrible dream came
569 out from that place; how welcome a thing it would be for my child and me.
570 Yet another thing I will tell you, and lay it away in your spirit:
571 now indeed there comes this dawn of evil name, which from the house
572 of Odysseus will sever me; for now I shall set down a contest,
573 those axes which he in the hall of his house would set up in order,
574 all twelve of them, set in a row like timbers that prop up a vessel;
575 then standing far off he would shoot an arrow clear through them.
576 Now before all the suitors I shall proclaim this trial:
577 whoever most easily strings the bow with the strength of his hands,
578 and drives an arrow straight through all the twelve axes together,
579 with him then I shall go, and forsake this house I am leaving,
580 my wedded home, very fair, and filled with the means of our living;
581 that house, I believe, I shall still remember even in dreaming.”
582 Her resourceful Odysseus answered and spoke in reply:
583 “Revered wife of Odysseus, son of Laertes in glory,
584 do not any longer delay this contest here in your dwelling;
585 for before they can do it, resourceful Odysseus will come here,
586 before these men, while handling this well-polished bow in their fingers,
587 string the cord on it tight and shoot the shaft clean through the iron.”
588 And in turn wise-minded Penelope answered and spoke to him:
589 “Stranger, if you were willing to sit here beside me in hall
590 and delight me, then sleep would never be shed on my eyelids.
591 But there is no means for human beings to stay without sleeping
592 always; for on each thing the immortals have set down a portion
593 for us mortals who live on the grain-giving face of the ploughland.
594 Therefore now I shall go up into my upper chamber
595 and lie down upon that bed which has been made a bed of mourning,
596 always befouled by my tears, from the hour when Odysseus
597 went away to behold accursed Ilios, never named rightly.
598 There I shall lie; and you must lie down somewhere in this dwelling,
599 either by spreading a bed on the floor, or having them make one.”
600 So having spoken, she went up to her silent upper chamber,
601 not alone, for other handmaids went there together beside her.
602 And when she had gone to the upper room with her serving women,
603 there she wept for Odysseus, her beloved husband, until
604 bright-eyed Athena cast sweet sleep down over her eyelids.
