# The Odyssey — Book 8

*The games; the song of Ares and Aphrodite; the wooden horse, and the weeping guest.*

1 When early Dawn had appeared with her beautiful rose-colored fingers,
2 up from his bed rose the sacred strength of the lord Alcinous;
3 Zeus-born Odysseus, sacker of cities, arose from his bedding.
4 Then the sacred strength of the lord Alcinous led him
5 there to the meeting place of Phaeacians, built by the vessels.
6 Coming together, they sat themselves down on the stones that were polished,
7 close to each other; but Pallas Athena went through the city,
8 taking the form of a herald who served wise lord Alcinous,
9 planning the homeward return of the great-hearted lord Odysseus.
10 Standing beside every man in the city, she spoke and addressed them:
11 “Come this way, leaders and counselors of the Phaeacian people,
12 into the meeting place, so you may hear of the wandering stranger
13 newly arrived at the house of the wise and renowned Alcinous,
14 after his sea-roads, a man who resembles the gods in his body.”
15 Thus having spoken, she stirred every man's strength and spirit to action.
16 Swiftly the meeting place and the seats became full with the people,
17 gathering there; many marveled at seeing the son of Laertes,
18 battle-wise lord Odysseus, for Pallas Athena had poured there
19 marvelous grace over both of his shoulders and over his head too,
20 making him taller to look at and broader as well in his body,
21 so he might be beloved by all of the people Phaeacian,
22 also be dreaded, revered, and accomplish the many athletic
23 trials in which the Phaeacians intended to test Odysseus.
24 Then, when they all had assembled and come to the meeting together,
25 lord Alcinous stood and addressed all the people before him:
26 “Hear me, Phaeacian leaders and counselors, while I am speaking
27 what in the heart in my breast has commanded me now to declare here.
28 This foreign man, and I know not who he may be, has arrived here,
29 whether from people who live in the east or the west, to my palace,
30 urgently begging a homeward escort and asking assurance.
31 Come then, as always before, let us hasten the stranger's returning.
32 No mortal man, no one at all who arrives at my household,
33 waits for a long time grieving because he is lacking an escort.
34 Come, let us haul down a black-hulled vessel to shine in the salt sea,
35 newly constructed, and let two-and-fifty young men be selected
36 all through the city, as many as ever have proven the finest.
37 Bind all the oars to the benches securely, and come to the dry land;
38 afterward make your way swiftly here to my house for a banquet.
39 I shall provide an abundance of food for you all in the palace.
40 These are the orders I give to the young men; but all of you others,
41 scepter-bearing kings, come into my beautiful palace,
42 so we may welcome the stranger inside; let no one reject it.
43 Call also godlike Demodocus here, the renowned singer;
44 more than to anyone else the divinity gave him a song-craft,
45 bringing delight however his heart may inspire him to singing.”
46 Thus having spoken, he led, and the scepter-bearers were following;
47 meanwhile, a herald departed to bring the renowned singer.
48 Then two-and-fifty young men, the selected crew of the city,
49 went, as the king had commanded, along to the beach of the barren
50 sea; and when they arrived at the ship and the edge of the water,
51 into the deep water first they launched the black-hulled vessel,
52 then in the black ship duly they stepped both the mast and the sails there,
53 fitting the oars to the thole-pins securely with straps made of leather,
54 all in appropriate order, and opening out the white sailcloth.
55 Out in the water they moored her securely, and afterward journeyed
56 into the great and beautiful house of the wise Alcinous.
57 All the verandas and courtyards and rooms of the palace were crowded,
58 filled with the gathering people, and many were young men and old men.
59 Twelve sheep there Alcinous slaughtered to serve them at banquet,
60 eight white-tusked hogs and a pair of the shambling cattle;
61 these they skinned and prepared, making ready the beautiful feasting.
62 Then the herald approached them and led the respected singer,
63 whom above others the Muse loved, and gave him both evil and goodness:
64 taking his eyesight away, she provided the sweetness of singing.
65 Then Pontonous set there for him a silver-studded
66 chair at the feast's middle place, against the great pillar reclining;
67 there on a peg he suspended the clear-voiced lyre above the
68 head of the singer, and showed how his hands could take hold of the lyre there.
69 Near him he placed a basket of food and a beautiful table,
70 also a cup full of wine he could drink when his heart should desire it.
71 All of their hands reached out to the excellent food spread before them.
72 Then, when desire for food and for drinking was put from their bodies,
73 Muse then inspired the singer to sing of the glories of heroes,
74 choosing a song whose fame at the time had ascended to heaven:
75 how great Odysseus quarreled with Achilles, the son of
76 Peleus, once in the rich feast held for the gods, and the two men
77 clashed in their terrible words, while lord of men Agamemnon
78 inwardly felt great joy that the Achaean best men were quarreling.
79 Thus lord Phoebus Apollo had told him in earlier prophecy,
80 there in the sacred Pytho, when he crossed the threshold of stonework,
81 asking the god; for the great first wave of disaster was rolling
82 over the Trojan and Danaan peoples by counsel of great Zeus.
83 These things the famous singer was singing; but lord Odysseus
84 took up the great purple cloak in the strength of his hands and he drew it
85 over his head, concealing the beautiful face he was bearing;
86 shame held him fast lest the people should see tears under his eyebrows.
87 Every time that the godlike singer would pause from the singing,
88 he would then wipe all the tears from his face and uncover his forehead,
89 lifting the two-handled cup and pouring the wine to immortals.
90 Yet when the singer began once again, and the best Phaeacians
91 urged him to sing, since they felt in their hearts great joy in the story,
92 then great Odysseus covered his head once again and lamented.
93 All of the others observed not a tear the great hero was shedding;
94 only Alcinous marked and observed what befell Odysseus,
95 sitting beside him and hearing his heavily burdened groaning.
96 Straightway he spoke to the oar-loving people of all Phaeacia:
97 “Hear me, Phaeacian leaders and counselors gathered around me.
98 Now we have filled our hearts with the feast we have equally shared here,
99 also the lyre that is always the rich banquet's faithful companion.
100 Come then, let us go outside and make trial of every
101 contest, so this foreign man may relate to his family later,
102 once he arrives at his home, by how much we surpass other people,
103 both in the boxing and wrestling and jumping and swiftness of running.”
104 Thus having spoken, he led, and the people departed behind him.
105 Then the herald hung up on its peg the clear-voiced lyre,
106 taking Demodocus' hand and conducting him out of the great hall,
107 leading him there on the selfsame road that the other Phaeacian
108 nobles had followed, intending to marvel at all the athletic games there.
109 There to the meeting place they went, while a crowd beyond counting
110 followed behind in their thousands, and many fine young men arose there.
111 Up came Acroneus, Ocyalus, Elatreus with him,
112 Nauteus and Prymneus and Anchialus and lord Eretmeus,
113 Ponteus, Proreus, Thoon, and young Anabesineus,
114 Amphialus, Polyneus’ son, whose own father was Tecton;
115 also Euryalus rose, like Ares destroyer of mortals,
116 child of Naubolus, best in the beauty and form of his body,
117 after the faultless Laodamas, out of the whole of Phaeacia.
118 Three sons born to the blameless Alcinous also arose there:
119 lord Laodamas, Halius, and the godlike Clytoneus.
120 First they began to make trial of each in the race of the runners.
121 Out from the turning place was the course stretched ahead, and together
122 rapidly all of them flew, and the dust of the plain rose around them.
123 Blameless Clytoneus proved by far to be best of the runners;
124 just as the width of a mule-team furrow extends over fallow,
125 so far he came to the crowd in advance, leaving all men behind him.
126 Next they began to make trial of painful and wearisome wrestling;
127 there Euryalus defeated the best of the men in the contest.
128 Lord Amphialus proved himself first of the men in the long jump;
129 far beyond others Elatreus threw with the discus;
130 boxing was won by the excellent son of Alcinous, Laodamas.
131 Then, when they all in their hearts had taken delight in the contests,
132 lord Laodamas, the son of Alcinous, spoke to the others:
133 “Come then, friends, let us ask if the stranger possesses experience
134 in any contest or learned it; in build he is not an unworthy
135 man, for his thighs and his calves are strong, and the arms that he carries,
136 massive the neck, and his strength is great; he is not without youthful
137 vigor, but manifold troubles have broken him down in affliction.
138 Nothing is worse than the sea for reducing the strength of a mortal,
139 shattering even the man who is formed with tremendous endurance.”
140 Then Euryalus spoke to Laodamas, giving an answer:
141 “Lord Laodamas, what you have said is appropriate measure.
142 Go there yourself and challenge the stranger, addressing him openly.”
143 Then, when the excellent son of Alcinous heard what was spoken,
144 standing in front of the people, he spoke to the lord Odysseus:
145 “Come then, fatherly stranger, and try the athletic contests,
146 if you have learned one; you likely have knowledge of contests and game-craft.
147 No greater glory exists for a man while he lives on the earth here
148 than what he gains as a work of his own strong feet and his own hands.
149 Come, make a trial, and scatter the cares from your heart in the contest.
150 Now your returning shall not be delayed for a long time, for already
151 launched is the ship and the men who shall travel beside you are ready.”
152 Then resourceful Odysseus spoke and addressed him in answer:
153 “Lord Laodamas, why do you urge me in mockery this way?
154 Cares in my heart are much closer to me than athletic contests.
155 Much I have suffered already and labored in manifold troubles;
156 now in your meeting place I am sitting and longing for homeward
157 travel, entreating the king and entreating all of the people.”
158 Then Euryalus spoke to him openly, mocking Odysseus:
159 “Stranger, indeed, you do not appear like a man of the contests,
160 such as are practiced in many events by the rest of mankind here,
161 rather a man who is sailing to places in ships of the many
162 benches, a ruler of sailors who all give attention to trading,
163 keeping the freight in his memory, watching the cargo and profits
164 seized in his greed; you do not resemble an athlete to me here.”
165 Looking beneath his brows, resourceful Odysseus answered:
166 “Stranger, you spoke without courtesy, like an irrational mortal.
167 So it is true that the gods do not grant to all people together
168 every grace, not beauty of form and intelligence and speaking.
169 One mortal man may be slighter than others in bodily presence,
170 yet some god wreathes words with a crown all around his appearance.
171 People look on with delight, while never confusedly he speaks,
172 sweetly and modestly, shining among all the people assembled;
173 then, when he walks through the city, they look at the man as a god there.
174 Yet there is another resembling immortals in bodily beauty,
175 but no grace like a crown has surrounded the words that he utters.
176 So it is now with your striking form; not even a god could
177 make you more handsome, but wholly devoid is the mind in your body.
178 You have aroused in my breast and my spirit a heart that is angry,
179 speaking without any measure. I am not unpracticed in contests,
180 whatever you may declare, and I reckon myself with the foremost,
181 while I could put my confidence yet in my youth and my own hands.
182 Now I am held in the grip of affliction and sorrow, for much I
183 suffered while cutting through warfare of men and the ruinous sea-waves.
184 Even so, after manifold evils, I shall try the contests,
185 since your insult has bitten my heart and your words have provoked me.”
186 Thus did he speak and sprang up still wearing his cloak, and he seized a
187 discus, the largest and thickest, of weight far greater than all those
188 used by Phaeacian men as they challenged each other in discus.
189 Whirling around, from his powerful hand he released it; the stone sang,
190 humming, and down to the earth then crouched all the men of Phaeacia,
191 oar-loving people renowned for their ships, at the onrush of rock-flight.
192 Past every mark it flew, lightly coursing away from his hand there.
193 Taking the form of a man, then Athena marked out the landing,
194 standing beside it and speaking a word to the lord Odysseus:
195 “Even a blind man, stranger, could pick out the sign of your casting,
196 feeling around with his hands, since it lies not among the great crowd of
197 others, but far in advance. Have confidence here in this contest:
198 no one among the Phaeacians shall reach it or cast far beyond it.”
199 Thus did she speak, and the much-enduring lordly Odysseus
200 felt great joy, for a loyal companion appeared on the field there.
201 Now with a lighter heart, he addressed the Phaeacian people:
202 “Young men, match that throw, and soon afterward I can assure you
203 I shall send forth another as far, and perhaps even farther.
204 All of the rest, every man whose heart and whose spirit command him,
205 come and make trial, since all of your people have moved me to anger,
206 boxing or wrestling or running; in nothing shall I make refusal,
207 any Phaeacian except for the host Laodamas.
208 He has befriended me here; who fights with the man who receives him?
209 Witless and wholly unworthy is any man who proposes
210 rivalry in the athletic games with his host in a strange land;
211 such a man cuts his own knees from beneath him and ruins his standing.
212 None of the others shall I now reject or regard with dishonor;
213 I shall become well acquainted and make trial face to face with him.
214 Not without skill am I found in the various contests of mankind.
215 Well do I know how to handle the bow that is smoothly constructed;
216 first of my company I could strike down an enemy warrior,
217 even when numerous comrades stood close at my side in the battle,
218 all of them shooting their arrows against an opposing battalion.
219 Only Philoctetes surpassed me in shooting the bow in
220 Troy's open country, whenever Achaeans contested in archery.
221 Yet I declare myself far beyond every other man living,
222 all of the mortals now living who eat their bread on the earth here.
223 Those who existed before I shall never presume to compete with,
224 neither Heracles nor with Eurytus, lord of Oechalia,
225 who with the bow made trial against the immortal divinities.
226 This is the reason great Eurytus died when his life was still youthful;
227 age never found him at home, for Apollo was angry against him,
228 striking him dead, since he dared to engage him in contest of archery.
229 I throw the spear farther than any man sends forth an arrow.
230 Only in running I fear one among the Phaeacian people
231 might pass me by, since greatly beyond any measure the sea-waves
232 broke me among them, and no provision was steady aboard ship;
233 therefore the joints of the limbs in my body are loosened and weary.”
234 Thus did he speak, and they all held themselves in a motionless silence.
235 Only Alcinous spoke and addressed the great lord Odysseus:
236 “Stranger, none of the words you have spoken among us are spoken
237 gracelessly; you are intending to show us the strength that attends you,
238 angered because this man on the field has arisen beside you,
239 taunting your prowess, which never a man would dishonor who knew well
240 how in his heart to express himself justly and speak in due measure.
241 Come now, listen to what I shall tell you, so later you may tell
242 some other hero, when you are sitting at home in your palace,
243 feasting beside both your wife and your children, and holding in memory
244 all of our prowess, the works that great Zeus has granted us also,
245 handed without interruption from days of the fathers before us.
246 We are not faultless as boxers at all, and neither as wrestlers;
247 fleetly we run on our feet, and with vessels we are the most skillful.
248 Always the feast and the lyre and the dances are dear to our people,
249 changes of clothing and warm bathing waters and beds for our pleasure.
250 Come then, all of Phaeacia's finest and most skillful dancers,
251 dance, so the stranger may tell his own people when homeward returning
252 how we surpass all other mankind in the skills we are given,
253 seamanship, running, and dancing together with singing and music.
254 Let someone hurry away and bring to Demodocus quickly
255 all of the clear-voiced lyre, which lies in the palace somewhere there.”
256 Thus spoke godlike Alcinous; then the herald arose there,
257 going to bring from the house of the king the hollow-formed lyre.
258 Nine chosen stewards of contests arose with the herald together,
259 officers set by the people to order events at assemblies;
260 smoothly they leveled a dancing place and made wider the fine ring.
261 Then the herald approached and carried the clear-voiced lyre there
262 for Demodocus, who walked to the middle, while standing around him
263 boys in the earliest flower of youth, all skilled in the dance steps,
264 struck with their feet at the radiant floor; and the lord Odysseus
265 watched the bright flicker and flash of their feet and marveled within him.
266 Striking the lyre, then the singer began a most beautiful song of
267 love between Ares and lovely-crowned goddess Aphrodite,
268 how they had first lain together in secret in lordly Hephaestus'
269 house, and how Ares gave manifold gifts and dishonored the marriage
270 bed of the lord Hephaestus. Yet there came one with a message:
271 Helios, Sun, had seen the two gods as together they lay there.
272 Then, when Hephaestus had heard the heart-stinging message delivered,
273 into his forge he journeyed, while deep in his heart he was brooding
274 evil; he placed the enormous anvil upon its foundation,
275 hammering chains that were never to break or be loosened, to hold them.
276 Then, when he fashioned the trap in his anger against god Ares,
277 into the chamber he went, where his own marriage bed had been placed there;
278 round every bedpost there he poured chains in a circle,
279 while many more from above, from the beam of the roof, he suspended,
280 fine as a spider's web, which none could discover by eyesight,
281 even the blessed gods, for they had been made with surpassing deception.
282 Then, when he poured the whole snare in a circle all over the bedstead,
283 he made a show of departing for Lemnos, the strongly built city,
284 which above all other places to him was the dearest of countries.
285 Ares who held the gold reins was not keeping watch without eyesight;
286 he saw the famous craftsman Hephaestus leaving the palace,
287 then made his way to the home of the widely renowned Hephaestus,
288 longing to lie in the love of the lovely-crowned Cytherea.
289 She had come newly from visiting father, the powerful son of
290 Cronos, and just had sat down when lord Ares entered the household,
291 taking her hand in his own and addressing the goddess directly:
292 “Come, my beloved, and lie in the bed, taking pleasure together;
293 lord Hephaestus is no longer at home, and I think he is now in
294 Lemnos, among the Sintians there with their wild-sounding language.”
295 Thus did he speak, and the thought of lying beside him was welcome.
296 Both of them went to the bed and lay down, while around them descended
297 all of the chains cleverly made by Hephaestus, the god of invention;
298 neither could move any limb or raise up the weight of a body.
299 Then they discovered the truth, that no avenue now could release them.
300 Close to them came the famous and powerfully armed Hephaestus,
301 turning around on his road before coming to Lemnos the island;
302 Helios watched for the god and told him the whole of the story.
303 Into his palace he went with his heart weighed down by the sorrow;
304 there in the gateway he stood, and terrible anger possessed him.
305 Crying aloud in a terrible voice, he called all the gods there:
306 “Father Zeus, and you other divinities blessed and deathless,
307 come here and look at a laughable thing that is wholly unbearable,
308 how Aphrodite, the daughter of Zeus, has always dishonored
309 me for my lameness and loves Ares, destroyer of people,
310 since he is handsome and straight in his legs, while I was created
311 crippled in body. Who else bears the blame? No one except my
312 parents together; I wish they had never at all brought me forth here.
313 Look at them now, as they lie with each other in love and are sleeping
314 there in my own marriage bed, while the sight makes my heart eaten by sorrow.
315 Yet I do not think they shall wish to remain there lying together,
316 even a moment, although they are deeply in love with each other;
317 soon they shall lose the desire to sleep. Yet the snare and the bindings
318 hold them until her own father pays back every one of the bride-gifts,
319 all I provided him there for this dog-eyed daughter of his house,
320 beautiful though she may be, for she holds not her heart within measure.”
321 Thus did he speak, and the gods came gathering into the bronze-floored
322 palace; Poseidon arrived, great god who encircles the earth-land;
323 Hermes the runner arrived, and the far-working lordly Apollo.
324 Yet all the goddesses stayed in their homes from a sense of the shame there.
325 Gods who provide all blessings were standing together in doorways;
326 then in the blessed gods rose laughter that never could be quenched,
327 seeing the clever device of Hephaestus, the god of invention.
328 Then one god would glance at his neighbor and speak in this manner:
329 “Wrongdoing does not prosper; the slow one captures the swift one.
330 See how Hephaestus, although he is slow, has captured god Ares,
331 fleetest of all of the gods who inhabit the heights of Olympus,
332 lame though he is, by his craft. Now the fine for adultery waits him.”
333 Thus did the gods speak words in this manner between one another.
334 Then lord Apollo, the son of great Zeus, spoke out to lord Hermes:
335 “Hermes, son of great Zeus, giving good things and guiding the traveler,
336 would you be willing, restrained under chains that were heavy and mighty,
337 there in the bed to lie close beside golden Aphrodite?”
338 Then the guide and the slayer of Argus replied to Apollo:
339 “If that were only to happen, far-shooting lordly Apollo!
340 Let even thrice the innumerable number of bindings
341 hold me, and all of you gods look on, with the goddesses also;
342 still would I gladly lie there beside golden Aphrodite.”
343 Thus did he speak, and the deathless divinities broke into laughter.
344 Only Poseidon laughed not, but continually pleaded
345 there with renowned Hephaestus the craftsman to set Ares free from
346 bondage; and raising his voice he spoke to Hephaestus in winged words:
347 “Release him, and I promise you this before all the immortals:
348 Ares shall pay all the fine you demand that is fitting and lawful.”
349 Then the famous and powerfully armed Hephaestus answered:
350 “Do not command me, Poseidon, great god who encircles the earth-land.
351 Worthless indeed is the pledge that is taken in trust for a worthless
352 man. How could I bind you fast in the sight of the gods everlasting
353 if Ares casts off both his binding and debt and escapes me?”
354 Then the great Earth-shaker lord Poseidon replied to Hephaestus:
355 “Even if Ares should cast off his debt and escape from the binding,
356 I shall myself pay all that he owes, and the fine shall be given.”
357 Then the famous and powerfully armed Hephaestus answered:
358 “It is not possible or right that I now should refuse you.”
359 Thus having spoken, the strength of Hephaestus released all the binding.
360 Then, when the two were set free from the bonds that were mighty around them,
361 straightway they sprang to their feet; Ares went off to the Thracians,
362 while Aphrodite who loves laughter went to the island of Cyprus,
363 Paphos, where she possesses a precinct and altar made fragrant.
364 There all the Graces bathed her and rubbed her with oil everlasting,
365 such as shines over the gods who continue in life without ending;
366 then they arrayed her in beautiful clothes, a great wonder to look on.
367 These things the famous singer was singing; and lord Odysseus
368 felt great delight in his heart as he heard, with the other Phaeacians,
369 people who loved the long oar and were famous for beautiful vessels.
370 Then Alcinous told Laodamas and Halius only
371 now to perform in the dance, since no one could rival their dancing.
372 Then in their hands they took a beautiful ball that was colored
373 purple, the work of the skilled craftsman Polybus, made for the young men.
374 One, having bent himself backward, would throw it toward shadowy cloud-land;
375 then the other would leap from the ground and would easily catch it,
376 light as he hung in the air, before his feet came again to the earth-land.
377 Then, when they tried their skill with the ball going straight up above them,
378 both of them danced on the grain-giving earth as they cast it between them,
379 rapidly throwing it backward and forward, while all the other
380 young men struck out the time at the ring, and an uproar arose there.
381 Then great Odysseus spoke to the glorious lord Alcinous:
382 “Lord Alcinous, most renowned among all of the people,
383 you made a boast that your dancers were best; it has come to completion.
384 Awe and amazement possess me at heart as I look at their dancing.”
385 Thus did he speak, and the sacred strength of the lord Alcinous
386 felt great joy; straightway he spoke to the oar-loving whole of the people:
387 “Hear me, Phaeacian leaders and counselors gathered around me.
388 This foreign man now seems to me to possess understanding.
389 Come then, as fitting, provide him a gift in our guest-friendship.
390 Twelve renowned kings hold their command in the country around us,
391 serving as leaders, and I am myself to be counted the thirteenth.
392 Let every one bring a freshly washed mantle and tunic before him,
393 also a talent in weight of the gold that is precious and costly;
394 let us collect them together at once, so the stranger may take them
395 into his hands and return to the feast with a heart full of gladness.
396 Let Euryalus make restitution to him, using language
397 also a gift, for the word that he spoke was not one of due measure.”
398 Thus did he speak, and they all gave approval and urged it together;
399 each of them sent off a herald to carry a gift from his household.
400 Then Euryalus spoke to Alcinous, giving an answer:
401 “Lord Alcinous, most renowned among all of the people,
402 I shall make full restitution to this foreign man, as you order.
403 I shall present him this sword, made wholly of bronze, with a handle
404 fashioned of silver upon it, and round it a newly cut ivory
405 scabbard; the gift shall be worth a great deal to him later.”
406 Thus having spoken, the silver-studded sword he set in the hands of
407 lord Odysseus, addressing the stranger in winged words:
408 “Hail, fatherly stranger. If some harsh word was unwisely
409 blurted, may storm winds catch it and carry it far in the distance.
410 May all the gods let you see your wife and return to your homeland,
411 since you have suffered long, far away from the people who love you.”
412 Then resourceful Odysseus spoke and addressed him in answer:
413 “Hail to you warmly, my friend, and may gods give you happiness always.
414 May you hereafter never experience longing to have this
415 sword you have given to me, after making amends in your language.”
416 Thus did he speak, and he slung the silver-studded sword round his shoulders.
417 Then the sun entered the west, and the famous gifts lay before him.
418 Heralds of lordship carried the gifts to the house of Alcinous;
419 there all the sons of the blameless Alcinous met them and took them,
420 placing the beautiful gifts with the reverend queenly mother.
421 Then the sacred strength of the lord Alcinous led them
422 into the palace, and there they sat on the high seats in order.
423 Then the strength of Alcinous spoke to the queenly Arete:
424 “Wife, bring a beautiful chest, now the best one you have in the palace,
425 laying yourself a freshly washed mantle and tunic within it.
426 Heat a great cauldron of bronze on the fire, so the water is warmed there,
427 so that the stranger, when bathed and when seeing collected in order
428 all of the gifts which the blameless Phaeacian nobles have brought him,
429 may in the feast take delight and in hearing the song of the singer.
430 I shall present him the beautiful cup I possess in my household,
431 fashioned of gold, so he may remember me all of his lifetime,
432 pouring to Zeus and the rest of the gods in his own great palace.”
433 Thus did he speak; then Arete commanded the serving women
434 swiftly to set on the fire a large tripod cauldron for bathing.
435 They set the great bathing cauldron directly on top of the bright fire,
436 pouring the water within, and they fed burning wood underneath it.
437 Fire wrapped all round the belly of bronze, and the water was heated.
438 Meanwhile, out of her chamber Arete brought forth a most lovely
439 chest for the stranger, and laid in it all of the beautiful presents,
440 clothing and gold the Phaeacian people had given Odysseus;
441 also she laid in a mantle and tunic of beautiful weaving.
442 Raising her voice, she spoke to the lord Odysseus in winged words:
443 “Look to the lid for yourself now, and fasten a knot on it quickly,
444 lest some man rob you of treasure when later you make the long voyage,
445 sleeping your sweet sleep aboard while the black-hulled ship carries onward.”
446 Then, when the much-enduring and lordly Odysseus heard her,
447 straightway he fitted the lid to the chest and securely he tied it,
448 using the intricate knot that the queenly Circe had taught him.
449 Straightway the housekeeper called him to enter the bath for his washing;
450 joyfully then his heart saw the warm bathing water before him,
451 since such attendance had not been provided to him for a long time,
452 not since he left the home of Calypso, the beautiful-haired one;
453 while in her home he had always been tended the same as a god there.
454 Then, when the serving women had bathed and anointed his body,
455 casting around him a beautiful cloak and a tunic for clothing,
456 out of the bath he came and went to the men at the wine feast.
457 Nausicaa, who received from the gods her surpassing beauty,
458 stood by the pillar supporting the roof that was strongly constructed;
459 there she beheld great Odysseus and marveled as she looked upon him.
460 Raising her voice, she addressed great Odysseus in winged language:
461 “Farewell, stranger; and someday, when you are home in your country,
462 hold me in memory, since first to me you owe the price of your lifetime.”
463 Then resourceful Odysseus spoke and addressed her in answer:
464 “Lord Alcinous' daughter Nausicaa, hear what I tell you:
465 only may Zeus so grant it, loud-thundering husband of Hera,
466 that I return to my home and behold the glad day of my homecoming;
467 then I shall pray to you even there as I pray to a goddess,
468 always through all of my days, for you saved my life, gentle maiden.”
469 Thus did he speak, and he sat by the side of the king Alcinous.
470 Already portions of meat were served and the wine bowl was mixing;
471 then the herald approached them and led the respected singer,
472 lord Demodocus, honored among all the people, and sat him
473 there in the midst of the feasters, against the great pillar reclining.
474 Then resourceful Odysseus called to the herald beside him,
475 cutting a piece from the chine of a white-tusked boar, with a larger
476 part still remaining, while rich fat lay in abundance around it:
477 “Herald, take this meat now and place it before Demodocus,
478 so he may eat, and I shall show him respect though I carry my sorrow.
479 Singers receive from all people throughout the expanse of the earth-land
480 honor and reverence as their portion, because the immortal
481 Muse has instructed their pathways and loves every tribe of the singers.”
482 Thus did he speak, and the herald carried and placed it within the
483 hands of the hero Demodocus, who received it with joy in his spirit.
484 All of their hands reached out to the excellent food spread before them.
485 Then, when desire for food and for drinking was put from their bodies,
486 then resourceful Odysseus spoke to Demodocus openly:
487 “Lord Demodocus, I give you praise beyond all other mortals.
488 Either the Muse, child of Zeus, gave you teaching, or lordly Apollo,
489 since you correctly in order sing the Achaeans' destruction,
490 all they accomplished and suffered, the manifold toil of Achaeans,
491 just as if you were there for yourself or had heard from a witness.
492 Come now, change your song's pathway and sing how the wooden horse was
493 built by Epeius together with Pallas Athena, a cunning
494 trap great Odysseus took to the heights of the fortress of Trojans,
495 filled with the warriors who sacked Ilium's city around them.
496 If you recount me this story as well and in proper proportion,
497 then I shall speak out at once and relate to the whole of humanity
498 how some divinity favored you, giving you godlike singing.”
499 Thus did he speak, and the singer began, by a god stirred to singing,
500 starting the song where the Argives embarked on the well-benched vessels,
501 sailing away when they cast burning fire on all of the war camp,
502 while other men with renowned Odysseus already were sitting
503 there in the Trojans' place of assembly, concealed in the horse-form,
504 since the Trojans themselves pulled the horse to the height of the fortress.
505 There it remained, while the Trojans talked endlessly, never deciding,
506 seated around it, and three different counsels were pleasing among them:
507 either to split the hollowed wood with the merciless bronze blade,
508 or to drag it to heights and cast it below from the cliff rocks,
509 or let it stand as a mighty gift to appease the immortals.
510 This was the way the affair would be ended at last by their portion;
511 Fate had appointed them all to be ruined whenever the city
512 took in the great horse made of wood, wherein all the Argives' best warriors
513 waited, conveying the doom and the death of the Trojans among them.
514 Then he sang how the sons of Achaeans had pillaged the city,
515 pouring out of the horse and abandoning hollow concealment.
516 Then he sang how they ravaged the high city's streets, each in his pathway,
517 how great Odysseus went like murderous Ares directly
518 into the house of Deiphobus, godlike Menelaus beside him;
519 there, he sang, Odysseus dared the most terrible combat,
520 conquering finally through great-hearted Pallas Athena.
521 These things the famous singer was singing; but lord Odysseus
522 melted, and out from beneath both eyelids the tears wet his cheekbones.
523 Just as a woman will weep when she falls on her well-beloved husband,
524 one who has fallen in front of his city and all of his people,
525 striving to ward the pitiless day from his town and his children;
526 seeing him dying and gasping the last of his breath, she around him
527 pours out her body and wails in a high clear voice, while behind her
528 spearmen strike at her back and her shoulders with spears, and they lead her
529 into captivity, there to endure all the labor and sorrow;
530 wasted away are her cheeks by the grief that arouses most pity.
531 So were the pitiful tears great Odysseus shed from his eyebrows.
532 All of the others observed not a tear the great hero was shedding;
533 only Alcinous marked and observed what befell Odysseus,
534 sitting beside him and hearing his heavily burdened groaning.
535 Straightway he spoke to the oar-loving people of all Phaeacia:
536 “Hear me, Phaeacian leaders and counselors gathered around me;
537 let Demodocus now hold back from the clear-voiced lyre he is playing,
538 since what he sings does not bring equal joy to all of the people.
539 Ever since we began eating supper, and since the divine
540 singer arose to perform, our stranger has never yet halted
541 his lamentation and grief; some mighty sorrow surrounds him.
542 Come then, let our singer now stop, so that we may take equal
543 pleasure together, both foreign guest and the people who host him.
544 Thus it is better by far, since all things are made for the guest we
545 honor, his homeward escort and gifts that in love we have given.
546 Just like a brother, the foreign stranger and suppliant must be
547 dear to the man whose intelligence has even a little attainment.
548 Do not conceal any longer beneath well-considered evasions
549 anything I ask you, for openly speaking is nobler.
550 Tell us the name that your father and mother have used there to call you,
551 also the people who live in the town and in places around it.
552 No mortal man in humanity ever is wholly without name,
553 neither the noble nor base, once he has been born among people;
554 parents give names to all children alike at the time they have borne them.
555 Tell me the land and the people you claim and the city you dwell in,
556 so that our vessels may carry you there with their thought for a pilot.
557 Ships of Phaeacians carry no helmsmen among them and never
558 carry the steering oars that the vessels of other men carry;
559 ships themselves understand every thought and the mind of the people,
560 knowing the cities of every humanity, knowing the rich fields;
561 swiftest of all things they cross every gulf of the sea and the salt waves,
562 covered in mist and in cloud, and they never have any fear of
563 coming to harm or enduring the ruin of wreck on their pathways.
564 Yet I once heard my father Nausithous speak in this manner:
565 he would declare that Poseidon regarded our people with anger,
566 since we give every man safe escort without any distinction;
567 therefore he said that a beautiful ship of Phaeacian people,
568 coming to home through the mist-covered sea from a voyage of escort,
569 would be destroyed by the god, and a mountain would cover the city.
570 Thus our elder had spoken; the god may bring it to fullness,
571 or leave the matter undone, in whatever way pleases his spirit.
572 Come now, tell me these things, and relate every matter with truth here:
573 where have you wandered and into what countries of mankind have you entered?
574 Tell of the people and cities where excellent houses are standing,
575 both of the violent, savage, and those who are strangers to justice,
576 also the friendly to strangers, with god-fearing hearts in their bodies.
577 Tell me the cause why you weep and lament in the heart you are bearing,
578 hearing the fate of the Argive Danaans and city of Ilium.
579 Gods made that destiny happen and spun the destruction for mortals,
580 so that among generations to come there might always be singing.
581 Did some relative dear to you die at the walls of the Trojans,
582 excellent man, some father-in-law or the husband of daughter,
583 those who are closest to us after those of our blood and our lineage?
584 Or did a companion die there, a man who was dear to your own heart,
585 excellent man, since never is less than the love of a brother
586 love for a comrade who carries clear thought and a true understanding?”
