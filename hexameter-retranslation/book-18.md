# The Odyssey — Book 18

*The fight with Irus; the warning to Amphinomus; Penelope shines before the suitors.*

1 There came up a common beggar, who begged through the city,
2 ranging through Ithaca town, and his greedy gut marked him,
3 ravenous always to eat and to drink without ceasing;
4 yet there was no strength in him, no force, though huge to look on.
5 Arnaeus was the name that his noble mother had given
6 him from his birth; but all the young men called him Irus,
7 since he would carry a message whenever one told him.
8 He came and began to drive Odysseus out of his own house,
9 and reviling him there he addressed him in winged words:
10 “Move from the doorway, old man, lest soon by the foot you be dragged off.
11 Do you not see that they all are winking and signaling to me,
12 telling me I should drag you? Yet even so I am ashamed to.
13 Up then, before our dispute and our hands come to fighting.”
14 Glaring darkly from under his brows, wise Odysseus answered:
15 “Strange man, I do you no harm at all, nor say anything evil,
16 nor do I grudge your receiving, though much may be given you.
17 This threshold here will hold us both, and there is no reason
18 you should envy another man’s share; you seem but a wanderer
19 just as I am, and the gods are the givers of fortune.
20 Yet do not challenge me overmuch with your hands, lest you rouse me;
21 old though I am, I might smear your chest and your lips over
22 all with blood; and quiet for me would be all the greater
23 on the morrow, for I do not think you would ever return
24 ever again to the hall of Laertes’ son Odysseus.”
25 Hot with his anger the vagabond Irus spoke out in answer:
26 “Oh for shame, how the glutton runs on with his rolling chatter,
27 like some old crone by her oven; I could devise him a harsh fate,
28 striking with both of my hands, and send all his teeth flying earthward,
29 out of his jaws, like the teeth of a sow that ravages cropland.
30 Gird yourself now, so all of these men may know us in battle.
31 How could you ever fight with a man who is younger than you?”
32 So the two of them there before the towering doors of the courtyard
33 stood on the polished threshold and faced one another in fury.
34 But the holy strength of Antinous noticed the two men,
35 and he laughed out sweetly and spoke among all the suitors:
36 “Friends, no such thing as this has ever befallen before us,
37 such delight as the god has brought to this house for our pleasure.
38 Here are the stranger and Irus disputing together
39 to fight with their hands; come, let us bring them together quickly.”
40 So he spoke, and all of them sprang to their feet with laughter,
41 and they gathered around the two beggars dressed in vile rags.
42 In their midst Antinous spoke, the son of Eupeithes:
43 “Hear me, proud suitors, so I may say what is in my thinking.
44 There at the fire lie paunches of goats, which for supper
45 we set down after filling with fat and with dark blood.
46 Whichever of these two men wins and proves the better fighter,
47 let him arise and choose for himself whichever he wishes.
48 Ever thereafter he shall dine in our company with feasting,
49 nor will we let any other beggar come in here asking.”
50 So spoke Antinous, and his saying was pleasing to all of them.
51 Craftily scheming, resourceful Odysseus spoke out among them:
52 “Friends, there is no way for an older man to fight with a younger,
53 one who is crushed down by suffering; yet this accursed belly
54 urges me on, worker of evil, to yield to his blows and be mastered.
55 Come then, all of you swear me a powerful oath I may trust in,
56 that no man, favoring Irus, will strike me a foul stroke
57 with his heavy hand and by force overpower me for his sake.”
58 So he spoke, and all of them swore as he had commanded.
59 But when they had sworn the oath and brought it wholly to completion,
60 then in his turn the holy strength of Telemachus answered:
61 “Stranger, if your heart prompts you and your proud spirit drives you
62 to beat this fellow off, then fear no one else of the Achaeans.
63 Whoever strikes you will have many to fight against after.
64 I am your host, and the kings give praise and approval,
65 Antinous and Eurymachus, men both sound in understanding.”
66 So he spoke, and all of them gave assent; then Odysseus
67 girded his rags at the loins, his two thighs revealing,
68 shapely and great, and plain to be seen were his broad shoulders,
69 with his chest and his sturdy arms; and Athena stood near him
70 and made the limbs more massive for the shepherd of the people.
71 All of the suitors were struck with amazement beyond measure,
72 and so would a man speak, glancing across at another beside him:
73 “Soon Irus will have drawn on himself a ruin past bearing,
74 such is the thigh that the old man shows from beneath his tatters.”
75 So they spoke, and for Irus his heart was stirred into anguish.
76 Yet even so the attendants, by force, came up and girded him,
77 terrified as he was; and the flesh on his limbs shook about him.
78 But Antinous rebuked him, and spoke and addressed him by name:
79 “Now may you neither exist, you loud ox, nor ever have been born,
80 if you indeed are trembling and fear this man so dreadfully,
81 an old man, worn down by the wretched affliction upon him.
82 But I will tell you this, and indeed it shall be accomplished:
83 if this man defeats you and proves himself stronger than you are,
84 I will send you away to the mainland, set in a black ship,
85 to King Echetus, the bane and destroyer of all mortal people,
86 who will shear off your nose and your ears with the pitiless war-bronze,
87 and tear out your genitals, and give them raw for the dogs to divide.”
88 So he spoke, and the trembling seized on his limbs still more strongly.
89 Into the midst they led him, and both of them lifted their hands up.
90 Then much-enduring noble Odysseus pondered deeply,
91 whether to strike him so that his life would leave him there as he fell down,
92 or else to deal him a lighter blow and stretch him there on the ground out.
93 As he considered, this course appeared to him more advantageous:
94 to strike him lightly, so the Achaeans would not perceive him.
95 Then when they had both raised guard, Irus struck at the right shoulder,
96 but he hit his neck beneath the ear, and the bones within it
97 he crushed inward; and instantly crimson blood came into his mouth,
98 and he fell in the dust with a bleat, and he knocked his teeth together,
99 kicking the ground with his feet; and the proud and lordly suitors
100 lifted their hands and died of laughter; but then great Odysseus
101 dragged him, clutching his foot, out through the forecourt doorway,
102 until he had come to the courtyard and the roofed colonnade's gateways;
103 there he sat him down propped up by the fence of the courtyard,
104 and placed in his hand a staff, and speaking addressed him in winged words:
105 “Sit there now, and keep the pigs and the dogs from the doorway,
106 and do not you be the master of strangers and beggars any longer,
107 vile as you are, lest some greater evil and worse one befall you.”
108 So he spoke, and cast round his shoulders the ugly beggar's satchel,
109 thickly patched and worn, and a twisted cord was its shoulder-strap.
110 Back to the threshold he went and sat himself down; they within doors
111 went back in, laughing with pleasure, and greeted him then with their speeches:
112 “May Zeus grant to you, stranger, and all the other immortals,
113 whatever you most desire, whatever is dearest unto your spirit,
114 since you have made that insatiable rover stop begging
115 through all the land; for soon we shall send him off to the mainland,
116 to King Echetus, the bane and destroyer of all mortal people.”
117 So they spoke, and noble Odysseus rejoiced in the omen of it.
118 And Antinous set a great goat's paunch down there beside him,
119 full of fat and of blood; and then Amphinomus lifted
120 two loaves out of the basket and set them there down beside him,
121 and pledged him in a golden cup, and speaking addressed him:
122 “Health to you, father and stranger; may wealth be yours hereafter,
123 though now for the present you are held fast in many afflictions.”
124 Making reply, resourceful Odysseus spoke and addressed him:
125 “Amphinomus, indeed you seem to me to be very discerning;
126 so was the father you spring from, for I have heard his good glory,
127 Nisus of Dulichium, that he was noble and wealthy;
128 they say you were born from him, and you look like a thoughtful man too.
129 Therefore I will tell you this; take heed and listen unto me.
130 Nothing more helpless than man does the earth ever foster,
131 of all things whatsoever that breathe and move on the earth's surface.
132 For he says that never hereafter will evil come on him,
133 while the gods grant him excellence and vigor in his knees still;
134 but whenever at last the blessed gods bring grievous things on him,
135 even these he endures, unwilling, with a patient spirit.
136 For such is the mind of the men who dwell on the face of the earth,
137 as is the day that the father of gods and of men brings upon them.
138 For I too once expected to be prosperous among men,
139 but I did many reckless deeds, yielding to force and to vigor,
140 trusting in my own father and trusting in my own brothers.
141 Therefore let not any man ever at all be lawless,
142 but in silence let him accept whatever gifts the gods grant him.
143 Such are the reckless designs that I see these suitors contriving,
144 wasting the substance away and dishonoring the lawful wife
145 of a man who, I say, will not be far from his friends and his fatherland
146 much longer, but is very near. Yet may some god lead you homeward,
147 and may you not encounter that man when he comes to his household,
148 whenever he returns again to the land of his own dear fathers.
149 For I do not think that without blood they will part from each other,
150 that man and the suitors, when once he comes under his roof-tree.”
151 So he spoke, and drank of the honey-sweet wine after pouring libation,
152 and placed the cup once more in the hands of the marshal of people.
153 But he went through the hall with his heart in sorrow and trouble,
154 nodding his head; for his spirit indeed was foreseeing destruction.
155 Yet not even so did he flee from fate; for Athena bound him
156 there to be overpowered by the hands and spear of Telemachus.
157 Back again he went and sat on the chair whence he had risen.
158 Into the mind of her put the gray-eyed goddess Athena,
159 into the mind of Icarius' daughter, wise Penelope,
160 to appear before the suitors, so she might most fully expand
161 so she might swell the proud hearts of the suitors, and be held in honor
162 more in the eyes of her husband and son than she was aforetime.
163 Laughing an empty laugh, she spoke out a word and she named her:
164 “Eurynome, never before did my heart feel such a longing,
165 to appear to the suitors, hateful though they are to me wholly;
166 and I would speak a word to my son, for that would be better:
167 he must not always consort with the arrogant-hearted suitors,
168 men who speak him fair to his face, but behind him imagine evil.”
169 Once again Eurynome the housekeeper answered and told her:
170 “Yes indeed, dear child, all this that you have said is well spoken.
171 Go now, and speak a word to your son, and let nothing be hidden,
172 when you have washed your skin and anointed the cheeks of your face first.
173 Do not go with your countenance thus befouled all over
174 with tears, since it is worse to go on in unceasing grieving forever.
175 For now indeed your son is of such an age as you chiefly
176 prayed to the deathless gods that you might behold him bearded.”
177 In her turn resourceful Penelope answered her, saying:
178 “Eurynome, do not urge me to this, though kindly you mean it,
179 to wash my skin and to anoint myself with the oil of gladness.
180 For the gods who possess Olympus have ruined the beauty
181 that once was mine, from the day when he sailed in the hollow ships from me.
182 But bid Autonoe and Hippodameia to join me
183 and attend me, so they may stand beside me here in the chamber;
184 for I will not go among men all alone; I am ashamed to.”
185 So she spoke, and the old woman went through the hall of the manor,
186 bearing the word to the women and urging them all to come to her.
187 Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena thought upon something further:
188 over the daughter of Icarius she poured a sweet slumber;
189 and she slept, reclining there, and all her limbs were loosened
190 there on the couch; and meanwhile the bright among goddesses gave her
191 deathless gifts, so the Achaeans might gaze on her there in wonder.
192 First with ambrosial unguent she cleansed her beautiful countenance,
193 of just such a kind as well-garlanded Cythereia
194 anoints herself with whenever she goes to the Graces’ desireful dancing.
195 And she made her seem taller and fuller to look upon also,
196 and she made her more white than ivory newly sawn and polished.
197 So having wrought all this, the bright among goddesses departed.
198 Out of the hall came the white-armed handmaids drawing near her,
199 making a stir as they came; and sweet sleep released her then also.
200 And with her hands she wiped her cheeks, and speaking she said to them:
201 “Surely a very soft trance enfolded me round in my sorrow.
202 If only holy Artemis would grant me a death just as gentle
203 instantly now, so no longer lamenting down in my spirit
204 I might wear out my life while yearning after my dear husband,
205 every virtue of his, since he was preeminent among Achaeans.”
206 So having spoken, she came down from her glimmering chamber,
207 not all alone; with her there followed also two of her handmaids.
208 But when she came to the suitors, that radiant queen among women,
209 she stood beside the pillar that held the strongly built roofing,
210 lifting the shining veil and holding it there by her cheeks with her hands;
211 and on either side a faithful handmaid stood beside her.
212 There on the spot their knees were unstrung, and their hearts were enchanted
213 with desire, and each one prayed that he might lie down by her bedside.
214 But she addressed Telemachus, her own beloved son, saying:
215 “Telemachus, your mind is no longer steadfast, nor your thinking.
216 Being still but a child, you held more prudent thoughts in your spirit;
217 now, when at last you are grown and come to the measure of manhood,
218 so that some stranger who looked on your stature and beauty
219 might say you were born as the son of some prosperous great man,
220 now your mind and your thought are no longer sound as they should be.
221 What is this deed that has happened here in the halls of the manor?
222 You allowed that stranger to be so shamefully handled before you.
223 How now, if some stranger who sat in our house as a suppliant
224 should suffer thus from disastrous rough treatment and torment?
225 On you would shame and reproach among all men surely be fallen.”
226 Wise Telemachus answered her openly, saying in turn:
227 “My mother, truly I do not resent you for being angry.
228 Yet I perceive in my heart and know every matter before me,
229 both the better and worse; but before this I was only a child still.
230 Yet I cannot in all things think everything through with good judgment;
231 for these men sitting beside me bewilder me, one after another,
232 plotting out evil; and there are no helpers to stand beside me.
233 Yet the affair of the stranger and Irus did not turn out
234 by the suitors’ wish, for in strength that man was the better.
235 If only, Father Zeus and Athena and lord Apollo,
236 so now in our halls may these suitors droop their heads, defeated,
237 some in the court-yard lying, and some inside in the dwelling,
238 and the limbs of each one be loosed, as now for that Irus
239 sitting there at the court-yard gate, with his head he keeps nodding
240 and is like a drunken man, unable to stand on his feet firm.
241 nor can he stand upright on his feet, nor make his way homeward,
242 home by whatever road his returning lies, since his own limbs are slackened.”
243 So the two of them spoke such things to each other together.
244 With his words Eurymachus answered prudent Penelope:
245 “Daughter of Icarius, wise-minded queen Penelope,
246 if all Achaean men through Jasonian Argos should see you,
247 even more suitors than these would feast in your halls from morning,
248 since you surpass all women by far in beauty and stature,
249 and in the good strong sense and understanding within you.”
250 Making her answer, prudent Penelope spoke and addressed him:
251 “Eurymachus, surely my worth and my beauty of figure
252 the immortals destroyed when the Argives embarked against Ilium,
253 and among them my husband Odysseus went sailing also.
254 If he came back again and took thought for the life I am living,
255 greater then would my glory be, and fairer also in this way.
256 But now I mourn, for the god has driven so many griefs on me.
257 Truly when he set out, leaving behind his fatherland country,
258 he took my right hand fast by the wrist and spoke these words to me:
259 “Wife, I do not suppose the well-greaved Achaeans
260 all will return from Troy to their homes unscathed and in safety.
261 For they say the Trojans themselves are warriors in battle,
262 both men who cast the spear and men who draw the bowstring,
263 and riders mounted upon swift-footed horses, who quickly
264 bring to decision the great contention of level war.
265 Therefore I do not know if some god will send me back homeward,
266 or if I shall fall there within Troy; but let all be your care here.
267 Be mindful there in the halls of my father and also my mother,
268 just as now, or even more, while I am absent and far off.
269 But whenever at last you see our son with beard on his chin grown,
270 marry whatever man you desire, and leave your own household.”
271 So he spoke in that way; and now all of these things are fulfilled.
272 There shall come a night when hateful marriage will face me and find me,
273 ruined woman that I am, from whom Zeus has taken prosperity.
274 But this terrible grief now comes on my heart and my spirit:
275 this is not how in former times the suitors behaved ever.
276 Men who are eager to court some noble woman and daughter
277 of a rich man, and strive with one another to win her,
278 those men bring in their own cattle and their stout sheep for slaughter,
279 making a feast for the girl’s own friends, and they give splendid gifts too;
280 they do not eat up another man’s livelihood free and unpurchased.”
281 So she spoke, and rejoicing was much-enduring noble Odysseus,
282 because she drew gifts out from them while bewitching their spirits
283 with her gentle words, though her mind was intent on other things wholly.
284 Once again Antinous spoke to her, son of Eupeithes:
285 “Daughter of Icarius, wise-minded queen Penelope,
286 whatever Achaean here may desire to bring gifts to you,
287 take them; for it is not seemly to refuse what is given.
288 But we will not go off to our work, nor elsewhere at all, lady,
289 not before you marry that Achaean who proves the best of us.”
290 So spoke Antinous, and his saying was pleasing to all of them.
291 Gifts to carry and fetch, each man sent out his herald,
292 For Antinous, his man brought in a robe, great and beautiful,
293 richly embroidered; and upon it there were twelve brooches
294 all of pure gold, fitted close with well-curved clasps in order.
295 And for Eurymachus at once his man brought a cunning-wrought necklace,
296 golden, threaded with amber beads, like the sun in its shining.
297 For Eurydamas, two serving-men brought a pair of earrings,
298 triple-clustered like mulberries; and much grace flashed from them brightly.
299 Out of the house of lord Peisander, the son of Polyctor,
300 a henchman brought a necklet, a marvel most lovely to look on.
301 And then the Achaeans brought fair gifts, one man after another.
302 Then up to her upper chamber went that radiant queen among women,
303 and with her, bearing the beautiful gifts, the handmaids followed.
304 But the men turned then to the dance and the song of desire and delighting,
305 and took their pleasure, awaiting the coming of evening upon them.
306 And upon them, delighting themselves, came the black dusk of evening.
307 Straightway they set up three braziers there inside the great hall,
308 so they might give them light, and around them they placed splintered firewood,
309 dry from long before, well seasoned, newly split with the bronze blade,
310 and among it they mingled pine torches; and in changing succession
311 the slave-women of long-enduring Odysseus kept them burning.
312 He himself, resourceful Odysseus, spoke to the women:
313 “Slave-women of Odysseus, your lord long absent and far off,
314 go to the chamber where our reverend queen has her dwelling;
315 sit there beside her and twist the distaff, and cheer her spirit,
316 sitting within her room, or card out the wool with your own hands.
317 I for my part will furnish light for all of these men here present.
318 Even if they should choose to wait for Dawn on her fair throne rising,
319 still they shall not wear me down; for I am most patient in hardship.”
320 So he spoke, and they laughed aloud, glancing at one another.
321 But fair-cheeked Melantho upbraided him harshly and shamelessly,
322 she whom Dolios fathered, but noble Penelope reared her,
323 cherishing her as a child, and gave her playthings to please her.
324 Yet for all that she never took Penelope's sorrow to heart, no,
325 but with Eurymachus lay, and loved him well as a lover.
326 She now addressed Odysseus with scornful and taunting reproaches:
327 “Wretched stranger, surely your wits have been wholly bewildered.
328 You will not go and sleep in the smithy after entering,
329 nor perhaps in some common shelter, but here you are talking
330 boldly among all these men in your overconfident spirit,
331 and there is no fear in your heart. Is wine now mastering
332 all of your mind, or is your thinking always thus foolish,
333 babbling empty words? Are you lightheaded now because lately
334 you defeated Irus, that vagabond beggar? Take care lest another,
335 better than Irus, quickly arise, who with hands that are sturdy
336 striking about your head may drive you blood-spattered from the house out.”
337 Glaring darkly from under his brows, wise Odysseus answered:
338 “Soon I will tell Telemachus, bitch, what things you are saying,
339 going to him there, so that he may hack you limb from limb instantly.”
340 So having spoken, with words he scattered the women in terror.
341 Off through the hall they went, and the limbs of each one were loosened
342 by their fear, for they thought that in truth he had spoken in earnest.
343 But he stood by the blazing torches that flared up in brightness,
344 watching them all; and within him his heart was pondering other
345 things in his inmost mind, and those were not left unaccomplished.
346 Yet Athena did not at all let the haughty suitors refrain from
347 hurtful abuse that cuts to the heart, so that still more sharply
348 grief might sink in the heart of Odysseus, son of Laertes.
349 And among them Eurymachus, Polybus' son, began speaking,
350 mocking Odysseus, and stirred up laughter among his companions:
351 “Hear me, suitors who seek the hand of our far-famed lady,
352 so I may say what the heart in my breast is bidding me utter.
353 Not without god has this fellow come here to Odysseus' household.
354 Yet to my mind the bright gleam of the torches is shining
355 straight from his head, since there is no hair there, not even the faintest.”
356 So he spoke, and then he addressed city-sacking Odysseus:
357 “Stranger, would you be willing to work for hire, if I took you
358 out to the edge of my fields? Your wage will surely be ample.
359 You could plant the tall trees, and gather the stones for the fences.
360 There I would furnish you bread in abundance all through the year round,
361 clothe you with garments to wear, and give shoes to bind on your feet too.
362 But since now you have learned such evil ways, you will not be willing
363 to go about doing work, but prefer to skulk through the city,
364 begging, so you may provide for your insatiable belly.”
365 Making reply, resourceful Odysseus spoke and addressed him:
366 “Eurymachus, if only there might come a contest of labor
367 between us two in the season of spring, when the days become longer,
368 out in the grassland, where I should hold in my hands a well-curved scythe,
369 and you should hold one as well, so we might make trial of labor,
370 fasting until deep dusk, with the grass still lying before us;
371 or if there should be oxen to drive, and they were the very best kind,
372 tawny and large, the two of them full-fed on the grasses,
373 equal in age and in pull, and their strength not slight nor exhausted,
374 and there should be four-acre tilth, with the soil crumbling under
375 plowshare, then you would see whether I could draw a furrow straight through.
376 Or if again the son of Cronos should stir up a battle
377 somewhere today, and I had a shield and a pair of spears with me
378 and a helmet all bronze, close-fitted around both my temples,
379 then you would see me among the first men joined in the fighting,
380 nor would you mockingly speak of this belly of mine any longer.
381 But you are violently insolent, and your spirit is pitiless;
382 and somehow you imagine that you are mighty and powerful,
383 because you live among only a few, and they are not worthy.
384 But if Odysseus should come and return to his fatherland country,
385 swiftly for you those doors, though indeed they are very broad ones,
386 would become narrow in flight through the forecourt out to the gateway.”
387 So he spoke, and Eurymachus felt even greater anger within him.
388 And with a glare from under his brows he addressed him in winged words:
389 “Ah, miserable man, very soon I will bring you to ruin outright
390 for these words that you boldly proclaim among all of these people,
391 nor is there any fear in your heart. Is wine now mastering
392 all of your wits, or is your mind always this sort of foolish,
393 since you speak empty words? Are you giddy because you bested Irus?”
394 So having spoken, he seized up a stool; but noble Odysseus
395 crouched at the knees of Amphinomos, lord from Dulichion,
396 fearing Eurymachus; so he struck the cupbearer's right hand,
397 and down to the earth with a booming clatter the wine-jug went falling,
398 Groaning aloud, the man went down in the dust, pitching backward.
399 All through the shadowy halls the suitors cried out in confusion,
400 and so would a man speak, glancing across at another beside him:
401 “If only that stranger had perished in roaming somewhere on another shore
402 ere he came here; then he would not have stirred up so mighty an uproar.
403 Now we are wrangling over beggars, and never a pleasure in feasting
404 will there be from a noble banquet, since what is baser is winning.”
405 But among them spoke also the hallowed strength of Telemachus:
406 “Strange men, you are mad, and no longer conceal in your spirits
407 food that you ate and the drink that you drank; some god is arousing you.
408 Come then, feast well, and go lie down at home when the spirit commands you;
409 as for me, I am driving no man at all from this household.”
410 So he spoke, and they all bit hard on their lips in amazement,
411 marveling much at Telemachus, how boldly indeed he had spoken.
412 In their midst Amphinomus rose in assembly and answered,
413 glorious son of Nisus, descended from lordly Aretias:
414 “Friends, let no man then be angry because of a word that is righteous,
415 answering back with hostile speech and with bitter contention.
416 Do not buffet the stranger about, nor any other servant
417 dwelling here in the halls of godlike Odysseus our master.
418 Come, let the wine-pourer begin with the cups and the first-poured libation,
419 so that, after pouring, we may lie down and go to our houses.
420 As for the stranger, let us leave him here in the halls of Odysseus
421 for Telemachus to care for, since to his dear house he came.”
422 So he spoke, and the speech that he uttered was pleasing to all of them.
423 Moulius the hero mixed in the bowl for the company gathered,
424 herald from Dulichium, and servant he was to Amphinomus.
425 Round to them all in order he served it; and they to the immortals
426 poured to the blessed ones, and then drank the honey-sweet wine up.
427 But when they had poured out libation and drunk to their heart's desiring,
428 then they went to go lie down, each man to his own house returning.
