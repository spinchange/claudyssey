# The Odyssey — Book XI

*The dead: Tiresias’s prophecy, Anticleia, the heroines, Agamemnon, Achilles, and Ajax.*

1 When we had come to the ship and descended again to the seashore,
2 first we proceeded to launch the vessel on glittering sea-brine,
3 setting the mast with its sail in the hollow black ship as we labored;
4 then we took hold of the sheep and conveyed them aboard her, and we too
5 climbed there in grief, while the great tears fell from our eyes in abundance.
6 Circe behind our vessel, the dark-blue-prowed ship of the seaway,
7 sent us a following wind that filled out the sail, a companion
8 excellent, Circe of beautiful hair, dread goddess, humanly speaking.
9 Then, when we carefully tended the tackle throughout the black vessel,
10 taking our seats, we were borne on our way by the wind and the steersman.
11 All of the day was the sail spread wide as she crossed through the sea-depths;
12 then went the sun from the sky, and the roads of the world became darkened.

13 So she arrived at the limits of Ocean, deep in his current.
14 There is the land and the city inhabited by Cimmerian people,
15 always enveloped in mist and in cloud; for the sun in his brightness
16 never looks down on the people below with the light of his sunbeams,
17 neither ascending the sky and approaching the starry dominion,
18 nor when he turns from the heavens again toward earth and its creatures;
19 murderous night is extended above these miserable mortals.
20 Coming ashore, we beached the vessel and unloaded the sheep there;
21 then by the stream of Ocean we made our way on our journey,
22 till we arrived at the place that the goddess Circe appointed.

23 There Perimedes and faithful Eurylochus held the victims.
24 Drawing the sharp-edged sword that hung from beside my strong thigh there,
25 I dug a trench that extended a cubit in each of its measures;
26 round it I poured a libation to all of the people departed,
27 honey and milk being first, then sweet wine poured as the second,
28 water the third, and above these sprinkled the white flour of barley.
29 Earnestly then I entreated the strengthless heads of the dead men,
30 vowing that, after my coming to Ithaca, there in my palace
31 I would give them my best barren cow and heap treasures for burning;
32 then for Tiresias alone I would slaughter a black-fleeced
33 ram that excelled all the others in strength and in worth in my sheepfold.
34 When with my vows and my prayers I had called on the races of dead men,
35 taking the sheep, I cut both of their throats with the pitiless bronze blade
36 over the trench, and the dark blood flowed; and the spirits assembled,
37 souls of the people who died, coming upward from Erebus’ darkness:
38 brides and unmarried youths and the old men broken by sorrow,
39 delicate maidens whose hearts were newly acquainted with anguish,
40 many men wounded and torn by the bronze-pointed spears in the battle,
41 warriors slaughtered in war with their blood-dark armor upon them.
42 Flocking around the pit in their crowds from every direction,
43 making a terrible cry—and the pale fear captured my spirit.
44 Then I encouraged my men and commanded the faithful companions
45 there to be skinning the sheep that lay by the pitiless bronze slain,
46 burning the bodies and offering prayers before the immortals,
47 powerful Hades and dread Persephone, queen of the darkness.
48 Drawing for myself the sharp-edged sword from beside my strong thigh there,
49 I sat and did not allow the strengthless heads of the dead men
50 near to the blood until I had questioned Tiresias fully.

51 First came the spirit of my companion Elpenor to meet me,
52 since he had not been buried beneath wide earth in a grave mound.
53 There in the palace of Circe we left his body behind us,
54 neither lamented nor buried, because other labor compelled us.
55 Seeing him there, I wept, and my heart took pity upon him;
56 raising my voice, I addressed him, and these words took wing as I uttered:
57 “Tell me, Elpenor, how you arrived in the shadowy darkness.
58 Coming on foot, you outstripped me although I was borne in a black ship.”
59 Thus did I speak, and he groaned with a wail and responded in answer:
60 “Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus endlessly resourceful,
61 evil fate from a god and immeasurable wine were my ruin.
62 Lying asleep in the palace of Circe, I did not remember
63 how to return by descending again on the long-built ladder;
64 headlong I fell from the roof, and the bones of my neck were completely
65 snapped from the spine, and my spirit descended to Hades’ dominion.
66 Now by those who remain and are absent from us, I beseech you:
67 first by your wife, then your father, the man who attended your childhood,
68 then by Telemachus, left as your only child in the palace.
69 Well do I know that on going from here and the house of the death-god,
70 you will return in your well-made vessel to Aeaea’s island.
71 There, O lord, I command you to call to remembrance my sorrow.
72 Do not depart and abandon me there unlamented, unburied,
73 turning away, lest I bring down the wrath of the gods upon you.
74 Burn me together with all of the armor that still is my own there;
75 pile up a tomb on the shore of the gray and unharvested sea-brine,
76 marking a luckless man, so the people to come may remember.
77 Do this for me, and upon the grave mound plant the oar that I handled,
78 rowing while I was alive in the midst of my faithful companions.”
79 Thus did he speak, and I answered the spirit and said in response then:
80 “Luckless man, I will carry out all these things and fulfill them.”
81 So we sat there, one answering one with sorrowful language,
82 I on this side of the trench while holding my sword by the blood-pool,
83 while on the other the phantom of my dead comrade continued.

84 Then came the spirit of my mother, already departed,
85 lord Autolycus’ daughter Anticleia, born to the great-heart,
86 whom I had left still living when going to sacred Ilium.
87 Seeing her there, I wept, and my heart took pity upon her;
88 yet, though mourning her bitterly, I did not allow her to come there
89 near to the blood until I had questioned Tiresias fully.

90 Then came the spirit of Theban Tiresias, prophet and seer-man,
91 holding a golden scepter; he knew who I was and addressed me:
92 “Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus endlessly resourceful,
93 why, unhappy one, have you left behind you the brightness of sunlight,
94 coming to look on the dead and this place where delight cannot enter?
95 Draw yourself back from the trench and withdraw your sharp sword from over it,
96 so I may drink of the blood and reveal you the certain prediction.”
97 Thus did he speak; I withdrew and I thrust the silver-embellished
98 sword in its sheath once again. When the prophet had drunk the black life-blood,
99 only then did the blameless seer turn toward me and answer:
100 “Honey-sweet homeward return is your wish, O shining Odysseus,
101 but an immortal will render it grievous. I do not imagine
102 earth-shaking lord Poseidon will fail to remember; the anger
103 planted within him endures, for you blinded the son he held dearest.
104 Even through hardships and sorrow, however, you still may return home,
105 if you are willing to master your own heart and those of your comrades,
106 from the first hour when your well-made vessel approaches the island
107 called Thrinacia, after escaping the violet sea-depths.
108 There you will find at their pasture the cattle and sturdy sheep-flocks
109 sacred to Helios, lord who sees all things and hears every utterance.
110 If you leave these all unharmed and remember your difficult homeward
111 journey, you still may arrive upon Ithaca, suffering greatly.
112 But if you harm them, I prophesy utter destruction
113 both for your ship and companions; and even if you should escape it,
114 late will you come and in evil condition, with all comrades lost there,
115 borne in another man’s vessel, and finding affliction at home there:
116 arrogant men who are eating away all the wealth of your household,
117 courting your godlike wife and bestowing their gifts for the bridal.
118 Surely, when home, you will punish the violent acts they committed.
119 After you slaughter the suitors within the halls of your palace,
120 whether by cunning or openly striking with sharp-edged bronze there,
121 then undertake one more journey, a well-shaped oar on your shoulder,
122 till you arrive among people who have no knowledge of sea-brine,
123 people who never eat food with the salt of the sea intermixed there,
124 having no knowledge of crimson-cheeked ships that travel the sea-roads,
125 nor of the well-shaped oars that serve as the wings of the vessels.
126 I will reveal you a sign unmistakable; you will not miss it.
127 When another traveler meets you abroad on the road there,
128 saying you carry a winnowing fan on your glittering shoulder,
129 then in the earth at that place you must fix the well-fashioned oar-blade,
130 offering beautiful victims to lord Poseidon, the sea-god:
131 sacrifice one ram there with a bull and a boar that mounts she-swine.
132 Then turn away toward your home and present to the gods their appointed
133 sacred hecatombs, to the deathless gods of the wide-ruling heavens,
134 every immortal in order. Your death will arrive from the seaward,
135 death without violence, peaceful, and take you away in the ripeness
136 under a sleek old age, with the people around you in plenty.
137 These words I speak are the truth, and the course of events will confirm them.”
138 Thus did he speak, and I answered the prophet and said in response then:
139 “Lord Tiresias, surely the gods spun all of this future.
140 Come now, tell me this other, and speak with the truth as I ask you.
141 There I behold the spirit of my dead mother beside us,
142 sitting in silence close to the blood, and she does not venture
143 either to look in the face of her son or address him with language.
144 Tell me, lord, by what means may she know I am truly her own son?”
145 Thus did I speak, and at once the spirit responded in answer:
146 “Easily spoken the word I will tell you and set in your thinking:
147 whomever among the people departed you willingly suffer
148 near to the blood, that spirit will tell you the truth in its fullness;
149 whomever you will not allow, that spirit will turn and depart you.”
150 Thus having spoken, the spirit of lord Tiresias departed,
151 into the house of Hades, when all of the fates were revealed there.
152 Yet I remained where I stood until my mother approached me,
153 drinking the dark-clouded blood, and at once she knew me and addressed me;
154 wailing aloud, she spoke, and these words took wing as she uttered:
155 “Child, how did you come living beneath the shadowy darkness?
156 Hard is the vision of all these things for the people still living;
157 terrible waters and powerful rivers are lying between us,
158 Ocean first, which no person on foot has the power to cross over,
159 never, unless in possession of one well-constructed black vessel.
160 Have you but now come wandering here from the country of Trojans,
161 after so long a time, with your ship and the faithful companions?
162 Have you not come to Ithaca yet, nor seen your wife in the palace?”
163 Thus did she speak, and I answered my mother and said in response then:
164 “Mother, necessity brought me below to the house of the death-god,
165 there to inquire of the spirit of Theban Tiresias, prophet.
166 I have not yet gone near to Achaea, nor have I ever
167 set foot again on the land that is ours, but wander in sorrow
168 since that first day I followed the noble lord Agamemnon,
169 marching to Ilium, land of fine horses, to battle the Trojans.
170 Come now, tell me this other, and speak with the truth as I ask you:
171 what kind of death that lays humankind low overwhelmed and subdued you?
172 Was it prolonged disease? Did Artemis, lady of arrows,
173 come with the gentle shafts of her bow and destroy you beside them?
174 Tell me as well of my father and son whom I left in the palace:
175 whether my honor is safe in their keeping, or whether another
176 man now possesses it all, and they say I will never be homeward.
177 Tell me the mind and the purpose as well of the wife I have wedded:
178 does she remain by the boy and keep everything safe in the household,
179 or has the best of Achaeans already taken and wed her?”
180 Thus did I speak, and at once my honored mother responded:
181 “Yes, she remains in your halls with a heart that is steadfast and patient;
182 there she abides in the palace, and always in misery grievous
183 nights are consumed from her life, and the days are dissolved in her weeping.
184 No man yet has taken possession of your royal honor;
185 still unopposed, Telemachus tends to the fields and is feasting
186 there at the equal banquets that suit an administrator of justice,
187 since every man calls him forth. But your father remains in the country,
188 never descending to town, and possesses no bed for his sleeping,
189 neither a bedframe nor cloaks nor the brilliant blankets to cover him.
190 All through the winter he sleeps where the slaves in the household are sleeping,
191 close to the fire in the ashes, and dresses his body in rags there;
192 but when the summer arrives and the generous autumn is ripening,
193 then on the rising ground of his vineyard, wherever he chooses,
194 beds of the fallen leaves are scattered along on the earth there.
195 There he lies grieving, and sorrow grows great in the heart that is in him,
196 yearning for your homecoming, while harsh old age bears upon him.
197 So it was likewise with me: so I perished and met my destruction.
198 Neither within the palace did keen-eyed Artemis find me,
199 pouring her gentle arrows upon me and taking my life there,
200 nor did disease come upon me, the kind which commonly wears down
201 limbs with its hateful wasting and draws from the body its spirit.
202 No; it was longing for you, for your counsels, O shining Odysseus,
203 and for your tenderheartedness, robbing me of honey-sweet lifebreath.”
204 Thus did she speak; and I, pondering deeply, was eager within me
205 there to embrace the spirit of my dead mother and hold her.
206 Three times I started toward her, and three times the heart bade me clasp her;
207 three times away from my hands like a shadow or dream she went flying.
208 Sharper each time did the pain in my own heart gather within me;
209 raising my voice, I addressed her, and these words took wing as I uttered:
210 “Mother, why will you not remain when I yearn to embrace you,
211 so that, even in Hades, our arms may be thrown round each other,
212 both of us taking our fill of the terrible chill of lamenting?
213 Or are you only a phantom that queen Persephone sent me,
214 so I may suffer yet more in my pitiful groaning and weeping?”
215 Thus did I speak, and at once my honored mother responded:
216 “Ah, my child, most luckless of all humankind in your sorrow,
217 Zeus’s daughter Persephone does not deceive you in any way.
218 This is the settled condition of mortals whenever they perish:
219 sinews no longer are holding together the flesh and the bone-frame;
220 these are destroyed by the fierce and powerful strength of the blazing
221 fire, when life has departed away from the white bones within them;
222 then like a dream the spirit flies free and is hovering onward.
223 Hasten toward light, however, as quickly as ever you can now;
224 mark these things, so you may hereafter report them all to your wife there.”
225 So the two of us stayed exchanging our words with each other.
226 Then came the women—for queen Persephone sent them before me—
227 all who had once been the wives and the daughters of excellent heroes.
228 Flocking in multitudes there, they surrounded the dark-colored life-blood;
229 I in my mind was considering how I could question each woman.
230 This, as I thought it all over, appeared to my mind as the best plan:
231 drawing the long-edged sword that hung from beside my strong thigh there,
232 I did not suffer them all at one time to drink the black life-blood.
233 One after one they approached in a file, and each woman recounted
234 all of her birth and her lineage; I questioned every one of them.

235 There as the first among women I saw highborn lady Tyro,
236 saying that she was the child of the blameless ruler Salmoneus,
237 also the wife of Cretheus, the son of the lordly Aeolus.
238 She fell in love with a river, Enipeus’ beautiful god-stream,
239 far and away the most beautiful river that runs on the earth-land;
240 often she walked by Enipeus’ beautiful waters and wandered.
241 Then lord Poseidon, the earth-holding shaker, assuming the river’s
242 likeness, lay down there beside her at mouths of the eddying water.
243 Darkly a wave like a mountain arose and surrounded them, arching
244 over them both, and it hid the immortal and mortal woman.
245 There he unfastened her maidenly girdle and poured sleep upon her.
246 After the god had completed the works of desire and of loving,
247 taking her hand in his own, he addressed her and called her by title:
248 “Woman, rejoice in our loving; when this same season returns here,
249 you will give birth to illustrious children; the bed of immortals
250 never is fruitless. Attend to your children and carefully rear them.
251 Go to your home now, remain in your silence, and utter no god’s name;
252 know I am lord Poseidon, the powerful shaker of earth-land.”
253 Thus having spoken, he plunged underneath the wave-surging sea-depths.
254 She, having then conceived, gave birth to both Pelias, Neleus,
255 each of whom grew into powerful servants of Zeus the almighty.
256 Pelias dwelt in Iolcus, the wide-dancing city, with rich flocks;
257 Neleus meanwhile inhabited Pylos, the city of sand-dunes.
258 Other sons this queen among women gave Cretheus as children:
259 Aeson, Amythaon, skilled with the chariot, Pheres.
260 Next I beheld Antiope, the daughter of river Asopus,
261 proud to have rested in love in the very embraces of great Zeus;
262 she gave birth to twin sons, Amphion and lordly Zethus,
263 first to establish the site of the seven-gated city of Thebes there,
264 walling it round, for without such a wall they could not have settled,
265 powerful though they were, in the broad-dancing spaces of Thebes there.
266 Next I beheld Amphitryon’s beautiful wife, lady Alcmene,
267 mother of lionhearted Heracles, bold in the battle,
268 born when she rested in love in the mighty embraces of great Zeus;
269 also Megara, high-spirited Creon’s beautiful daughter,
270 wife of Amphitryon’s son, whose strength was eternally tireless.

271 Then I beheld Epicaste, the beautiful mother of Oedipus,
272 she who accomplished a terrible deed in her mind’s unawareness,
273 wedding her son, after he had taken the life of his father;
274 soon the immortals made these things known to the people around them.
275 Yet in beloved Thebes he continued to rule the Cadmeians,
276 suffering anguish because of the gods’ destruction-appointed
277 counsels; but she went down into Hades’ strongly barred palace,
278 fastening high on the roof beam a noose that would hang her in midair,
279 seized by her sorrow, and leaving her son those torments unnumbered,
280 all that the Furies belonging to mothers are bringing to fullness.

281 Then I saw Chloris, a woman of beauty beyond every telling,
282 whom lord Neleus married for beauty with gifts beyond counting,
283 youngest of daughters descended from Iasus’ son lord Amphion,
284 who once ruled in his strength in Minyan Orchomenus’ city.
285 She was the queen of Pylos and bore him illustrious children:
286 Nestor and Chromius, also the lordly prince Periclymenus;
287 after the sons she bore mighty Pero, a marvel to mortals,
288 courted by every man in the neighboring lands; but her father
289 would not give her to any man unable to steal from the country
290 mighty Iphicles’ broad-browed cattle with horns that were curving,
291 out of Phylace, dangerous herd. The blameless prophet
292 only attempted the deed, but the merciless fate of a god bound
293 him in cruel bonds, and the brutal herdsmen detained him.
294 But when the months and the days at last had approached their completion,
295 after the year turned round and the seasons again had returned there,
296 mighty Iphicles finally freed him from bonds in the prison,
297 after he told every fate; and Zeus’s will was accomplished.

298 Then I beheld queen Leda, the wife of the lord Tyndareus,
299 she who bore two sons to Tyndareus, powerful-hearted:
300 horse-breaking Castor, and Polydeuces, excellent boxer.
301 Both are embraced by the life-giving earth, yet both are still living;
302 even beneath the earth they have honor from Zeus the almighty,
303 living in turn every other day and then dying in turn there,
304 each on his separate day; they possess equal honor with godhood.

305 Next I beheld Iphimedeia, the wife of lord Aloeus,
306 she who declared she had lain in the arms of the god Poseidon.
307 Two sons she bore—but their days in the world of the living were brief ones—
308 godlike Otus and Ephialtes, renowned among all men,
309 tallest by far of the mortals the grain-giving earth ever nurtured,
310 also the fairest in form with the sole exception Orion.
311 Nine years old, they measured nine cubits across in their broadness,
312 also in height their bodies extended to nine mighty fathoms.
313 Even the gods they threatened, proposing to carry the tumult
314 of furious war to Olympus, the home of the deathless immortals.
315 Ossa they labored to set on Olympus, then leafy and forested
316 Pelion high upon Ossa, that heaven might be made accessible.
317 This they would surely have done if they came to the measure of manhood;
318 but the great son of Zeus whom Leto of beautiful hair bore
319 slew them both, before on the sides of their heads the first down bloomed,
320 growing to thicken their chins with the flourishing beard of the young man.

321 Phaedra I saw and Procris, and beautiful princess Ariadne,
322 daughter of deadly-minded king Minos, whom Theseus carried
323 once from the island of Crete, making sail for the sacred Athenian
324 hill, but he took no delight in her; Artemis earlier killed her
325 there on the sea-ringed Dia, because Dionysus accused her.
326 Maera I saw, and Clymene, also detestable Eriphyle,
327 she who accepted precious gold for betraying the husband she married.
328 Yet I could never recount every woman I saw there or name them,
329 all of the wives and the daughters belonging to powerful heroes;
330 god-given night would come to an end before I had finished.
331 Now it is time I should sleep, whether going to my swift vessel
332 and my companions, or staying. My sending belongs to the gods and to you all.”
333 Thus did he speak, and all of the people remained without speaking,
334 held by the spell of his story throughout the shadowy palace.
335 Then white-armed Arete arose and began to address them:
336 “Men of Phaeacia, how does this man now appear to your judgment,
337 both in his physical form and his stature and balanced perception?
338 He is my guest, but every one of you shares in the honor.
339 Therefore do not be eager to send him away, nor diminish
340 gifts to a man with such need, since many beautiful treasures
341 lie in your houses through all the generous will of immortals.”
342 Then the old hero Echeneus arose and addressed the assembly,
343 eldest of all of the other Phaeacian men by his birth date:
344 “Friends, our intelligent queen has not spoken wide of our purpose,
345 nor has she missed it; obey every word that the queen has commanded.
346 Yet on Alcinous here both the word and the deed are depending.”
347 Then Alcinous answered the elder and said in response there:
348 “This word will stand, and as surely as I have my life and am reigning
349 over the oar-loving people Phaeacian, so I will fulfill it.
350 Let this guest, though he hungers for homecoming, bear to remain here
351 till tomorrow arrives and I make every gift full in measure.
352 All of the men will attend to his sending; but I above others,
353 since in the land of Phaeacians I hold the kingly dominion.”
354 Then resourceful Odysseus addressed him in words of response there:
355 “Lord Alcinous, most renowned among all the peoples,
356 if you were asking that I should remain for a year in the palace,
357 then made ready my sending and gave me illustrious treasures,
358 even would I choose that, for it surely would be more profitable,
359 coming at last to the land that I love with a fuller possession.
360 Then I should certainly find myself more honored and cherished
361 among the people who saw me come sailing again to my Ithaca.”
362 Then Alcinous answered the hero and said in response there:
363 “Lord Odysseus, looking at you, we never could liken
364 you to a swindler or thief, such as dark earth nurtures in plenty,
365 scattering crops of such men through the length and the breadth of the nations,
366 fashioning falsehoods from things no mortal perception could question.
367 Beautiful form clothes every word, and within is a noble
368 spirit; and just like a singer who knows, you have told us the story,
369 both the disastrous sorrows of Argives and those you encountered.
370 Come now, tell me this other, and speak with the truth as I ask you:
371 whether you saw there any of your companions, the godlike,
372 those who accompanied you into Troy and encountered their death there.
373 Marvelously long is the night, inexhaustible; this is no hour yet
374 here in the hall to be sleeping. Continue to tell me your wonders.
375 I could endure until shining Dawn in her glory appeared here,
376 if you could bear in the hall to recount every sorrow you suffered.”
377 Then resourceful Odysseus addressed him in words of response there:
378 “Lord Alcinous, most renowned among all the peoples,
379 there is a season for many a story, a season for sleeping.
380 Yet if your heart still desires to listen, I will not deny you
381 stories of other events that are even more worthy of pity:
382 all of the sorrows of friends who died at a later time there,
383 men who escaped from the groan and the war cry of Trojan battalions,
384 only to die on the homeward way through the will of an evil woman.

385 When Persephone, holy and queenly, had driven the spirits
386 this way and that of the women who died and had gone to the death-world,
387 there came the soul of the lord Agamemnon, the son born of Atreus,
388 grieving; and round him the spirits were gathered of all those companions
389 slaughtered with him in Aegisthus’ palace and meeting their death there.
390 At once he knew me after he drank of the dark-colored life-blood;
391 shrilly he wept with a cry, and the great tears fell from his eyelids,
392 stretching his hands in his longing to reach me and take me and hold me.
393 Yet there was neither the strength still fixed in the limbs nor the sinewy
394 force which had filled his supple and powerful body in old days.
395 Seeing him there, I wept, and my heart took pity upon him;
396 raising my voice, I addressed him, and these words took wing as I uttered:
397 “Atreus’ son Agamemnon, O most renowned king of armies,
398 what kind of death that lays humankind low overwhelmed and subdued you?
399 Did lord Poseidon overcome you aboard the black vessels,
400 raising a terrible blast of the ruinous winds in his anger?
401 Or did malicious men cut you down on the dry land while you were
402 stealing their cattle and beautiful sheep, or perhaps while you battled
403 over their city and all of the women inhabiting in it?”
404 Thus did I speak, and at once his spirit responded in answer:
405 “Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus endlessly resourceful,
406 lord Poseidon did not overcome me aboard the black vessels,
407 raising a terrible blast of the ruinous winds in his anger;
408 nor did malicious men cut me down on the dry land and slaughter me.
409 No: my death and my doom were devised by the treacherous lord Aegisthus,
410 aided by my accursed wife, when he called me inside and then killed me,
411 feasting me first, as a man at the manger will slaughter an ox there.
412 Thus I died by the most pitiable death, and around me the others,
413 all my companions, were ceaselessly slaughtered like white-tusked swine there,
414 cut down inside of the house of a wealthy and powerful ruler,
415 whether to furnish a wedding, a feast, or a generous banquet.
416 You have been present before at the violent deaths of great numbers,
417 slaughtered alone or together within the ferocity of battle;
418 yet you would certainly grieve in your heart above all at that one sight,
419 seeing how there by the wine bowl and tables abundantly loaded
420 all of us lay in the hall, while the floor smoked upward with life-blood.
421 Most to be pitied of all was the voice I heard of Cassandra,
422 Priam’s daughter, whom treacherous Clytemnestra then murdered
423 over my body; and dying, I tried to lift up both my hands there,
424 striking them down on the sword, while that dog-faced woman withdrew then.
425 Nor, though I went into Hades, did she have the heart to turn toward me,
426 closing my mouth with her hands or shutting my eyes for the death-world.
427 Nothing is truly more dreadful or shamelessly doglike than woman
428 when she establishes such atrocious deeds in her thinking,
429 just as the woman designed this deed that cannot be spoken,
430 plotting the death and destruction of him who had married and loved her.
431 I had supposed I would come to the joy of my children and servants;
432 she, however, the mistress of outrage, has poured a dishonor
433 over her own head and that of all women who ever come after,
434 all of the feminine sex, and even the woman of virtue.”
435 Thus did he speak, and I answered the spirit and said in response then:
436 “Ah, how terribly Zeus of the far voice hated and harried
437 Atreus’ line from the first, through the treacherous planning of women.
438 We for the sake of Helen were dying in thousands at Troas;
439 Clytemnestra for you, while you were far off, spread out the death-snare.”
440 Thus did I speak, and at once his spirit responded in answer:
441 “Therefore never be gentle yourself, even dealing with your wife;
442 never reveal every word that you know and disclose it before her,
443 but tell part of the story, and let the remainder be hidden.
444 Yet no murder will come to Odysseus ever from woman;
445 far too wise and intelligent-minded within is the daughter
446 born to Icarius, prudent and thoughtful Penelope, your wife.
447 Young as a bride was the woman we left when we went to the battle;
448 there was a boy at her breast, no more than an infant, who doubtless
449 now takes his seat among men and is counted among them, a fortunate
450 child! For the father who loves him will see him on coming to homeland,
451 and he will lovingly clasp his father, the fitting tradition.
452 But my own wife never allowed me to fill both my eyes there,
453 looking upon my child, before she destroyed me, his father.
454 One other thing I will tell you; remember and store it within you:
455 secretly, never in public, bring your ship into your own
456 cherished country, since women can never be trusted hereafter.
457 Come now, tell me this other, and speak with the truth as I ask you:
458 have you heard whether somewhere my son still dwells with the living,
459 either in Orchomenus or perhaps in the sandy-soiled Pylos,
460 or with the lord Menelaus in wide-spreading Sparta?
461 Surely the noble Orestes has not yet died on the earth-land.”
462 Thus did he speak, and I answered the spirit and said in response then:
463 “Atreus’ son, why question me this? I know no report of him,
464 whether he lives or has died; empty words are an evil to utter.”
465 So the two of us stood exchanging our sorrowful language,
466 grieving deeply in heart and letting the great tears fall from us.

467 Then came the spirit of lord Achilles, the son born of Peleus,
468 also Patroclus’ spirit, and blameless Antilochus with him,
469 Ajax as well, who exceeded in beauty and bodily stature
470 all of the Danaan host but the blameless son born of Peleus.
471 Then the soul of Aeacus’ swift-footed grandson knew me;
472 wailing aloud, he spoke, and these words took wing as he uttered:
473 “Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus endlessly resourceful,
474 reckless one, what deed even greater than this can your mind plan?
475 How did you dare come down into Hades’ realm, where the dead men
476 dwell without understanding, the phantoms of wearied mortals?”
477 Thus did he speak, and I answered the spirit and said in response then:
478 “Peleus’ son Achilles, most powerful far of Achaeans,
479 seeking Tiresias I came from necessity, hoping the prophet
480 might have a counsel to bring me again to ruggedly coasted Ithaca.
481 I have not yet gone near to Achaea, nor have I ever
482 set foot again on my land, but affliction is always around me.
483 Yet, Achilles, no man was ever more blessed than you in the old days,
484 neither before nor hereafter; while living, the Argives revered you
485 just like the gods; and now you rule powerfully here among dead men.
486 Therefore do not be grieved that you died, Achilles, the godlike.”
487 Thus did I speak, and at once his spirit responded in answer:
488 “Shining Odysseus, do not try soothing my death with your language.
489 I would prefer to be bound to the soil as the serf of another,
490 even a landless man who possesses a little subsistence,
491 rather by far than to rule over all of the dead who have wasted.
492 Come, tell me now of the son I have fathered, the proud-hearted young man:
493 whether he followed to war so that he might be first in the battle.
494 Tell me as well of the blameless Peleus, whatever you heard there:
495 whether he keeps the honor he had in the Myrmidon nation,
496 or is dishonored throughout both Hellas and Phthia because now
497 terrible old age captures his hands and his feet and confines him.
498 I no longer am there as his champion under the sunbeams,
499 such as I was in the former days when throughout the broad Troas
500 I killed the best of the armies, defending the people of Argos.
501 If I could come to the house of my father again for an hour,
502 such as I was, I would make my rage and unconquerable hands there
503 dreadful to those who abuse him with force and deny him his honor.”
504 Thus did he speak, and I answered the spirit and said in response then:
505 “Nothing concerning the blameless Peleus ever has reached me.
506 Yet of the son whom you love, Neoptolemus, I can recount you
507 all of the certain truth, as you ask and desire that I tell it.
508 I myself in my hollow and balanced vessel conveyed him,
509 out of the island of Scyros to join strong-greaved Achaeans.
510 Whenever around Troy’s city we shaped our counsels together,
511 always he spoke as the first, and his words never missed their intention;
512 godlike Nestor alone and I could surpass him in counsel.
513 Whenever we fought on the plain of the Trojans with bronze in our hands there,
514 never he lingered behind in the crowd and the press of the people,
515 but ran far out in front, yielding fury to none of the fighters,
516 slaughtering many men in the terrible shock of the battle.
517 All of the people he killed I could neither recount to you nor name,
518 all of the host he destroyed in defending the Argive battalions;
519 yet he killed such a man as the hero Eurypylus, Telephus’ offspring,
520 cutting him down with the bronze, and around him his numerous Ceteian
521 comrades were slaughtered because of the gifts to a woman presented.
522 Him I beheld as the handsomest man after Memnon, the noble.
523 When we went down in the wooden horse that Epeius fashioned,
524 we who were best of the Argives, and all the command was upon me,
525 either to open the close-packed ambush or shut and restrain it,
526 then all the other leaders and counselors serving the Danaans
527 wiped the tears from their eyes, and the limbs under each man were trembling;
528 never once did my eyes then see your son’s beautiful color
529 pale in his skin, nor see him wipe tears from his eyelids in terror.
530 No; he entreated me over and over again, and he never
531 ceased from his hand on the hilt of the sword and the bronze-heavy spear-shaft,
532 carrying evil intent for the Trojans within him throughout it.
533 After we sacked the towering city of Priam in battle,
534 then he embarked with his share and a beautiful prize of distinction,
535 wholly unscarred, neither struck by a spear from afar in the battle
536 nor by the bronze in the close work of war, as happens so often;
537 Ares in blind fury makes no distinctions among men.”
538 Thus did I speak; and the spirit of swift-footed Aeacus’ grandson
539 strode with long steps away through the fields of the pale asphodel plants,
540 joyful because I had said that his son had excelled among heroes.

541 All of the rest of the souls of the people who died and departed
542 stood there in sorrow, and each one asked of the things it held dearest.
543 Only the spirit of Telamon’s son, lord Ajax, remained far
544 off, still enraged at the victory I had secured and defeated
545 him when beside the vessels I argued my claim for the armor
546 worn by Achilles; his honored mother had offered the prizes,
547 sons of the Trojans made judgment of us, and Pallas Athena.
548 Would that I never had won such a victory! Down in the darkness
549 earth had enclosed such a head for the sake of that armor, lord Ajax,
550 he who surpassed all Danaans in bodily beauty and war-work,
551 all but the blameless son of Peleus, Achilles, the godlike.
552 Him I addressed as I tried to persuade him with honey-sweet language:
553 “Ajax, child of blameless Telamon, will you not ever,
554 even in death, put away your anger with me for the cursed
555 armor? The gods made that armor a plague for the Argive battalions;
556 such was the tower overthrown when you died. All the people Achaean
557 mourn for you always, no less than the head of the great lord Achilles,
558 Peleus’ son. No other is guilty of causing your sorrow,
559 only great Zeus, who hated the Danaan army of spearmen,
560 terribly, laying the doom that you suffered upon you in anger.
561 Come here, lord, so you may hear the word and the speech we would give you.
562 Conquer your fury and pride in the heart; let your anger be ended.”
563 Thus did I speak; he answered me nothing, but went on his journey,
564 joining the other souls in the Erebus of the departed.
565 There, although in his anger, he still might have spoken toward me,
566 or I to him; but the heart in the breast that was in me desired
567 there to behold the spirits of others among the departed.

568 There I beheld Minos, the splendid son born of great Zeus,
569 holding his scepter of gold and dispensing the laws to the dead men,
570 seated, while round him the dead were appealing their cases before him,
571 some of them seated, some standing throughout Hades’ wide-gated palace.
572 Then after him I distinguished the giant Orion,
573 herding together the beasts on the field of the pale asphodel plants,
574 creatures that he had killed on the lonely mountains in old days;
575 holding a club made entirely of bronze, forever unbroken.

576 Tityus also I saw, the child of the glorious Earth-goddess,
577 lying extended along the ground over nine roods in measure;
578 vultures, two of them, perched on the sides of his body and tore at
579 both sides of his liver, their beaks in the caul, while he could not repel them.
580 This was because he assaulted Zeus’s honored consort, the goddess
581 Leto, when she went through lovely-dancing Panopeus toward Pytho.

582 Tantalus also I saw, enduring a terrible torment,
583 standing in water, the pool lapping up to the point of his chin there;
584 suffering thirst, he could not take up even one drop for drinking.
585 Every time that the old man bent in his eagerness, hoping
586 he might drink, all the water was swallowed away and had vanished;
587 black earth showed at his feet, made dry by a god in the sunlight.
588 High-leafed trees above him were pouring their fruit by his forehead,
589 pears and pomegranates and apple trees shining with fruit in abundance,
590 figs in their sweetness and olives that flourished in fullest profusion.
591 Every time that the old man stretched out his hands to take hold of
592 these, a wind would toss them away toward the dark-shadowed cloud banks.

593 Sisyphus also I saw, enduring a terrible torment,
594 grappling a monstrous stone in his arms and attempting to raise it;
595 bracing his body and straining with powerful hands and with both feet,
596 upward he pushed the stone to the summit; but every time he
597 nearly was going to thrust it across, its terrible weight turned,
598 rolling the shameless boulder again to the plain at the hill-foot.
599 Then he would struggle again and would push, while sweat from his members
600 streamed, and the dust from the labor arose from his head in a cloud there.

601 Then after him I distinguished the powerful Heracles—only
602 phantom of him, for the man with the deathless immortals is feasting,
603 filled with delight, and possesses as wife fair-ankled Hebe,
604 daughter of Zeus the almighty and Hera of gold-glimmering sandals.
605 Round the phantom the cries of the dead men arose like the cries of
606 birds as they scatter in terror; like black night he stood there before them,
607 holding his naked bow in his hand, with the arrow on bowstring,
608 terribly glaring about like a man who was always about to be shooting.
609 Dreadful to see was the baldric that crossed the great breadth of his chest there,
610 belt for the sword made of gold, where marvelous works were depicted:
611 bears and the wild forest boars and the lions with fire in their eyesight,
612 battles and combats and violent murders and slaughter of heroes.
613 May he who put such designs in the belt never fashion another,
614 he who conceived in his craft and accomplished a wonderful object.
615 He in his turn recognized me as soon as his eyes fell upon me;
616 wailing aloud, he spoke, and these words took wing as he uttered:
617 “Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus endlessly resourceful,
618 luckless one, are you also enduring the terrible fortune
619 I had to bear underneath the illuminating rays of the sunlight?
620 Though I was Zeus’s own son and descended from Cronus, my troubles
621 never were ended; I served under one who was far my inferior,
622 and on my shoulders the man laid labors of terrible hardship.
623 Once he commanded me here to recover the Hound of the death-god,
624 reckoning no other trial could ever be stronger than this one.
625 Yet I dragged up the Hound and conveyed him outside of the death-world;
626 Hermes accompanied me, and Athena, the gray-eyed goddess.”
627 Thus having spoken, he went to the house of the god Hades once more.
628 Yet I remained where I stood in the hope some other would come there,
629 one of the hero men who had died in the generations before us.
630 Then I would surely have seen the men of the old age I longed for,
631 Theseus and Pirithous, illustrious children of godhood;
632 but the innumerable tribes of the dead first gathered around me,
633 thousands on thousands, with cries no earthly thing could resemble;
634 pale fear seized me lest queen Persephone send out of Hades
635 toward me the terrible Gorgon’s head, that monstrous abomination.
636 Straight to the ship I went and commanded the faithful companions
637 all to embark and release every cable that fastened the stern there.
638 Swiftly they came on the ship and sat down by the tholes of the oarlocks;
639 then did the current’s billow convey her along Ocean’s river,
640 first under rowing, and afterward borne by a beautiful fair wind.
