# The Odyssey — Book 23

*The bed; the test that Penelope wins; the long night held back.*

1 Up to the upper room ran the old woman with laughter about her,
2 going to tell her mistress: inside was her own dear husband.
3 Fast beat her knees in haste, and her feet kept stumbling beneath her.
4 Standing above her head, she spoke a word, and addressed her:
5 “Wake, Penelope, wake now, dear child, so that you may witness
6 with your own eyes the thing you have longed for all of your lifetime.
7 Here is Odysseus, come back home to his house, though he came late.
8 And he has killed the haughty suitors, those men who in his household
9 wrought him affliction, and ate his possessions, oppressing his offspring.”
10 And in return the circumspect Penelope answered:
11 “Dear nurse, the gods have made you mad, they who are able
12 even to make a very wise mind foolish and witless,
13 and can endow a thoughtless one with a share of good judgment.
14 They have unhinged your mind; before, your senses were steady.
15 Why do you mock me, when I carry a heart full of sorrows,
16 telling me these wild things, and waking me up from my slumber,
17 sweet sleep, that closed all around my dear eyelids and bound them?
18 Never until now have I slept so deeply as on this occasion,
19 since Odysseus went away to see that evil-named Ilion.
20 Come now, go down again, and make your return to the hallway.
21 If any other among all the women that wait in my household
22 came and told me these things and awakened me out of my slumber,
23 I would have sent her quickly away to return in sorry fashion,
24 but in your case your old age will be your profit in this matter.”
25 To her in turn the dear nurse Eurycleia answered:
26 “Child, I am not mocking you, dear one, but very truly
27 here is Odysseus, come back home to his house, as I tell you,
28 that very stranger whom all men dishonored here in the palace.
29 Long since Telemachus had known that he was here in the household,
30 but in his prudence he kept concealed his father’s intentions,
31 so that he might exact punishment for the force of those proud men.”
32 So she spoke, and she rejoiced, and sprang from the bed in her gladness,
33 clasped the old woman around, and tears came down from her eyelids.
34 And lifting her voice she addressed her with winged words, saying:
35 “Come then, dear nurse, tell me this truly and with no falsehood:
36 if in very truth he has come to the house, as you are saying,
37 how did he ever lay hands on the shameless suitors and master them,
38 being alone, while always they stayed within all together?”
39 To her in turn the dear nurse Eurycleia answered:
40 “I did not see it, nor did I learn it; I only heard groaning
41 from the men being killed; while we in the innermost chamber
42 sat there confounded, and the close-fitted doors held firmly.
43 Not until then did your son summon me out of the hallway,
44 Telemachus; for his father had sent him ahead to call me.
45 Then I found Odysseus standing among the bodies of the slaughtered;
46 and they about him, holding the beaten hard floor all around him,
47 lay there piled on one another; your heart would have softened to see it.
48 Now they all in a mass are lying beside the courtyard doorways,
50 but he meanwhile purges the beautiful house with sulfur,
51 having kindled a great fire; and he sent me to call you.
52 Come now and follow, so joy may come over the dear heart of both of you,
53 since very many afflictions and evils have both of you suffered.
54 Now at long last this ancient desire has reached its fulfillment.
55 He himself has come living to his own hearth and dwelling,
56 and he has found both you and his son still here in the household;
57 and he has punished them all in his own house — the suitors who wronged him.”
58 And in return the circumspect Penelope answered:
59 “Dear nurse, do not yet exalt too greatly in laughter and triumph.
60 You know how welcome his appearing would be in these chambers
61 to all, and most of all to me and the son we begot together.
62 But this tale you tell is not in truth exactly as spoken;
63 rather some one of the immortals killed the glorious suitors,
64 angered by their spirit-grieving outrage and evil actions.
65 For they respected no one at all of the earth-dwelling mortals,
66 neither the bad man nor yet the good, whoever approached them.
67 Therefore through their own recklessness they have suffered. But Odysseus
68 lost his homecoming far from the land of Achaea, and perished.”
69 Thereupon the dear nurse Eurycleia gave her an answer:
70 “My child, out past the fence of your teeth what a word has escaped you,
71 that, though your husband is here by the hearth in the house, you declare
72 he will not come back home? Your heart is forever distrustful.
73 Come then, I will tell you another clear sign for your knowing:
74 the scar, which once a boar struck into him with his white tusk.
75 Washing him, I observed it, and I wished to tell you of it,
76 you yourself; but he seized me, and over my mouth clasped his fingers,
77 and would not let me tell it, for his mind is full of all cunning.
78 But come now and follow; and I will lay down my life as the wager:
79 if I am deceiving you, kill me with the most pitiful ruin.”
80 Thereupon the circumspect Penelope gave her an answer:
81 “Dear nurse, the deathless immortals’ designs are hard for your discerning,
82 to draw them forth, though indeed you are very wise in your thinking.
83 Still, even so, let us go to my son, so that I may behold
84 these suitors lying dead, and the man who has slaughtered them.”
85 So she spoke and came down from her upper chamber; and deeply her spirit
86 wavered, whether apart she should question her dear lord and husband,
87 or should go and stand by and kiss his head and his hands as she held them.
88 But when she had entered, and over the threshold of stone had come stepping,
89 then she sat down facing Odysseus, there in the glow of the firelight,
90 close by the opposite wall; and he over against the tall pillar
91 sat with his eyes to the ground, still waiting to hear whether something
92 his noble wife would say to him, now that her eyes had beheld him.
93 Long she sat there in silence, and wonder came over her spirit;
94 sometimes she looked straight on him and knew him there by his features,
95 sometimes she failed to perceive him, because of the vile clothes upon him.
96 But Telemachus scolded her, and spoke, and called to her plainly:
97 “Mother of mine, hard mother, with pitiless heart in your bosom,
98 why do you keep so apart from my father, and why do you never
99 sit yourself near him and ask him questions and search out the matter?
100 Surely no other woman with heart that endured would have held back
101 thus from her husband, if he after suffering many afflictions
102 came in the twentieth year to the land of his fathers and homeland.
103 But in your breast your heart is always more stubborn than stone is.”
104 To him resourceful Penelope answered and said in reply:
105 “Child of mine, in my breast my spirit is struck into wonder;
106 I cannot say any word to him, I cannot ask him a question,
107 nor can I look in his eyes and face him. But if in earnest
108 this is Odysseus and he has come to his home, then more surely
109 we shall know one another, and better; for tokens are with us,
110 signs that are hidden from others, which only we two have knowledge of.”
111 So she spoke, and he smiled, the great-souled, long-enduring Odysseus,
112 and at once to Telemachus spoke these winged words in answer:
113 “Telemachus, let your mother within these halls make her trial
114 of me; soon enough she will know me more surely and better.
115 Now because I am foul, and I have mean garments around me,
116 therefore she slights me, and still she does not admit I am that man.
117 But let us ourselves consider how this may best be accomplished.
118 Even when one man has killed a single man in the country,
119 one who has not many others behind him to aid and avenge him,
120 still he goes into exile, leaving his kinsmen and homeland.
121 But we have killed the mainstay of all the city, the noblest
122 youths in Ithaca. On this I bid you to turn your mind fully.”
123 In answer to him spoke forth prudent Telemachus wisely:
124 “Look to these things yourself, dear father; for men say your counsel
125 is the best among mortals, and no man born for dying
126 could contend against you in wisdom, nor strive there beside you.
127 We for our part will follow with zeal, and I do not think ever
128 we shall be lacking in courage, so far as our power is present.”
129 Answering him, the much-counseling Odysseus told him:
130 “Therefore I shall declare what seems to me to be the best way.
131 First of all, you must bathe and put tunics on your bodies,
132 and command the serving women within these halls to dress also.
133 But let the godlike singer, holding the clear-toned lyre in his fingers,
134 lead us on in the measures of dancing that gladdens the spirit,
135 so that whoever may hear it outside will suppose it a wedding,
136 whether some passer-by on the road, or a neighbor about us.
137 Let not the wide report of the slaughter of all these suitors
138 spread through the town before we have gone from here and departed
139 out to our farm in the fields, rich-grown with trees; and thereafter
140 we shall consider what gain Olympian Zeus may provide us.”
141 So he spoke, and they heard him with care, and obeyed what he told them.
142 First of all they bathed, and clothed themselves in their tunics;
143 while the women made themselves ready; and the godlike singer took up
144 into his hands the carved lyre, and stirred in them longing
145 for sweet song and for dancing in blameless joy and delightfulness.
146 All around them the great hall echoed aloud to the footsteps
147 of men making merry, and women fair-girdled in dancing.
148 So would a man say, hearing the sound from outside of the dwelling:
149 “Surely at last someone now has wedded the queen much courted.
150 Cruel woman, she had not the heart to keep safe the great household
151 of her own lawful husband until he returned there in person.”
152 So would a man say, but they did not know how matters had happened.
153 Meanwhile within his own house great-hearted Odysseus was tended:
154 Eurynome the housekeeper bathed him and rubbed him with olive,
155 and about him she cast a fair cloak and a tunic beside it.
156 But down from his head abundant beauty poured Athena,
157 making him taller to look on and fuller; and down from his head she
158 sent curling locks to flow, like the bloom of the hyacinth flower.
159 As when a man pours gold in a layer over the silver,
160 whom Hephaestus and Pallas Athena have taught in all craftsman’s
161 art, and he brings to completion his work with grace and with beauty,
162 So she poured grace and beauty around on his head and his shoulders.
163 Out of the bath he stepped, in form like the deathless immortals;
164 back once again he sat on the chair from which he had risen,
165 facing his wife, and spoke and addressed her directly in words:
166 “Strange woman, surely to you beyond all the daughters of women
167 they who possess the halls of Olympus have given a heart unyielding.
168 No other woman would ever have held herself off in her spirit
169 thus from her husband, who, bearing so many afflictions and hardships,
170 came in the twentieth year to the land of his fathers and homeland.
171 Come then, dear nurse, make ready my bed for me, so that I also
172 may lie down by myself, for the heart in her breast is of iron.”
173 Him resourceful Penelope answered, prudent in spirit:
174 “Strange man, I am not exalting myself, nor slighting your person,
175 nor am I over-amazed; I know very well what you looked like
176 when you set out from Ithaca there on the long-oared sea-vessel.
177 Come then, Eurycleia, prepare for him the close-jointed bedstead
178 outside the strong-built chamber, the chamber that he himself fashioned.
179 Set out there for him the close-jointed bed, and upon it cast bedding,
180 fleeces and mantles and rugs that gleam with their polished brightness.”
181 So she spoke, while testing her husband; and great Odysseus,
182 stung into anger, spoke to his wife who knew good and faithful counsel:
183 “Woman, indeed you have spoken a word that pains my heart deeply.
184 Who has set my bed in another place? Hard would the labor
185 be, even for one well skilled, unless some god should come to it
186 and, with ease if he wished, set it down in a different station.
187 None of mankind that lives, no mortal man, not even in prime strength,
188 could with ease shift it away; for a notable sign has been fashioned
189 in that well-crafted bed, and I wrought it, no one beside me.
190 There was a long-leaved olive tree growing inside the enclosure,
191 flourishing still in its prime, and its trunk was thick as a pillar.
192 Round it I built our chamber and labored until I had finished,
193 using close-fitted stones, and I roofed it well high above us,
194 and I set on the doors, fitted close, with panels compacted together.
195 Then after that I lopped off the crown of the long-leaved olive,
196 and from the root upward, trimming the trunk, with the bronze I planed it
197 well and with careful skill, and I brought it straight by the line-rule,
198 shaping a bedpost from it, and I bored it all through with the auger.
199 Starting from this I shaped out the bed, till I brought it to finish,
200 decorating it over with gold and with silver and ivory;
201 across it I stretched out a strap of oxhide, bright crimson.
202 Thus I reveal to you this sign; yet I do not know in my spirit,
203 woman, whether my bed is still fixed and steadfast, or whether
204 some man has moved it elsewhere, by cutting the olive beneath it.”
205 So he spoke, and her knees were unstrung, and her dear heart within her,
206 as she knew once again the sure signs that Odysseus had shown her;
207 then, bursting in tears, she ran straight toward him, and with both arms
208 clasped the neck of Odysseus, kissed his head, and then told him:
209 “Do not be angry with me, Odysseus, since in all matters
210 you were the wisest of men; but the gods assigned us affliction,
211 gods who begrudged that we, abiding beside one another,
212 should rejoice in our youth and arrive at the threshold of old age.
213 Yet do not now be vexed with me for this, nor hold it against me,
214 because when first I saw you I did not embrace you so fondly.
215 Always my heart in the breast that is mine kept trembling within me,
216 lest some mortal man should come and deceive me by speaking;
217 for there are many who plot for themselves base profits and evil.
218 Not even Argive Helen, of Zeus the daughter begotten,
219 would have mingled in love and in bed with a man from a strange land,
220 had she known that the warlike sons of the Achaeans after
221 all would bring her back to her home and her dear native country.
222 It was a god who impelled her to do that shameful action;
223 nor before then did she lay in her heart that ruinous blindness,
224 from which there came to us too the grief that first overtook us.
225 But now, since you have recounted the signs most clear and unmistakable
226 of our own bed, which no other mortal had ever looked on,
227 save only you and I, and one lone handmaid beside us,
228 Actoris, whom my father once gave me when first I came here,
229 she who would guard for us both the doors of our close-built chamber,
230 now you persuade my heart, for all its exceeding stubbornness.”
231 So she spoke, and in him she roused still stronger longing for weeping;
232 and he wept as he held in his arms his dear wise-hearted lady.
233 As when the land appears most welcome to men who are swimming,
234 those men whose stout-built ship Poseidon has shattered on the waters,
235 driving it on with the wind and the towering blast of the billows;
236 few escape from the gray sea and swim till they come to the mainland,
237 and round about their skin the brine has crusted in plenty;
238 gladly they set foot then upon land, having fled their destruction:
239 so to her gaze her husband was welcome as she looked upon him,
240 nor yet from around his neck did she wholly let go her white forearms.
241 And now on their weeping would Dawn have appeared with rose-red fingers,
242 had not the goddess grey-eyed Athena conceived one thing further.
243 At the world's rim she held back the long night, and Dawn in her turn she
244 stayed by the stream of Ocean, gold-throned, nor let her harness
245 her swift-foot horses that carry the light of day to men,
246 Lampos and Phaethon, the colts that draw in the Dawn light.
247 Then to his wife spoke resourceful Odysseus in answer:
248 “Wife, we have not yet come to the end of all our trials,
249 but still there lies before us labor beyond all measure,
250 great and grievous, and all of it I must bring to completion.
251 So did the spirit of Teiresias prophesy to me
252 on that day when I went down within the house of Hades,
253 seeking the homecoming there for my comrades and for myself.
254 But come now, wife, let us go to our bed, so that already
255 laid in sweet sleep's arms we may take our delight in slumber.”
256 In turn to him the prudent Penelope made her answer:
257 “Surely your bed will be ready whenever your own heart
258 wishes it, since the gods have truly accomplished your homecoming
259 to your well-builded house and the land of your fathers.
260 But since you have spoken, and a god has cast in your spirit
261 this, come, tell me the trial; for later also, I think,
262 I shall learn it, and to know it at once is nothing the worse.”
263 Answering her in return spoke resourceful Odysseus:
264 “Strange woman, why now are you urging and pressing me so much
265 to tell it? Yet I shall speak and hide nothing from you.
266 Truly your heart will not take any joy in it; for not even
267 I rejoice, since he ordered me still to go among many
268 towns of men, with a shapely oar held fast in my hands,
269 until at last I come where there are men who know nothing
270 of the sea, and never eat food that is mixed up with salt.
271 Neither indeed do they know of the ships with cheeks painted red,
272 nor of the shapely oars that are wings to the ships upon water.
273 And he declared to me this unmistakable sign, nor will I
274 hide it from you: when another wayfaring man meets me
275 and says that upon my bright shoulder I carry a winnow,
276 then there he ordered me, planting the oar in the earth, to
277 offer fair sacrifices to lord Poseidon the ruler,
278 a ram and a bull, and a boar that over the sows goes mounting,
279 and to go homeward again, and offer sacred hecatombs
280 to the immortal gods who possess the broad heaven above us,
281 all in due order. And death out of the sea for myself too
282 shall come, most gentle indeed, such a death as will slay me
283 when I am worn by sleek old age; and the people around me
284 shall be prosperous. All these things he declared will be fulfilled.”
285 In turn to him the prudent Penelope made her answer:
286 “If indeed the gods will accomplish a better old age for you,
287 then there is hope that after our evils escape will be given.”
288 So they were speaking such things in turn to one another.
289 Meanwhile Eurynome and the nurse were preparing the bedstead
290 with soft woven cloths, while bright torches flared in the chamber.
291 But when in their eager haste they had spread the close-jointed bedstead,
292 the old woman turned and went back to the house to lie down;
293 and for the two Eurynome, attendant within the bedchamber,
294 guided them onward in going to bed, with a torch in her hands.
295 And when she had led them into the chamber she turned back from them.
296 They then gladly approached the old law of their marriage bed.
297 And then Telemachus and the keeper of cattle and swineherd
298 rested their feet from the dancing, and made the women stop also,
299 and themselves lay down to sleep through the shadowy hallways.
300 But when the two had taken their fill of the lovely love union,
301 they took their delight in talk, telling stories to each other:
302 she told what things in the halls she had borne, noble lady,
303 watching the deadly throng of the suitor men in the palace,
304 who for her sake had slaughtered so many cattle and fat sheep,
305 and much wine had been drawn from the jars and poured out in plenty.
306 And god-born Odysseus told of all the grief he had dealt men,
307 and all that he himself had suffered and toiled through in sorrow;
308 all he told, and she took delight as she listened, nor did sleep
309 fall on her eyelids before he had told the whole tale fully.
310 He began first with how he subdued the Cicones, and after
311 came to the rich tilled land of the Lotus-eating people,
312 and all that the Cyclops did, and how he exacted repayment
313 for his brave comrades, whom he devoured and never had pitied,
314 and how he came to Aeolus, who welcomed him freely
315 and sent him onward, yet still it was not his portion to reach
316 his own dear fatherland; but a stormwind seized him again and
317 bore him, heavily groaning, across the fish-filled deep sea.
318 And how he came to Telepylus, city of the Laestrygonians,
319 who destroyed his ships and his well-greaved comrades beside them,
320 all of them; but Odysseus alone slipped clear in the black ship.
321 And Circe's guile he recounted, and all her manifold cunning,
322 and how to the house of Hades, the moldering, down he had journeyed,
323 seeking the counsel there of the shade of Theban Teiresias,
324 in his many-benched ship, and beheld all the rest of his shipmates,
325 and his mother, who bore him and nursed him when he was a little one;
326 and how he heard the voice of the close-thronged song of the Sirens,
327 how he came to the Wandering Rocks and to dread Charybdis,
328 and to Scylla, whom never have men escaped from uninjured;
329 and how his comrades slaughtered the cattle of Helios;
330 and how Zeus of the high thunder struck at the swift ship
331 with his sooty bolt, and the brave companions around him
332 perished all in a mass, while he himself fled the evil fates from him;
333 how he came to Ogygia's isle and the nymph Calypso,
334 who detained him there, desiring that he should be her husband,
335 in her hollow caverns, and fed him, and always was promising
336 she would make him immortal and ageless for all of his lifetime;
337 but she could never persuade the heart in his breast within him;
338 and how after much suffering he came to the land of Phaeacians,
339 who honored him truly, deep in their hearts, as one who was godlike,
340 and sent him forth in a ship to the land of his fathers and homeland,
341 giving bronze and gold in abundance, together with raiment.
342 That then was the latest word he had spoken, when sweet sleep upon him,
343 loosener of limbs, came rushing and loosed the heart's cares within him.
344 But then again the gray-eyed goddess Athena considered
345 one further thing, when now in her heart she supposed that Odysseus
346 had taken his fill of the bed of his wife and likewise of slumber.
347 Straightway out of Ocean she roused the gold-throned early Dawn-goddess,
348 so she might bring light unto mortals; and up rose Odysseus
349 from the soft bed, and to his wife he spoke and enjoined her:
350 “Wife, by now of our many hard trials we both have had surfeit,
351 both of us: you here weeping for my care-burdened homecoming,
352 while upon me Zeus and the other gods with afflictions
353 held me and checked me far from the land of my fathers, though eager.
354 But now, since we both at last have attained the much-longed-for chamber,
355 all the possessions I have, you must tend them within these halls for me;
356 and the sheep that the proud suitors slaughtered and wasted for me,
357 many indeed I myself shall win back by foray and raiding,
358 and the Achaeans will give me the rest, till they fill every sheepfold.
359 But now to the field rich in trees I shall go on my journey,
360 so I may see my noble father, who sorely is grieving within him.
361 And on you, my wife, I lay this charge, though prudent you are fully:
362 straight with the sunrise the rumor will spread among men all together,
363 of the suitor-men whom I killed here within these halls of my household.
364 Go to the upper room and sit there with all your serving women;
365 look upon no one at all, and do not inquire and ask anything.”
366 So he spoke, and over his shoulders he put on the splendid armor,
367 and roused Telemachus, and the neat-herd, and also the swineherd,
368 and bade them all to take in their hands the weapons of battle.
369 They did not disobey him, but clothed themselves round in bronze armor,
370 opened the doors, and went on their way; and Odysseus led them.
371 Now already the light spread over the earth, but Athena
372 wrapped them in night and swiftly led them out of the city.
