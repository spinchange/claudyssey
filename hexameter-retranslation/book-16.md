# The Odyssey — Book 16

*Father and son: the recognition at the swineherd's hut.*

1 There in the hut were Odysseus and noble swineherd,
2 making the morning meal at dawn, after kindling the firelight,
3 and they had sent out the swineherds along with the huddled swineherds;
4 round Telemachus meanwhile the sharp-barking dogs came fawning,
5 neither barked as he approached; and noble Odysseus marked it,
6 noticing how the dogs fawned round, and the sound came of footsteps.
7 Quickly then he addressed Eumaeus with winged words, saying:
8 “Eumaeus, surely now some comrade of yours will be coming,
9 or some other familiar man, since the dogs are not barking,
10 but they are fawning around him; and I hear the thud of feet too.”
11 Not yet had the whole word been spoken, when there stood his dear son
12 there in the doorway; the swineherd sprang up in amazement,
13 and from his hands fell down the vessels with which he was toiling,
14 mixing the flaming wine; and he went straight toward his master,
15 kissed his head, and both of his eyes, so beautiful, radiant,
16 and both of his hands; and a warm tear fell from his eyelids.
17 As when a father with love in his heart folds close his own dear son,
18 coming back from a distant land in the tenth returning year,
19 his only child, late-born, for whom he has suffered great anguish,
20 so then the noble swineherd embraced godlike Telemachus,
21 kissed him all over and round, like one who escaped from destruction,
22 and in his lamentation he spoke winged words and addressed him:
23 “You have come, Telemachus, sweet light of my eyes and my spirit.
24 I had said I would never behold you again, once you sailed to Pylos.
25 Come now, dear child, come inside, so that deep in my spirit
26 I may delight in the sight of you, come from elsewhere returning.
27 Not very often you come to the farm or visit the herdsmen,
28 but you remain in the town; for so, it seems, your heart has preferred it,
29 gazing upon that ruinous throng of the men who are suitors.”
30 And in return wise Telemachus answered and spoke to him:
31 “It shall be so, old father; for your sake I have come hither,
32 so I may see you with my eyes and hear from your speaking
33 whether my mother still remains in the halls, or whether already
34 some other man has married her, while perhaps the bed of Odysseus
35 lies without sleepers and keeps only foul-spun webs of the spider.”
36 In turn the swineherd replied to him, that captain of people:
37 “Surely indeed she remains there, enduring in patient spirit,
38 in your halls; and always for her in unending affliction
39 nights and days alike are consumed while she sheds many tear-drops.”
40 So having spoken, he took from his hand the bronze-shining spearshaft;
41 then he himself went inward, and stepped across the stone threshold.
42 As he came toward the seat, his father Odysseus yielded;
43 but Telemachus checked him from the other side and called to him:
44 “Sit, stranger; elsewhere in our own farmstead we shall find seating.
45 There is a man at hand who will set it in place for you promptly.”
46 So he spoke, and Odysseus, turning back, went and was seated;
47 and underneath him the swineherd strewn green brushwood, and over
48 laid a fleece; and there sat down the dear son of Odysseus.
49 Platters of roasted meats the swineherd set down beside them,
50 portions they had left over before from the meal of the past day;
51 quickly he brought and heaped up the bread in the wicker baskets,
52 and he mixed in the ivy-wood cup the wine sweet as honey;
53 then he himself sat down opposite godlike Odysseus.
54 They reached out their hands to the good things laid ready before them.
55 But when they had cast from themselves the desire for eating and drinking,
56 then Telemachus spoke to the noble swineherd and asked him:
57 “Old father, from where has this stranger of yours come hither to us?
58 How did the sailors bring him to Ithaca? Who did they claim they
59 were? For I do not suppose that he came to this place here on foot.”
60 Answering him in turn you spoke, Eumaeus the swineherd:
61 “Therefore, my child, I shall tell you the whole truth of the matter.
62 He declares that his stock is from spacious dancing-grounded Crete-land,
63 and he says he has wandered through many the cities of mortals,
64 driven astray; for thus has some god spun the thread of it for him.
65 Now having fled from a ship of the Thesprotian fighting men yonder,
66 he has come to my farmstead, and I place him now in your keeping.
67 Do as you will; for he claims and declares he is your suppliant.”
68 And in return wise Telemachus answered and spoke to him:
69 “Eumaeus, surely this word that you spoke is painful and heartfelt.
70 How in the world shall I welcome this stranger into my dwelling?
71 I am still young myself, and not yet do I trust in my hand-strength
72 to defend any man, when another begins with aggression.
73 And in my mother the spirit within her breast is divided,
74 whether to stay here beside me and keep the house in good order,
75 revering her husband’s bed and the voice of the people about her,
76 or else by now to go with whatever Achaean is noblest,
77 one who woos in the halls and offers the greatest bride-gifts.
78 Yet for this stranger, since he has come to your house as a comer,
79 I shall give him a cloak and a tunic, fine beautiful clothing,
80 and I shall give him a two-edged sword and sandals for his footsoles,
81 and I will send him wherever his heart and his spirit may bid him.
82 But if you wish it, keep him here, giving him care at the farmstead;
83 here I will send him clothing and all of the food that is needful
84 for him to eat, so he may not wear you away and your comrades.
85 But I for my part would not let him go there among suitors;
86 far too reckless indeed is the violent outrage they harbor.
87 They would make him their mock, and for me it would be a great heartache.
88 Hard is the task for one man to accomplish a thing against many,
89 though he be strong; for indeed they are men of much greater power.”
90 To him in answer spoke much-enduring noble Odysseus:
91 “Dear friend, since now it is right that I also should make you an answer,
92 truly my own dear heart is gnawed away here as I listen,
93 hearing the things you tell of the suitors’ reckless contrivings
94 there in the halls, against your will, though you are such a man as this one.
95 Tell me whether you bow beneath them by choice, or whether the people
96 hate you throughout the land, as they follow a god’s declaration,
97 or whether you blame your brothers, on whom a man may rely still
98 there in the clash of battle, even should great strife be arising.
99 If only I were so young, with this spirit here in my bosom,
100 or were a son of blameless Odysseus, or else were Odysseus himself;
102 then may some stranger at once strike the head from me and cut it away off,
103 if I did not become a bane to the whole of that company, all of them,
104 going into the hall of Odysseus, son of Laertes.
105 And if they should overcome me alone by the weight of their numbers,
106 I would choose rather, struck down in my own halls, to meet death,
107 than forever to go on seeing these shameful deeds before me,
108 guests being brutally mauled, and the female servants dragged by force
109 shamefully all through the rooms of the beautiful house and the chambers,
110 and the wine being drawn off and wasted, and the food being eaten
111 vainly and without end, for a thing that will never be finished.”
112 In turn clear-headed Telemachus answered and spoke to him thuswise:
113 “Therefore, stranger, I now will tell you the truth of the matter.
114 It is not that the whole of the people hates me and bears me resentment,
115 nor am I blaming brothers, on whom a man may rely still
116 there in the clash of battle, even should great strife be arising.
117 So has the son of Cronos made thin our line and diminished it:
118 Arceisius fathered Laertes, an only son only;
119 and Laertes’ son was Odysseus, born as an only son likewise;
120 and Odysseus fathered only me in the halls, and he had no joy of me.
121 Therefore the house is full of foes beyond all counting against me.
122 For all the nobles who hold lordship over the islands,
123 over Dulichium and Same and wooded Zacynthus,
124 and all who rule as lords throughout rocky Ithaca,
125 all these are wooing my mother and wearing away at the household.
126 She neither refuses the hateful marriage nor can she bring matters
127 to an end; and they keep consuming and wasting away my possessions.
128 Soon enough they will rend me apart and will utterly destroy me.
129 But surely indeed these things lie still on the knees of the deathless.
130 Come, old friend, go quickly and tell discreet Penelope
131 that I am safe for her and have returned from sandy Pylos.
132 I myself will remain in this place; but you must come back here
133 after reporting to her alone; let none of the other Achaeans
134 learn it, since many indeed are devising destruction against me.”
135 Answering him in turn you spoke, Eumaeus the swineherd:
136 “I know it, I understand; you command one who thinks on these matters.
137 But come now, tell me this and recount it exactly and truly:
138 should I go on the same road and carry the message to Laertes,
139 miserable man, who before, though grieving sore for Odysseus,
140 still used to oversee the work with the slaves on his farmland,
141 and to eat and to drink in the house, when the spirit within him should prompt him?
142 But now, from the day that you went with the ship on your way to Pylos,
143 they say that he no longer eats and drinks in the old way,
144 nor looks to the work of the fields, but with moaning and bitter lamenting
145 sits there in mourning, and all of the flesh from his bones is wasting.”
146 In turn clear-headed Telemachus answered and spoke to him thuswise:
147 “More painful indeed; but even so, though we grieve, we will leave him.
148 For if all things whatever could come by the choosing of mortals,
149 first of all we would choose the day of my father’s returning.
150 No, but go with the message, then turn yourself back; do not wander
151 over the fields in search of him; but say to my mother
152 that she should urge the housekeeper handmaid to go with all quickness,
153 secretly; she indeed could carry the news to the old man.”
154 He spoke, and he roused up the swineherd; and into his hands he was taking
155 both of his sandals, and bound them beneath his feet, and went cityward. Neither
156 did it escape Athena that gone from the farm was Eumaeus
157 but she herself came near; and in bodily form she resembled
158 a woman beautiful, tall, and well skilled in glorious handwork.
159 There by the doorway she stood at the hut, made visible to Odysseus.
160 Yet Telemachus did not see her before him nor notice;
161 for not to all men alike do the gods show plainly their presence.
162 But Odysseus saw her, and so did the dogs, yet they kept from barking,
163 but with frightened whimpering shrank to the far side of the farmyard.
164 She with her brows gave a signal; resourceful Odysseus perceived it,
165 and he came out of the hall, passing the high wall of the courtyard,
166 and stood there facing her; then Athena spoke and addressed him:
167 “Son of Laertes, seed of Zeus, Odysseus of many devices,
168 now at this moment speak to your son and no longer conceal it,
169 so that, when you have fixed doom and death for the suitors upon them,
170 you may go to the famed-walled town; and I myself will not linger
171 long away from you both, for my heart is eager for battle.”
172 She spoke, and Athena reached out and touched him with her golden wand.
173 First she cast about his breast a mantle well washed and a tunic,
174 and she increased his stature and brought back the bloom of his young manhood.
175 Once more his skin became dark, and his cheeks filled out firmly,
176 and blue-black grew the beard that encircled the whole of his jawline.
177 She, when she had done this, went back again; but Odysseus
178 went to the hut, and his dear son looked on him in amazement.
179 Fearful, he turned his eyes elsewhere, in dread it might be immortal,
180 and lifting his voice he addressed him in winged words, saying:
181 “Stranger, you seem to me changed from the man that you were aforetime.
182 Other indeed are the clothes that you wear, and your skin is not the same.
183 Surely you are one of the gods who possess the broad heaven above us.
184 Be gracious, that we may present you with welcome offerings and with gifts,
185 fashioned in gold; only spare us, I pray, and take pity upon us.”
186 Then in answer to him spoke the much-enduring noble Odysseus:
187 “I am no god. Why is it that you compare me with the immortals?
188 No, I am your father, for whose sake you suffer in sorrow and groaning,
189 bearing much pain and enduring the violent outrage of menfolk.”
190 So having spoken, he kissed his son; and down from his cheeks, earthward,
191 tears fell, though before he had always held back his weeping.
192 Yet Telemachus, since he had not yet been persuaded within him
193 that this was truly his father, again made answer and said to him:
194 “You are not Odysseus, not my father, but some divinity tricks me,
195 so that hereafter I may lament and grieve in yet greater abundance.
196 For no mortal man could contrive these things by the wit in his own breast,
197 unless some god himself had come here and stood by him in person,
198 and with ease made him young or else made him an old man, at pleasure.
199 For indeed just now you were old and wore most unseemly clothing,
200 but now you are like the gods who possess the broad heaven above us.”
201 Answering him in turn spoke the craft-rich, wise Odysseus:
202 “Telemachus, it is not fitting that you, with your father beside you,
203 should marvel beyond all measure or be so greatly astonished.
204 No other Odysseus will ever come here to you any longer,
205 but I am he, just such as I am, after suffering evils and wandering much,
206 and in the twentieth year I have come to the land of my fathers.
207 Yet this is the work of Athena, the driver and giver of plunder,
208 who made me such as I am, in whatever way she desires, for she can,
209 now making me like a beggar, and now once more in another fashion
210 like to a young man wearing fine garments close over his body.
211 Easy it is for the gods who possess the broad heaven above us
212 both to exalt a mortal man and also to bring him to ruin.”
213 So having spoken, he sat down; and Telemachus, flinging his arms round
214 his noble father, wept aloud, and tears ran down in a torrent.
215 In both of them there arose the desire for lamentation and mourning.
216 They cried aloud and sharply, more ceaselessly even than wild birds do,
217 ospreys or vultures with hooked claws, whose little unfledged nestlings
218 country men have taken away from the nest before they could fly upward.
219 So from beneath their brows they let fall piteous tears in abundance.
220 And the light of the sun would have set on them as they still lamented,
221 had not Telemachus suddenly spoken to his own dear father:
222 “Tell me, dear father, by what ship did sailors bring you hither
223 into Ithaca? Who did they claim to be, what manner of people?
224 I do not think that you could have come here traveling on footpaths.”
225 And in answer to him spoke the much-enduring noble Odysseus:
226 “Therefore indeed, my child, I will tell you the truth of this matter.
227 The Phaeacians brought me, renowned for the speed of their shipping,
228 they who convey other men as well, whoever reaches their people.
229 They were carrying me over the sea in a swift ship while I slumbered,
230 and they set me ashore in Ithaca and gave me splendid possessions,
231 bronze in abundance and gold and woven garments in plenty.
232 These now by the will of the gods lie stored away in the caverns.
233 Now I have come here following counsels and promptings of Athena,
234 so that together we may take thought for the slaughter of foemen.
235 Come then, tell me the number of suitors and count them before me,
236 so that I may know how many they are and what manner of men they are.
237 And after turning it over within my impeccable spirit,
238 I will consider whether we two could stand up against them in combat,
239 all alone without others, or whether we should seek additional helpers.”
240 To him in answer spoke the thoughtful prince Telemachus:
241 “Father, indeed I have always been hearing the greatness of your glory,
242 how your hand is a spearman’s in fight, and your counsel is prudent;
243 but what you have said is too vast; amazement has seized me, for never
244 could two men battle with many, and those men stalwart in prowess.
245 For the suitors are not in exactness ten men, nor twice ten only,
246 but far more numerous; quickly you will know the full tale of their number.
247 Out of Dulichium there are two and fifty young nobles,
248 picked men, and with them six servants attending in order;
249 and out of Same there are four and twenty men altogether,
250 out of Zacynthus there are twenty young Achaean princes,
251 and from Ithaca itself twelve men, all of them finest;
252 with them are Medon the herald, and the godlike singer,
253 and two attendants well skilled in the carving and sharing of portions.
254 If we two should encounter them all while they are within doors,
255 take care lest bitter and grim be the price of your vengeance upon them.
256 No, but do you, if you can devise some champion to aid us,
257 think on one who will stand by us two with a heart that is willing.”
258 Once more in answer spoke the much-enduring great Odysseus:
259 “Therefore I will tell you; do you attend and hearken unto me fully.
260 Consider if Athena with father Zeus beside her in favor
261 will be enough for us two, or I should seek some other defender.”
262 And clear-headed Telemachus answered and spoke to him thuswise:
263 “Noble indeed are those two champions of whom you are speaking,
264 though they sit high up above in the clouds; and over all others
265 they hold sway, over mortal men and the gods who are deathless.”
266 Again in answer spoke the much-enduring great Odysseus:
267 “Those two shall not for long stand apart from the turmoil and onrush
268 of the strong battle, whenever between the suitors and us two
269 in my halls the might of Ares is brought to the test and decision.
270 But for now you must go at the hour when dawn is appearing
271 home to the house, and mix with the suitors so haughty and reckless;
272 later the swineherd himself will conduct me on into the city,
273 like to a beggar in wretchedness, and to an old man in semblance.
274 If they dishonor me there through the house, then let your dear spirit
275 bear it within your breast while I suffer evils before you,
276 even if they drag me by both of my feet through the hall and outdoors,
277 or if they pelt me with missiles; do you, though looking, endure it.
278 Yet indeed you may bid them desist from their folly and madness,
279 speaking to them with gentle words by way of persuasion; but never
280 will they obey you, for now their day of destiny nears them.
281 One thing more will I tell you, and do you lay it in your spirit:
282 when resourceful Athena shall put this design in my inward heart,
283 I will give you a sign with my head, and you then, when you mark it,
284 all the war-gear that lies in the hall and belongs unto Ares,
285 lift and carry away and store in the nook of the high chamber,
286 every bit of it; then if the suitors should ask and should miss them,
287 turn them aside with gentle words whenever they question:
288 “Out of the smoke I removed them, since now they no longer resembled
289 such as Odysseus left when he went away thence unto Troy-land,
290 but are begrimed, to the degree that the breath of the fire has reached them.
291 And besides, the son of Cronos has set this greater thought in me:
292 lest perhaps, when wine has inflamed you, you stir up contention
293 and wound one another, and bring disgrace on the feast and the wooing;
294 for iron itself by its own force draws and entices a man on.”
295 But leave for the two of us only two swords, and two spears likewise,
296 and a pair of oxhide shields as well, for our hands to lay hold of,
297 so that we may rush on them and master them once we have armed us;
298 after that Pallas Athena and Zeus of the counsels will daze them.
299 One thing more will I tell you, and do you lay it in your spirit:
300 if truly you are my son and are sprung of the blood of our kindred,
301 let no one thereafter hear that Odysseus is within the dwelling,
302 let not Laertes know it, nor even the swineherd be told it,
303 nor any one else of the household, nor even Penelope learn it;
304 but let only you and I find out the temper of women,
305 and thereafter we might even test the men among all your servants,
306 learning where one of them honors us two and fears us within him,
307 and who has no care and dishonors you, noble though you are.”
308 And his shining son in return made answer and spoke unto him:
309 “Father, indeed you will know my spirit hereafter, I think so;
310 for no weakness of mind and no folly possesses me, surely.
311 Yet I do not suppose that this plan will turn to advantage
312 for us both; I bid you consider it well in your own spirit.
313 Long will you spend in going about and testing each person,
314 visiting each at his work, while they in the halls without trouble
315 raven and waste our substance with violence, sparing none of it.
316 Yet as for the women, I urge that you learn and distinguish them,
317 both the ones who dishonor you and the guiltless among them.
318 But the men at the farmsteads I for my part would not have us testing,
319 rather let us leave that for later and suffer it afterward,
320 if indeed you truly know some sign from Zeus who carries the aegis.”
321 So in such words they spoke and addressed one another together.
322 Then the well-fashioned ship was now bearing in toward Ithaca,
323 she that brought Telemachus home from Pylos with all his companions.
324 When at last they had come inside the deep-bosomed harbor,
325 then they hauled the black ship up and ashore on the mainland,
326 and the high-hearted servants carried away all their armor,
327 and at once they conveyed the beautiful gifts to Clytius’ dwelling.
328 But they sent on a herald ahead to the house of Odysseus,
329 bringing word to wise-hearted and wary-minded Penelope,
330 that Telemachus stayed in the fields, but ordered the vessel
331 now to sail on to the town, so the queen in her spirit
332 might not fear and let the tender tear run down from her eyelids.
333 There the herald and noble swineherd encountered each other,
334 both on the selfsame errand, to speak to the woman and tell her.
335 But when they came at last to where the godlike king was dwelling,
336 then the herald spoke out in the midst of the serving-women:
337 “Now indeed, my queen, your beloved son has come to you homeward.”
338 But the swineherd, standing near, spoke then to Penelope,
339 all that her own dear son had commanded that he should tell her.
340 But when now he had spoken the whole of the charge entrusted,
341 then he went his way to the swine, leaving court and hall behind him.
342 But the suitors were pained, and in spirit were darkened and downcast,
343 and they came from the hall and passed by the great wall of the courtyard,
344 and there in front of the doors they sat themselves down together.
345 Among them Eurymachus, son of Polybus, first began speaking:
346 “Friends, this is truly a great exploit brought off in presumption,
347 this voyage of Telemachus; and yet we said he would not end it.
348 Come, let us haul down a black ship, whichever is best for us,
349 and let us gather rowers, men of the sea, to man her,
350 so they may swiftly announce to those others to voyage homeward.”
351 He had not yet spoken it all, when Amphinomus saw her,
352 turning about from his place, within the deep-bosomed harbor,
353 as they were both furling the sail and holding the oars in their fingers,
354 With easy laughter he called out and spoke to his comrades:
355 “Let us send out no further message; these men are within doors.
356 Either one of the gods has told them of this, or they themselves saw
357 the ship passing by, but they had no power at all to overtake her.”
358 So he spoke; and rising up they made their way to the sea-shore.
359 Quickly they hauled the black ship up and ashore on the mainland,
360 and the high-hearted servants carried away all their armor.
361 They themselves all together went into the place of assembly,
362 nor did they allow any other to sit there, young men or old ones.
363 Among them Antinous spoke out, the son of Eupeithes:
364 “Oh for shame, how the gods have released this man out of ruin.
365 Day after day our lookouts sat on the windy headlands,
366 ever in relays; and then whenever the sun had gone under,
367 never on shore did we spend the night, but out on the sea
368 sailing our swift ship onward we waited for bright Dawn’s appearing,
369 lying in ambush for brave Telemachus, so we might take him
370 and make an end of his life; but a god brought him home in the meanwhile.
371 Come then, here let us ponder a grim destruction against him,
372 Telemachus, so he may not slip free from our hands; for I tell you,
373 while he is living, I think this affair will not come to fulfillment.
374 For he himself now is skilled in counsel and strong understanding,
375 and the people are no longer wholly inclined to support us.
376 Come then, before he can gather the Achaeans into assembly,
377 let us act; for I think he will not put off the matter much longer,
378 but in his anger will stand in the midst of them all and speak out,
379 how we contrived sheer murder for him, yet could not destroy him.
380 They will not praise us at all when they hear of these evil actions.
381 Let us beware lest they do us some harm and drive us in exile
382 out of our own dear land, and we come to a people of strangers.
383 Rather let us kill him when we catch him out in the farmland,
384 far from the city, or else on the road; then his livelihood, all his
385 substance and goods, we ourselves may divide into portions among us,
386 while to his mother we give the house to possess with the man who shall wed her.
387 But if this plan is displeasing to you, and rather you wish it
388 that he himself should live and hold all his father’s possessions,
389 then let us no more continue assembling here in abundance,
390 wasting his rich stores away, but let each man go from his own house
391 and seek her hand with gifts of wooing and costly bride-offerings;
392 then she may marry the one who gives most and comes by fate to her.”
393 So he spoke, and they all became utterly silent in stillness.
394 Amphinomus stood up to address them and answer among them,
395 glorious son of Nisus, descended from lord Aretias,
396 who from Dulichium rich in wheat and abundant in meadow
397 led the suitors, and far more than all of the rest he was pleasing
398 to Penelope, by his words, for he had a well-meaning spirit.
399 Thinking good for them all, he rose and spoke in their gathering:
400 “Friends, for my part I would not be willing to slaughter
401 brave Telemachus; dreadful it is to kill the blood of a ruler.
402 Slaying him; yet first let us ask for the counsels of heaven.
403 If the decrees of great Zeus give praise and assent to the killing,
404 I myself will kill him, and urge all the others to help me;
405 but if the gods turn us back, I command you to stop.”
406 So he spoke, Amphinomus, and his counsel was pleasing among them.
407 Then at once they arose and went to the house of Odysseus,
408 and when they had entered, they sat themselves down on chairs well-polished.
409 In turn resourceful Penelope pondered another intention:
410 to appear to the suitors, those men of arrogant outrage.
411 For she had heard in the halls of the murder designed for her dear son;
412 for the herald Medon had told her, who heard their devising.
413 Forth she went on her way to the hall with her women attendants.
414 But when among the suitors had come the godlike queen of women,
415 she stood by the pillar that held the close-fitted roof of the chamber,
416 lifting her shining veil and holding it there by her fair cheeks,
417 and she rebuked Antinous, spoke, and addressed him directly:
418 “Antinous, full of outrage, deviser of evil and cunning,
419 they say in the land of Ithaca, there with the men of your own age,
420 you are the best in counsel and speech; but you were not such a man.
421 Reckless one, why are you weaving death and destruction for Telemachus,
422 and why do you not care for suppliants, over whom lord Zeus
423 stands as witness? It is not holy to weave evil against one another.
424 Do you not know when your own father came here as a fugitive,
425 in fear of the people? For so bitterly angry were they growing,
426 because he had joined the Taphian sea-raiders in harrying
427 the Thesprotians, men who had long been allies and friends to us.
428 Him they were eager to kill and to tear out the heart in his bosom,
429 and to devour all his abundant and heart-delighting substance;
430 but Odysseus restrained them and held them back in their eagerness.
431 Now you dishonor his house as you eat it away, and you woo his wife too;
432 you are killing his son, and you grieve me exceedingly deeply.
433 Therefore I bid you stop, and command all the rest to stop likewise.”
434 In answer Eurymachus, son of Polybus, spoke before her:
435 “Daughter of Icarius, wise Penelope, take courage.
436 Do not let these things be matters of care in your spirit.
437 There is no such man, nor will there be, nor can one be born ever,
438 who will lay violent hands on your son Telemachus
439 while I am living yet and upon the earth still am gazing.
440 For thus I will speak, and surely the thing will be brought to fulfillment:
441 quickly his black blood would flow all around my spear-shaft,
442 that man’s; for in truth city-sacking Odysseus often
443 sat me upon his knees and placed roast meat in my own hands,
444 and held out to me the ruddy wine for my drinking.
445 Therefore Telemachus is by far the dearest to me of all mortals,
446 and I tell him he need not fear death at the hands of the suitors;
447 but from the gods there is no escape for any man living.”

452 At evening the noble swineherd came back to Odysseus
453 and his son; and eagerly they were making the evening meal ready,
454 after they sacrificed a yearling pig; but Athena beside them,
455 standing close by Odysseus, the son of Laertes, there present,
456 struck him with her wand and turned him again to an old man,
457 and clothed his body around with wretched garments, lest the swineherd
458 seeing him there face to face should know who he was and then hasten
459 to bring the news to prudent Penelope, not keeping it hidden.
460 Telemachus was the first of the two to address him in speaking:
461 “You have come, noble Eumaeus. What word is abroad through the city?
462 Are the proud suitors already at home, come back to the palace,
463 from their ambush, or are they still watching my road as I travel?”
464 Answering him in turn you spoke, Eumaeus the swineherd:
465 “I had no care to inquire of these things or ask as I traveled
466 down through the town; my spirit urged me as quickly as possible
467 first to deliver the message, then to turn and come back here again.
468 And a swift messenger met me, sent on before by your comrades,
469 a herald, who indeed was the first to speak to your mother.
470 Yet this much also I know, for I saw it myself with my own eyes.
471 Already above the city, where stands the hill that is Hermes',
472 I was walking along, when I saw a swift ship that was descending
473 into our harbor; and many men were aboard within her,
474 and she was laden with shields and spears with points at both edges.
475 I supposed they were those men, but I do not know it for certain.”
476 So he spoke, and there smiled the sacred strength of Telemachus,
477 glancing toward his father with his eyes, while avoiding the swineherd.
478 But when they had ceased from their labor and made all the meal ready,
479 then they feasted, and none of their hearts lacked the equal portion.
480 But when they had put from them the desire for drinking and eating,
481 then they remembered the bed, and accepted the gift of sleep.
