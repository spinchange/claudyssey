# The Odyssey — Book XX

*The last night; omens and the suitors’ final feast.*

1 Godlike Odysseus lay down to sleep in the forecourt.
2 Under himself he spread an untanned hide from an ox there,
3 then many fleeces of sheep that Achaean men had slaughtered;
4 Eurynome cast a mantle upon him when he had lain down.
5 There Odysseus lay, thinking evil against all the suitors,
6 wakeful; and out of the hall came women who formerly often
7 slept with the suitors, departing and going away from the household,
8 bringing each other laughter and merriment as they went onward.
9 Then the heart in the breast of Odysseus stirred with commotion.
10 Many things he pondered deep in his heart and his spirit:
11 whether to leap on them now and deal out death to each woman,
12 or to permit them one final time to sleep with the suitors,
13 those overbearing men; and the heart deep within him was barking.
14 Just as a mother dog stands over her tender young puppies,
15 barking when she does not know a man and is eager to fight him,
16 so did the heart in him bark, indignant at deeds that were evil.
17 Striking his breast, he rebuked his heart and addressed it directly:
18 “Bear it, my heart! You endured an even more doglike horror,
19 on the day when the Cyclops, ungovernable in his fury,
20 ate my powerful comrades; but you endured till my cunning
21 led you away from the cave when you thought you surely would perish.”
22 So he spoke in rebuke to the dear heart held in his bosom.
23 Patiently then his heart remained in obedience firmly,
24 steadfast; but he himself kept turning this way and that way.
25 Just as a man by the blaze of a great fire turns a paunch over,
26 filled up with fat and blood, now this way and now the other,
27 eager to have it roasted exceedingly quickly and fully,
28 so he kept turning himself now this way and now the other,
29 pondering how he might lay his hands on the shameless suitors,
30 one man against the many. Athena came close beside him,
31 down from the heavens, assuming the bodily shape of a woman.
32 Over his head she stood and addressed him directly with these words:
33 “Why are you wakeful again, most wretched of all mortal people?
34 Here is your house; your wife is here in the house that is yours now,
35 also a son such as anyone prays that his own son may be here.”
36 Then the resourceful lord Odysseus answered the goddess:
37 “Yes indeed, goddess, all you have spoken is right and in order,
38 yet this one thing in the depths of my mind and spirit I ponder:
39 how I shall lay my hands on the company of shameless suitors,
40 one man against them, while they are always together within here.
41 Further, I ponder a thing still greater than this in my spirit:
42 though I should kill them all by the will of Zeus and your own will,
43 where should I flee from vengeance? I ask you to think and advise me.”
44 Then the bright-eyed goddess Athena spoke to him, saying:
45 “Obstinate man, another will trust an inferior comrade,
46 one who is mortal and does not possess so many devices;
47 I am a goddess, and I protect you always and wholly
48 through every labor of yours. I shall tell you the thing openly:
49 even if fifty battalions of mortal human people
50 ringed us around, all burning to kill us in battle,
51 still you would drive away their cattle and sturdy sheep with you.
52 Let sleep take you now; it is wearying work to keep watch through
53 all of the night while awake. You will soon emerge from your troubles.”
54 So she spoke, and she poured sweet sleep on his closing eyelids;
55 then the bright goddess returned once more to lofty Olympus.
56 Sleep seized hold of Odysseus, loosening cares from his spirit,
57 limb-relaxing; but then his good and discerning wife wakened.
58 Sitting upright on the soft bed, she began to weep there.
59 When she had fully sated her heart with weeping and sorrow,
60 first of all the glorious woman prayed to Artemis, saying:
61 “Artemis, sovereign goddess and daughter of Zeus, would that now you
62 shot an arrow into my breast and took away life from me straightway,
63 or would that some tempest seized me and carried me off in a moment,
64 sweeping me onward along the dark paths traveled by cloudbanks,
65 then cast me down at the outflow of backward-flowing Ocean,
66 as the storm-winds swept away the daughters of Pandareus once.
67 All of their parents the gods destroyed, and the girls were left behind,
68 orphaned within their halls; but glorious Aphrodite fed them
69 cheese and honey exceeding sweet and wine that was pleasant.
70 Hera gave them beauty and wisdom beyond all other
71 women; chaste Artemis gave them height, and Athena
72 taught them the way to accomplish illustrious work with their fingers.
73 When divine Aphrodite ascended the height of Olympus,
74 meaning to ask for the girls the completion of flourishing marriage—
75 going to Zeus who delights in thunder, for he clearly knows all things,
76 both the allotment and lack of allotment for death-fated mortals—
77 meanwhile Harpies snatched those girls from the earth and swept them
78 off, then gave them to serve the loathsome avenging Furies.
79 So may the gods who possess Olympus destroy me completely,
80 or may Artemis strike me, the goddess with beautiful tresses,
81 so I may go under hateful earth while gazing at my lord,
82 never delighting the heart of a husband who is his inferior.
83 Yet that evil is one a person still might endure, when a woman
84 weeps through the days, though the heart in her breast is crowded with sorrow,
85 then sleep holds her at night—for it makes her forget all her troubles,
86 evils as well as blessings, whenever it closes her eyelids—
87 but an evil spirit has driven bad dreams to me also.
88 This very night one lay by my side who looked like Odysseus,
89 just as he was when he went with the army; the heart in me felt joy,
90 since I believed it was not a dream, but waking reality.”
91 So she spoke, and at once gold-throned Dawn came into the heavens.
92 Godlike Odysseus heard the voice of the woman lamenting.
93 Then he pondered, and deep in his heart it appeared that she knew him
94 now and was standing beside his head as he lay in the forecourt.
95 Gathering up the mantle and fleeces on which he had rested,
96 into the hall he carried and laid them upon a chair; then the oxhide
97 he carried outdoors and laid it down, then prayed with hands lifted:
98 “Father Zeus, if willingly you gods led me through dry land
99 and through water home to my country, although you afflicted me sorely,
100 let one of the people now waking speak some word for my hearing
101 inside the house, and let another sign from Zeus appear outside.”
102 So he spoke in prayer, and Zeus of the counsels heard him.
103 Straightway he thundered out of the radiant height of Olympus,
104 high in the clouds; and godlike Odysseus felt joy within him.
105 Then from the house a woman who ground at the mill spoke the omen,
106 close by the place where the mills of the shepherd of peoples were standing.
107 There twelve women together were always laboring round them,
108 grinding barley and wheat flour, marrow and strength of the people.
109 All of the others slept, having ground their assigned grain,
110 but one had not yet finished; she was the weakest among them.
111 Stopping the millstone, she uttered a word as a sign for her master:
112 “Father Zeus, who rules over gods and mortal people,
113 greatly you thundered just now from the starry sky in the heavens,
114 though there is not any cloud; you are showing a sign to someone.
115 Bring to fulfillment the word that I, poor woman, shall utter:
116 let the suitors this day take their last and final
117 welcome feast in the halls of Odysseus, godlike in counsel.
118 They with heart-aching labor have loosened the knees from beneath me,
119 grinding their flour; may this be the final meal they consume here.”
120 So she spoke. Odysseus felt joy at the cry of good omen,
121 also the thunder of Zeus, and he thought he would punish the guilty.
122 Other servant women throughout Odysseus’ beautiful household
123 gathered together and kindled untiring fire on the hearthstone.
124 Then Telemachus rose from his bed, that man like a god there,
125 putting his clothes on; he slung the sharp sword around his shoulder,
126 then underneath his shining feet bound beautiful sandals,
127 grasped the powerful spear that was sharpened with pointed bronze on it.
128 Going to stand at the threshold, he spoke to Eurycleia:
129 “Nurse, dear mother, have you given our guest in the household
130 honor in bedding and food, or does he lie there uncared for?
131 Such is my mother, though she is discerning: blindly and rashly
132 she honors one of the men among mortals at random,
133 though he is worse, and dishonors the better and sends him away.”
134 Circumspect Eurycleia gave him an answer directly:
135 “Child, you should not now blame her when she is guiltless of wrongdoing.
136 Sitting, he drank all the wine he desired for his portion;
137 bread he said he no longer was hungry to eat when she asked him.
138 But when he thought of his bed and remembered the hour of his sleeping,
139 she commanded the women to spread out a bed for the stranger.
140 Yet, like one who was utterly wretched and cursed by his fortune,
141 he did not wish to sleep in a bed with blankets upon it,
142 but on an untanned oxhide and on the fleeces of sheep there
143 slept in the forecourt; we ourselves cast a mantle upon him.”
144 So she spoke. Telemachus went out through the hall of the household,
145 holding his spear, and two swift-footed dogs went beside him.
146 Off he went to assembly among the bronze-greaved Achaeans.
147 Then to the serving women the glorious lady gave orders,
148 Eurycleia, the daughter of Ops, Peisenor’s offspring:
149 “Hurry now, some of you diligently sweep through the household,
150 sprinkle it, and onto well-fashioned chairs lay the carpets,
151 purple in color; let others wipe all the tables with sponges,
152 clean all the mixing bowls and the double-handled goblets;
153 then let the others go out after water for us;
154 go to the fountain and hurry to carry it back here.
155 Suitors will not remain very long away from the palace,
156 but will return very early, since today is a feast day for all men.”
157 So she spoke; they heard her well and obeyed her commandment.
158 Twenty of them went off to the dark-water fountain;
159 others remained in the house and skillfully labored within it.
160 Then the Achaean serving men entered; some of them quickly
161 and expertly split all the wood, while the women
162 came from the fountain. The swineherd came in behind them,
163 driving three fattened pigs, the best of the entire herd there.
164 These he allowed to feed around the beautiful courtyard,
165 then he addressed Odysseus gently with mild words:
166 “Stranger, do these Achaeans regard you with any more honor,
167 or do they still dishonor you here in the halls as they did then?”
168 Then the resourceful lord Odysseus answered Eumaeus:
169 “Would that the gods, Eumaeus, avenged the shameful abuses
170 these men inflict as they recklessly plan such arrogant evils,
171 here in another man’s house, without any portion of shame left.”
172 Thus with words such as these they spoke to each other.
173 Close to the two came Melanthius now, the goatherd among them,
174 driving the goats that surpassed all others among the goat-herds
175 for the suitors’ dinner; two herdsmen followed behind him.
176 These he tethered below the portico loud with its echo,
177 then he addressed Odysseus there with these taunting insults:
178 “Stranger, will you even now continue to trouble the household,
179 begging the men, and will you not take yourself out of the doorway?
180 Surely I do not think we two will part from each other
181 until we have tested our hands, since your begging is not in good order;
182 other Achaean banquets are held where you might be begging.”
183 So he spoke, but the resourceful lord Odysseus made him no answer.
184 Silently he shook his head and was brooding on evils.
185 Third in succession came Philoetius, a leader among men,
186 bringing a barren cow for the suitors and goats that were fattened.
187 Ferrymen had carried them over, the men who convey other
188 people across as well, whoever may come and approach them.
189 Firmly he tethered them all beneath the portico loud-echoing,
190 then he stood by the swineherd nearby and asked him a question:
191 “Who is the guest, swineherd, who recently came to our household?
192 Out of what race of men does he claim he descends from?
193 Where are the line of his family and the fields of his homeland?
194 Luckless man! In the shape of his body he looks like a ruler.
195 But the gods bring wandering people down under misery,
196 even when they have spun out sorrow and woe for kings among people.”
197 So he spoke and stood by, clasping his right hand in greeting,
198 then he addressed Odysseus with the following winged words:
199 “Hail to you, stranger and father! May prosperity reach you
200 later, though now you are held and surrounded by many afflictions.
201 Father Zeus, no other god is more cruel than you are;
202 you have no pity on men, though you were yourself their begetter,
203 but entangle them deeply in wretchedness and in pitiful sorrow.
204 Sweat broke over me seeing you; tears filled up both of my eyes here,
205 as I remembered Odysseus, since I imagine that he too
206 wanders among human people with rags of this kind on his body,
207 if he is still alive and beholds the brightness of sunlight.
208 But if he now has died and is in the halls of lord Hades,
209 sorrow for blameless Odysseus, who when I was still but a youngster
210 set me in charge of his cattle among the people of Cephallenes!
211 Now they increase beyond measure; no other mortal
212 ever could make broad-browed cattle spring up in such number.
213 Other men order me now to bring these cattle before them,
214 for their own feasting; they pay no heed to the son in the household,
215 nor do they tremble at godly vengeance, for they are eager
216 now to divide the goods of a master long absent and missing.
217 Deep in the breast of my body the heart keeps wheeling and turning
218 many things over. It is a terrible wrong while the son lives
219 here to go to another people and country, taking these cattle
220 among foreign men; yet worse still is it here to remain,
221 sitting beside another man’s cattle and suffering sorrows.
222 Long before now I would have reached some other powerful monarch,
223 fleeing this place, since these things are no longer endurable;
224 but I still think of the luckless man and hope he may come from
225 somewhere and scatter the suitors throughout his halls and his household.”
226 Then the resourceful lord Odysseus answered the cowherd:
227 “Cowherd, you clearly resemble no witless or wicked man,
228 and I see for myself that wisdom has entered your spirit,
229 therefore I tell you this and shall swear with a powerful oath now:
230 Zeus be my witness first of the gods, and this table of strangers,
231 also the hearth of blameless Odysseus, to which I have come here:
232 while you are present Odysseus shall come to his home here;
233 you with your own eyes shall behold, if you desire it,
234 all of the suitors who lord it here being slaughtered.”
235 Then in reply spoke the man who attended and herded the cattle:
236 “Would that the son of Cronus, old stranger, fulfill what you tell me!
237 Then you would know what strength I have and what hands go beside it.”
238 Likewise Eumaeus prayed in his turn to all of the high gods,
239 asking that wise Odysseus come to his own home again there.
240 Thus with words such as these they spoke to each other.
241 Meanwhile suitors were plotting Telemachus’ death and destruction;
242 but to the left of the men a bird came flying beside them,
243 high-flying eagle holding a trembling dove in its talons.
244 Then Amphinomus spoke and addressed the other men, saying:
245 “Friends, this plan will not come together well for our purpose,
246 killing Telemachus; let us instead remember the banquet.”
247 So Amphinomus spoke, and the words were pleasing among them.
248 Coming inside the house of Odysseus, godlike in counsel,
249 first they laid their mantles down on the couches and armchairs;
250 then they slaughtered great sheep and goats exceedingly fat there,
251 slaughtered the fattened pigs and an ox from the herd for the banquet.
252 Roasting the entrails, they shared them round; and inside the mixing
253 bowls they blended wine, while the swineherd passed out the goblets.
254 Bread Philoetius served to the men, a leader among men,
255 using the handsome baskets; Melanthius poured them the wine there.
256 All of them reached out their hands to the good things lying before them.
257 Then Telemachus, working with craft, seated lord Odysseus
258 well inside the well-built hall, beside the threshold of stone there,
259 setting a wretched stool for his seat and a little table.
260 There he set portions of entrails and poured out wine for Odysseus,
261 using a golden cup, then he spoke to him there directly:
262 “Sit in this place for the present among these men and drink wine.
263 I shall protect you myself from their taunting words and their hands—
264 from all of the suitors; this house does not belong to the people,
265 but to Odysseus, who gained and acquired it for me here.
266 You there, suitors, restrain every urge toward insult and striking,
267 so that no quarrel or strife between us may rise in the household.”
268 So he spoke; but all of the suitors bit down on their own lips,
269 marveling at Telemachus, who spoke out so boldly.
270 Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, addressed them and told them:
271 “Hard though it is, Achaeans, let us accept what he tells us—
272 Telemachus; he has spoken to us with a serious menace.
273 Zeus son of Cronus has not allowed us, or we would already
274 silence him here in the halls, though he is a clear-voiced speaker.”
275 So Antinous spoke, but Telemachus paid no heed to his speaking.
276 Heralds were leading the sacred hecatomb of the high gods
277 on through the city; the long-haired Achaean men were assembling
278 under the shadowy grove of far-striking lord Apollo.
279 When they had roasted the outer meat and drawn it from fire,
280 sharing portions, they feasted upon the illustrious banquet.
281 Those who were serving placed a portion beside Odysseus,
282 equal to that which each of the other men was allotted,
283 as Telemachus ordered, the dear son born to divine Odysseus.
284 Yet Athena did not allow the arrogant suitors
285 wholly to hold from heart-aching outrage, so that still more
286 grief might sink into the heart of Laertes’ son Odysseus.
287 One of the suitors was there who was versed in lawless behavior,
288 named Ctesippus, whose house and possessions were in Same.
289 Trusting the wealth of his property, great beyond all calculation,
290 he sought the wife of Odysseus, long from the country.
291 Then he addressed the overbearing suitors and told them:
292 “Listen to me, proud suitors, so I may tell you a word now.
293 Long has the stranger possessed his portion, as is fitting;
294 equal it is, for it is neither handsome nor right to deprive
295 a guest of Telemachus, whoever may come to this household.
296 Come now, I too shall give him a gift of friendship, so he may
297 give it himself as a prize to the woman who pours out his bathwater,
298 or to another servant who works in the house of Odysseus.”
299 So he spoke, and he hurled an ox’s foot with his powerful broad hand,
300 taking it up from the basket where it lay; but Odysseus dodged it,
301 gently inclining his head; within his spirit he smiled then,
302 grim and sardonic; the ox-foot struck the well-fashioned wall there.
303 Then Telemachus harshly rebuked Ctesippus, saying:
304 “Truly this has turned out for the good of your spirit, Ctesippus:
305 you did not hit the stranger, because he avoided the missile.
306 Otherwise I would have struck through the middle of you with a sharp spear,
307 and your father would labor at burial instead of your marriage;
308 here. Therefore no one display in my house any shameful behavior.
309 I now understand and know each thing that is happening,
310 both what is good and the worse, though before now I was a child here.
311 Yet we have nevertheless endured these things as we watched them,
312 sheep being slaughtered, wine being drunk, and the bread being eaten,
313 since it is hard for a single man to restrain the many.
314 Come now, no longer do evils to me out of hostile intention.
315 But if you are eager already to kill me myself with the bronze blade,
316 even that I would prefer; it would be much better for me to
317 die than forever to witness these shameful deeds in my household,
318 strangers being abused and women who serve in the palace
319 shamefully dragged this way and that through the beautiful chambers.”
320 So he spoke; and all of them stayed completely silent.
321 Late at last Agelaus, the son of Damastor, addressed them:
322 “Friends, no man should become angry and use words of defiance,
323 answering what has been spoken with justice and right by another.
324 Do not abuse this stranger, nor anyone else of the servants
325 who labor here in the house of Odysseus, godlike in counsel.
326 Yet to Telemachus and his mother I would give gentle
327 counsel, if it should please both hearts in the breasts of the two there.
328 While the heart in you both still hoped and believed that Odysseus,
329 wise in his counsels, would come to his own home and household,
330 no one could rightly resent that you waited and held back the suitors
331 here in the house, since that was the better and more profitable
332 course if Odysseus came and returned to his own halls and household.
333 Now it is clear that he no longer has any homecoming.
334 Come then, sit by your mother and tell her the whole thing directly:
335 marry whichever man is the best and gives the most presents,
336 so you may joyfully tend and possess all the wealth of your fathers,
337 eating and drinking, while she tends the home of another.”
338 Then wise-minded Telemachus gave him an answer directly:
339 “No, by Zeus, Agelaus, and by the woes of my father,
340 who somewhere far from Ithaca either has died or is wandering,
341 I do not delay the marriage of my mother, but urge her
342 marry whichever she wills, and I offer her limitless presents.
343 Yet I am ashamed to drive her unwillingly out of the household,
344 using coercive speech. May no god bring that to fulfillment.”
345 So Telemachus spoke. Then Pallas Athena awakened
346 unquenchable laughter among all the suitors and twisted their thinking.
347 Now they were laughing with jaws that seemed no longer their own there,
348 eating meat that was smeared with blood; and both of their eyes were
349 filling with tears, while the hearts in their breasts were foreboding lament.
350 Then godlike Theoclymenus also addressed them and told them:
351 “Wretched men, what is this evil you suffer? Night has enshrouded
352 all of your heads and your faces and knees underneath you.
353 Wailing is set all ablaze, and your cheeks are covered with teardrops;
354 walls and the beautiful crossbeams all have been sprinkled with blood now.
355 Phantoms are filling the forecourt, and phantoms are filling the courtyard,
356 hastening under the gloom toward Erebus; meanwhile the sunlight
357 has vanished out of the heavens, and evil mist overwhelms you.”
358 So he spoke; but all of the suitors laughed at him sweetly.
359 Then Eurymachus, the son of Polybus, started to tell them:
360 “Mad is the stranger who recently came here out of a far land.
361 Hurry, young men, and send him away from the house and outdoors;
362 let him go to assembly, since here he imagines the nighttime.”
363 Then godlike Theoclymenus gave him an answer directly:
364 “Eurymachus, I do not command you to give me an escort.
365 Eyes of my own I possess, and ears, and two feet beneath me,
366 also a mind in my breast that is fashioned not badly or basely.
367 Using them I shall go outside, for I see an approaching
368 evil upon you that none shall escape or avoid in the household,
369 none of the suitors who here in the house of divine Odysseus
370 outrage men and keep devising your reckless offenses.”
371 So having spoken, he left the beautifully settled dwelling,
372 going to lord Piraeus, who gladly received him and welcomed.
373 All of the suitors were looking at one another and trying
374 to provoke Telemachus, laughing because of his strangers.
375 Thus one spoke from among the young men with arrogant spirits:
376 “No one is worse at hosting his guests than you, Telemachus!
377 Look at this wandering beggar you keep who is greedy for handouts,
378 needing his bread and wine but possessing no knowledge of labor,
379 nothing of strength, but only a burden upon the plowland.
380 Now yet another arose and decided to make his predictions.
381 But if you should obey me in anything, it would be much better:
382 let us throw both of these guests aboard a many-benched vessel,
383 send them away to the Sicels, where they could fetch you a good price.”
384 So the suitors spoke, but Telemachus paid no heed to their speeches.
385 Silently he kept looking toward his father, all the while
386 waiting for when he would lay his hands on the shameless suitors.
387 She had set down a beautiful chair directly opposite them,
388 daughter of lord Icarius, circumspect Penelope,
389 and in the hall heard every separate word of the men there.
390 Laughing, they had prepared their dinner together before her,
391 pleasant and much desired, for they sacrificed beasts in abundance;
392 yet no other supper could ever become more joyless
393 than the one soon a goddess and powerful man would provide them,
394 for they were first to devise and inflict their shameful behavior.
