# The Odyssey — Book XXIV

*The shades of the suitors; Laertes; peace in Ithaca.*

1 Hermes the lord of Kyllene summoned the souls from the bodies
2 of the dead suitors; he carried his wand in both hands there,
3 beautiful golden wand, with which he can charm human eyelids,
4 those that he chooses, and waken again those who are sleeping.
5 Stirring them forth with the wand, he led; they followed him, shrilling.
6 Just as the bats in the innermost depth of a cavern uncanny
7 flutter and shrill when one of their number falls loose from the others,
8 down from the chain on the rock, while they cling to each other in rows,
9 so they went shrilling together; before them proceeded their leader,
10 Hermes the Helper, who led them through dank and moldering pathways.
11 Past the Ocean River they went, past the White Rock gleaming,
12 past the Gates of the Sun and the shadowy country of dream-shapes;
13 onward they traveled, and soon reached the meadow of asphodel flowers,
14 where the souls have their dwelling, the phantoms of men who have labored.
15 There they encountered the spirit of Achilles, the son of Peleus,
16 Patroclus too, and the blameless spirit of brave Antilochus,
17 Ajax as well, who surpassed in beauty and bodily stature
18 all of the other Danaans except the peerless Peleides.
19 These were assembled around him; and close to them, drawing nearer,
20 came the spirit of Agamemnon, the son of Atreus,
21 grieving; around him gathered the rest of the spirits of warriors
22 slain with him in the house of Aigisthus, meeting their fate there.
23 First the spirit of Peleus’ son addressed him, saying:
24 “Son of Atreus, we thought that Zeus who rejoices in thunder
25 favored you all of your days above other men among heroes,
26 since you commanded so many powerful men as their ruler
27 there in the land of the Trojans, where we Achaians suffered.
28 Yet it was fated that you too early would meet with the deadly
29 doom that nobody born into life has ever avoided.
30 If only, still possessing the honor with which you were sovereign,
31 you had met death and accomplished your fate in the land of the Trojans!
32 Then the whole Achaian host would have raised up a tomb for your body,
33 and you would have won for your son great glory in ages to follow.
34 Now it was fated that you should be caught by a pitiful death-stroke.”
35 Then the spirit of Atreus’ son in answer addressed him:
36 “Blessed Achilles, Peleus’ son, like the gods in your beauty!
37 You died far from Argos in Troy; all around you the greatest
38 sons of the Trojans and sons of Achaians were falling in battle,
39 fighting to win you; and you lay vast in the whirlwind of dust there,
40 vast in your greatness, forgetting your chariot-driving forever.
41 All that day we continued to fight; we never would wholly
42 cease from the battle, had Zeus not stopped us at last with a tempest.
43 Then, when we carried you out of the battle and back to the vessels,
44 there on a bed we laid you, cleansing your beautiful body
45 both with warm water and oil; all around you, in streams without number,
46 Danaans poured burning tears and cut off the locks of their hair.
47 Out of the sea came your mother with all the immortal sea-maidens,
48 hearing the news; and a cry arose on the face of the waters,
49 strange and unearthly, and trembling seized every Achaian warrior.
50 They would have leapt to their feet and run to the hollow vessels,
51 had not a man restrained them whose ancient knowledge was ample—
52 Nestor, whose counsel had always before proved best among mortals.
53 Kindly disposed toward all, he stood and addressed the assembly:
54 ‘Hold, Argives! Do not flee, young sons of Achaians!
55 Here from the sea comes his mother with all the immortal sea-maidens,
56 coming to meet her son who has died and mourn for his passing.’
57 So he spoke, and the great-hearted sons of Achaians ceased fleeing.
58 Round you the daughters of Nereus, ancient lord of the sea, stood
59 wailing in sorrow and wrapped your body in deathless garments.
60 All of the nine Muses sang in response with beautiful voices,
61 chanting the dirge; you would not have seen one Argive tearless,
62 such was the piercing lamentation the clear-voiced Muse awakened.
63 Seventeen nights and an equal number of days passed over you;
64 deathless gods and the mortal-born race of humanity wept there;
65 then on the eighteenth day we surrendered your body to fire,
66 slaughtering round you many fat sheep and spiral-horned cattle.
67 You were consumed in the garments of gods, in quantities of oil
68 and in the sweetness of honey; and many Achaian heroes,
69 both in their armor, went charging around your burning pyre there,
70 some upon foot and others on horseback; the clamor was mighty.
71 Then, when the flame of Hephaistos at last had completed its labor,
72 early at dawn we gathered your whitened bones, Achilles,
73 washed them in unmixed wine and oil; and your mother provided
74 one golden two-handled jar, the glorious gift of Dionysus—
75 so she declared—and the work of the widely renowned Hephaistos.
76 There your white bones lie now, illustrious lord Achilles,
77 mingled with those of Patroclus, the dead son of Menoitios;
78 separate lie Antilochus’ bones, whom you honored above all
79 all of your other companions, after Patroclus himself had fallen.
80 Then all around those bones stood a tomb, magnificent, flawless,
81 heaped by the sacred Achaian host of spear-bearing fighters,
82 out on a headland projecting above broad Hellespont waters,
83 so it would shine from afar on the sea for the eyes of all mortals,
84 both for the people now born and the people arising hereafter.
85 Then your mother entreated the gods and set splendid prizes
86 there in the midst of the games for the greatest Achaian champions.
87 Many a funeral you have attended for heroes already,
88 when, upon the death and the passing of some great king among mortals,
89 young men gird themselves up and prepare themselves for the contests;
90 yet had you seen these prizes, you would have marveled deep in your spirit,
91 such were the beautiful prizes the goddess set down in your honor,
92 silver-footed Thetis, for you were dear to the deathless.
93 So even death never robbed you of name; but forever your glory
94 will be a noble renown among mortals, illustrious Achilles.
95 What consolation was mine when I finished the labor of warfare?
96 Zeus on the road of my homecoming planned my pitiful ruin,
97 under the hands of Aigisthus and my accursed wife beside him.”
98 So in this manner the two of them spoke to each other together.
99 Near them now came the Guide, the destroyer of Argus, approaching,
100 leading the spirits of suitors whom great Odysseus had slaughtered.
101 Both went amazed straight toward them as soon as they saw them.
102 Then the spirit of Agamemnon, the son of Atreus,
103 knew Amphimedon, the famous and well-loved son of Melaneus,
104 since he had hosted him once in his home in the land of Ithaca.
105 First the spirit of Atreus’ son addressed him, saying:
106 “What has befallen you, Amphimedon, that all of you entered
107 into the shadowy earth, men chosen and equal in lifetime?
108 No one selecting could choose a finer group out of the city.
109 Did Poseidon subdue you all as you sailed in your vessels,
110 raising against you terrible winds and mountainous billows?
111 Or did hostile men destroy you when you were on dry land,
112 as you were cutting away their cattle or beautiful sheep-flocks,
113 or as you fought for a city and all of its women together?
114 Answer the questions I ask; I claim I remain your guest-friend.
115 Do you not remember the time when I came to your house there,
116 urging Odysseus, together with godlike lord Menelaus,
117 both to accompany us to Ilion on well-benched vessels?
118 All of the wide sea took us a whole month to traverse,
119 scarcely persuading Odysseus the sacker of cities to join us.”
120 Then the spirit of Lord Amphimedon answered him, saying:
121 “Glorious son of Atreus, lord Agamemnon, king of the war-host,
122 Zeus-reared king, I remember all these events as you tell them;
123 now I will give you a full and entirely accurate telling
124 of the evil way our death was accomplished and how it befell us.
125 Long we had courted the wife of Odysseus, long since departed;
126 neither would she refuse the hateful marriage nor finish it,
127 all the while planning death and black destruction against us.
128 But in her heart she devised still another scheme to deceive us:
129 setting a mighty loom in her hall, she began to weave on it
130 delicate cloth of enormous breadth; then she said in our hearing:
131 ‘Young men, suitors of mine, since great Odysseus has perished,
132 wait, though you hasten my marriage, until I complete this burial
133 shroud, so none of the yarn I have spun may be wasted in idleness.
134 It is a shroud for the hero Laertes, awaiting the moment
135 murderous fate lays hold of him in the pain of his dying.
136 Otherwise one of the Achaian women may blame me among them
137 if, after winning great wealth, he should lie without any winding-sheet.’
138 So she spoke, and our lordly hearts were persuaded to trust her.
139 Then in the daytime she wove at the huge loom standing before her,
140 but in the night she unraveled her work by the light of the firebrands.
141 Three full years with her cunning she fooled and persuaded Achaians;
142 then, when the fourth year came and the seasons returned in their courses,
143 as the moons all waned and the many days were accomplished,
144 one of her women at last, who clearly knew, came and told us,
145 and we discovered her there unraveling the splendid weaving.
146 So she completed the work unwilling, constrained by necessity.
147 When she displayed the shroud, after weaving the great cloth fully
148 and washing it clean, it shone with the light of the sun or the moonlight.
149 Then from somewhere an evil power brought great Odysseus
150 out to the farthest edge of his farm, where his swineherd was living.
151 There the beloved son of divine Odysseus joined him,
152 coming from sandy Pylos to him in his black-hulled vessel;
153 both of them fashioned a wretched death for the suitors together,
154 then they arrived at the famous city—Odysseus, indeed,
155 coming behind, while Telemachus went as the leader before him.
156 Led by the swineherd, Odysseus wore foul garments around him,
157 looking just like a miserable beggar, aged and broken,
158 leaning upon his staff, with the pitiful rags draped round him.
159 None of us there could recognize who he was when he suddenly
160 stood in our sight, not even the men who were older among us,
161 but we abused him with wicked words and pelted him freely.
162 Meanwhile he endured in the halls of his very own palace,
163 struck and abused as he was, with a heart that patiently suffered.
164 But when the mind of the aegis-bearing Zeus finally stirred him,
165 he and Telemachus lifted the splendid arms there together,
166 laid them away in the storeroom and fastened the bolts behind them.
167 Then with abundant cunning he gave his own wife the order
168 to set out bow for the suitors together with gray iron axes—
169 contests for us ill-fated and also the start of our slaughter.
170 Not one man among us possessed enough strength for the powerful bow,
171 stretching its string; by far we were lacking in strength for the effort.
172 But when the mighty bow of Odysseus came to his own hands,
173 every one of us shouted aloud in a single outcry,
174 never to give him the bow, however much he might argue.
175 Only Telemachus urged that man and gave him the order to do it.
176 Then much-enduring Odysseus took up the bow in his hand there,
177 easily strung it and sent the arrow through all of the iron.
178 Going to stand on the threshold, he poured his swift arrows before him,
179 glaring with terrible eyes, and struck royal Antinous down.
180 Then he discharged his agonizing shafts at the others,
181 aiming directly; and crowded together the suitors kept falling.
182 Clearly some one of the gods was present to aid his companions;
183 for they immediately hunted us down through the house in their fury,
184 slaughtering right and left; an unspeakable groaning arose as
185 heads were battered, and all of the floor ran seething with bloodshed.
186 That was the manner, Agamemnon, in which we all perished;
187 still even now our neglected bodies lie in Odysseus’ palace.
188 None of our friends in the houses of each of our families knows yet,
189 friends who would wash from the wounds the black and clotted blood on us,
190 lay out our bodies and mourn—the honor the dead should be given.”
191 Then the spirit of Atreus’ son in answer addressed him:
192 “Fortunate son of Laertes, resourceful lord Odysseus,
193 truly you won for yourself a wife of magnificent virtue.
194 Noble indeed was the heart of the blameless queen Penelope,
195 daughter of Icarius; well she remembered Odysseus,
196 husband of all her youth; therefore her fame shall never
197 die, the fame of her virtue; immortals will fashion for mortals
198 songs full of grace in the praise of the constant-hearted Penelope.
199 Not so the daughter of Tyndareus, who contrived evil actions,
200 killing the man of her youth; a detestable song shall pursue her
201 among humankind, and she will bestow an evil repute on
202 all of the race of women, even the woman who acts well.”
203 So in this manner the two of them spoke to each other together,
204 standing in Hades’ house underneath the secret places of earth.
205 They came down from the city and soon arrived at the farmstead,
206 beautiful, well ordered, which once old Laertes acquired
207 as his possession, after enduring a great many labors.
208 There stood his house, with a row of huts running all around it;
209 there the slaves under bondage would eat and sit down and sleep there,
210 slaves who accomplished the work on the farm which pleased their master.
211 There too lived an old Sicilian woman who always
212 carefully tended the old man there on the farm, far from the city.
213 There Odysseus spoke to his slaves and beloved son, saying:
214 “You now go inside the well-built house there together;
215 slaughter at once for our dinner whichever of swine is the finest.
216 Meanwhile I shall put our father himself to the test there;
217 whether he recognizes me and discerns me with both of his eyes, or
218 fail to know me after the long time I have been absent.”
219 So he spoke and handed the slaves all the warlike arms for the fighting.
220 They then quickly went to the house, while lord Odysseus onward
221 went toward the orchard abundant in fruit to conduct his testing.
222 He did not find Dolios there as he entered the spacious orchard,
223 nor any slave nor any son of Dolios; all of them had gone
224 out to gather stones for the orchard’s enclosing wall there,
225 while the old servant himself went leading the way before them.
226 There he discovered his father alone in the well-ordered orchard,
227 digging around a plant; the tunic he wore was filthy,
228 patched and unseemly; around both his shins he wore leggings of oxhide,
229 stitched at the seams and tied fast, to protect himself against scratches;
230 gloves he had drawn on his hands for protection against the brambles;
231 over his head was a goatskin cap, and he nurtured his sorrow.
232 When much-enduring Odysseus saw him there in the orchard,
233 worn by the weight of his age, with a mighty grief in his spirit,
234 standing beneath a tall pear tree, he let fall a tear from his eyelids.
235 Then he debated at length in the innermost depth of his spirit
236 whether to kiss and embrace his father and tell him the whole truth,
237 telling him how he had come and arrived in the land of his fathers,
238 or to question him first and test him thoroughly in all things.
239 As he considered, this course in the end seemed better before him:
240 first with words of mockery to test the mind of his father.
241 Keeping this purpose in mind, divine Odysseus went toward him.
242 Bending his head, the old man was loosening earth round a sapling.
243 Standing beside him, his glorious son addressed him, saying:
244 “Old man, you do not lack any skill in tending an orchard;
245 rather, it all is well tended, and nothing whatever among it—
246 neither a plant nor a fig tree, vine nor olive tree growing,
247 neither a pear nor a bed—has been left without care in the garden.
248 Yet I will tell you another thing; do not be angered against me:
249 you yourself have not been well cared for, but wretched old age has
250 taken you; squalid you are, and you wear clothing shameful and ragged.
251 Surely your master does not neglect you because you are idle,
252 nor is there anything slavish in you when somebody looks at
253 all of your height and your features; you seem like a king among mortals.
254 You seem the kind of a man who, after he bathes and has eaten,
255 ought to lie softly asleep, as is fitting and right for the aged.
256 Come then, tell me this now and give me an accurate answer:
257 whose slave are you among men, and whose is the orchard you tend here?
258 Also declare to me truthfully this, so that I may know well
259 whether indeed we have come to Ithaca here, just as he told me—
260 that man whom I met just now on the road as I came on my journey,
261 not an entirely sound-minded man, for he would not dare to
262 tell me the whole tale or hear what I said when I questioned him closely
263 touching a guest-friend of mine, whether still he lives in the daylight
264 or has already died and gone to the houses of Hades.
265 I will explain it to you; now listen to me and remember.
266 Once in the land of my fathers I entertained as my guest-friend
267 one who had come to our house; and no other mortal among all
268 faraway strangers who came to my home was dearer than that man.
269 He claimed that his family line had its roots in the island of Ithaca;
270 Arkeisias’ son Laertes, he said, was his father.
271 Bringing him into my house, I generously gave him welcome,
272 carefully honoring him from the wealth that was stored in my household.
273 I also gave him the proper gifts that are due to a guest-friend:
274 seven whole talents of gold that was beautifully fashioned I gave him,
275 also a mixing-bowl wrought all in silver and flowered with patterns,
276 twelve single-fold cloaks and as many rugs for his bedding,
277 that many beautiful mantles and that many tunics beside them,
278 also, apart from these, women accomplished in blameless handiwork,
279 four lovely women, whom he himself had desired and selected.”
280 Then his father replied, while the tears streamed down from his eyelids:
281 “Stranger, indeed you have reached the land you have asked me about here,
282 but it is held by presumptuous men who are wicked and lawless.
283 All these gifts you bestowed, though countless, were given in vain there.
284 If you had found that man still living here in the country
285 of Ithaca, he would have well repaid you in gifts and then sent you
286 forth with excellent welcome, the right for whoever gives first.
287 Come then, tell me this now and give me an accurate answer:
288 how many years have passed since you entertained that man,
289 that unfortunate guest-friend of yours, my son—if he ever existed—
290 ill-fated man, who far from his friends and the land of his fathers
291 either was eaten by fish somewhere in the sea, or on dry land
292 became a spoil for the beasts and the birds; nor did his mother
293 mourn as she wrapped him, nor father—we two who gave him his being;
294 nor did his richly dowered wife, constant-hearted Penelope,
295 keen for her husband there on his bed, as was fitting and proper,
296 closing his eyes; for this is the honor accorded the dead ones.
297 Also declare to me truthfully this, so that I may know well:
298 who are you, whence among men? Where are your city and parents?
299 Where is the swift ship moored that brought you here to this country
300 with your companions like gods? Or have you come as a trader
301 borne in another man’s vessel, whose crew put you ashore and departed?”
302 Then resourceful Odysseus answered his father and told him:
303 “Therefore I shall relate all things with complete exactness.
304 I come from Alybas, where in glorious halls I have made my dwelling,
305 son of King Apheidas, who was the son of Polypemon;
306 as for myself, my name is Eperitus. Yet some divinity
307 drove me from Sicania here against all the wish in my spirit.
308 Yonder my ship lies moored by the farm, far outside of the city.
309 As for Odysseus, now it is fully the fifth year completed,
310 when he departed from me and went away from the land of my fathers,
311 ill-fated man; yet the birds were favorable omens above him,
312 flying at right, so I joyfully sent him upon his journey,
313 he too rejoicing to go; both our hearts still hoped for another
314 meeting in guest-friendship and giving of beautiful presents.”
315 So he spoke, and a dark cloud of sorrow descended upon him;
316 taking the black and the sooty dust up in both of his hands there,
317 he poured it over his gray head, heavily groaning without pause.
318 Then the heart of Odysseus stirred, and up through his nostrils already
319 surged a keen force as he looked on the face of his well-beloved father.
320 Leaping upon him, he clasped and kissed him and spoke to him, saying:
321 “I myself, my father, am here—that man you are seeking!
322 In the twentieth year I have come to the land of my fathers.
323 Hold back now from your weeping and tearful lamentation.
324 I will explain it to you, but we must act quickly in all things:
325 I have slaughtered the suitors within the halls of our household,
326 taking revenge for their heart-wounding insolence and their wicked actions.”
327 Then Laertes replied to his son, addressing him plainly:
328 “If you indeed are Odysseus, my son who has come here,
329 tell me some evident sign that will make me believe what you tell me.”
330 Then resourceful Odysseus answered his father and told him:
331 “First with your own eyes look at the scar I carry upon me,
332 where the boar on Parnassus once struck with his white tusk and tore me.
333 There I went; you yourself and my honored mother had sent me
334 forth to Autolycus, dearly beloved father of my mother, to gather
335 gifts he had promised to give me when first he came here and confirmed it.
336 Come, I will tell you the trees throughout this well-ordered orchard
337 which you once gave me when I asked you to give me each one;
338 I was a child then, following after you all through the garden;
339 while you named and told me the nature of every tree as we passed it.
340 Thirteen pear trees you gave me and ten full apple trees also,
341 forty fig trees; and there you named rows of vines in the vineyard
342 which you would give me, fifty in all, each yielding its vintage in sequence;
343 clusters of every sort hang there throughout the whole vineyard,
344 whenever seasons of Zeus from above make the grape clusters heavy.”
345 So he spoke; and the old man’s knees and beloved heart loosened,
346 recognizing the steadfast signs Odysseus clearly had given.
347 Round his beloved son he threw both arms in embracing;
348 much-enduring divine Odysseus caught him as he fainted.
349 Then when at last he revived and the life in his spirit was gathered,
350 once again in reply he addressed Odysseus, saying:
351 “Father Zeus, truly still there are gods on the height of Olympos,
352 if the suitors truly have paid for their reckless presumption!
353 Yet now terrible fear fills my innermost heart, lest quickly
354 all the Ithacans come against us here on the farmstead,
355 sending out word everywhere to the cities of all Kephallenians.”
356 Then resourceful Odysseus answered his father and told him:
357 “Courage! Do not let any of these things trouble your spirit.
358 Let us go instead to the house which lies by the orchard;
359 there I sent Telemachus, cowherd, and swineherd before us,
360 I sent on ahead, so they might prepare us the dinner most swiftly.”
361 So after speaking together, they went to the beautiful farmhouse.
362 Then, when the two of them came to the well-inhabited dwelling,
363 there they discovered Telemachus, cowherd and swineherd together,
364 cutting abundant meat and mixing the wine with its fire-glow.
365 Meanwhile, great-hearted Laertes was there in his own household;
366 the Sicilian woman first bathed him and rubbed him with olive oil,
367 then wrapped him in a beautiful cloak; but meanwhile Athena,
368 standing beside him, increased all the limbs of the shepherd of peoples,
369 making him taller than formerly, greater and broader to look at.
370 Out of the bath he stepped, and his son was amazed as he saw him,
371 for face-to-face he appeared like the gods who continue forever;
372 lifting his voice, he addressed him in winged words, saying:
373 “Father, surely some one of the gods who exist for all ages
374 has made you seem nobler now both in beauty and stature before me.”
375 Then in reply wise-minded Laertes addressed him, saying:
376 “If only, Father Zeus and Athena and lord Apollo,
377 such as I was when I captured Nericus, well-built city,
378 set on the mainland shore, while I ruled the Kephallenian people,
379 such had I been beside you yesterday here in our household,
380 wearing the arms on my shoulders, to stand in the battle beside you,
381 warding off suitor-men; then I would have loosened their knees there—
382 many inside those halls—while you would rejoice in your spirit.”
383 So in this manner the two of them spoke to each other together.
384 Then when the others had ended their labor and readied the dinner,
385 all in order they sat themselves down on the couches and high chairs.
386 There they began to reach for the food; and near them approaching
387 came old Dolios now, and the sons of the elderly servant,
388 weary from labor, for she who had gone on before them had called them,
389 their elderly Sicilian mother, who raised them and always
390 carefully tended the old man after old age had caught him.
391 When they saw Odysseus there and knew in their hearts who he was, they
392 stood in amazement and wonder inside of the hall; but Odysseus
393 welcoming all of them gently, he spoke and addressed them as follows:
394 “Sit down, old man, and dine now; put all astonishment from you.
395 Long have we yearned to begin with the food, yet we kept ourselves waiting
396 here in the halls, all the while expecting your coming among us.”
397 So he spoke; Dolios went straight toward him with both hands
398 stretching before him, and took Odysseus’ hand at the wrist and kissed it.
399 Lifting his voice, he addressed him in winged words, saying:
400 “Dear one, now you have come home, greatly longed for by all of us,
401 though no longer expected; the gods themselves have returned you,
402 Health to you, and great joy! May the gods give you prosperous fortune.
403 Also declare to me truthfully this, so that I may know well:
404 does constant-hearted Penelope clearly know already
405 you have returned to this place, or should we send her a messenger?”
406 Then resourceful Odysseus answered the servant and told him:
407 “She knows already, old friend; why need you labor about it?”
408 So he spoke; Dolios sat once again on the polished chair there.
409 Likewise the sons of Dolios gathered around famed Odysseus,
410 greeting him warmly with words and clasping his hands in affection;
411 then they sat down in order beside Dolios, their father.
412 So in the halls of the farmhouse they busied themselves with the dinner.
413 Rumor meanwhile, the messenger, swiftly traversed the whole city,
414 telling the suitors’ abhorrent death and the fate that had seized them.
415 All of the people who heard it came streaming from every direction,
416 groaning and wailing aloud in front of Odysseus’ palace.
417 Each one carried his dead from the house and conducted the burial;
418 those whose homes were located in other cities, however,
419 men placed aboard the swift ships of fishermen, sending them homeward.
420 Then in a body they went to assembly, hearts full of sorrow.
421 When they had gathered together and all were assembled among them,
422 then Eupeithes arose in their midst and addressed the assembly,
423 since unforgettable grief for his son lay heavy upon him—
424 Antinous, whom first of the suitors divine Odysseus slaughtered.
425 Weeping his tears for his son, he spoke and addressed the assembly:
426 “Friends, this man has contrived an enormous outrage against us!
427 First in his vessels he led a great multitude, noble companions;
428 then he destroyed hollow ships and utterly destroyed his comrades;
429 now on returning he murdered the finest men of Kephallenes.
430 Come, before this man can swiftly reach the country of Pylos
431 or even sacred Elis, the place where Epeians govern,
432 let us go after him, or forever afterward bear our dishonor.
433 This is a shame for the people to come in the future to hear of,
434 if we do not avenge our sons and our brothers, murdered by those men.
435 Life would no longer be sweet in the innermost depth of my spirit;
436 I would prefer to die soon and join the departed among them.
437 Let us go, before those men cross over the water and leave us.”
438 So he spoke, shedding tears, and pity took all the Achaians.
439 Near them now came Medon and also the godlike singer,
440 out of Odysseus’ hall, when their sleep at last had released them.
441 Both stood there in the midst, and amazement seized every man there.
442 Then with sound understanding Medon addressed the assembly:
443 “Now give ear to me, people of Ithaca! Surely Odysseus
444 did not contrive all these deeds against the will of the deathless.
445 I with my own eyes saw an immortal god who was standing
446 close to Odysseus’ side, exactly resembling Mentor.
447 At one time that immortal god appeared in front of Odysseus,
448 giving him courage; at another, rousing the suitors to panic,
449 he stormed through the hall till crowded together they fell in the slaughter.”
450 So he spoke, and the pallor of fear took hold of them deeply.
451 Then the old hero Halitherses addressed the Ithacan people,
452 Mastor’s son, who alone saw the things both behind and before them;
453 kindly disposed toward all, he spoke and addressed the assembly:
454 “Listen to me now, Ithacans, and what I shall tell you!
455 Friends, through your own wickedness all these deeds have arisen.
456 You would not listen to me or Mentor, the shepherd of peoples,
457 when we entreated you all to restrain your sons from their follies.
458 They committed a terrible deed in their wicked presumption,
459 wasting possessions and bringing dishonor upon his consort,
460 wife of a peerless man, whom they thought would never return here.
461 Now let it happen as follows, and heed the advice I shall give you:
462 let us not go, lest somebody find self-invited disaster.”
463 So he spoke; and the men sprang up with a mighty commotion;
464 more than half went away, while the rest stayed gathered together.
465 What he had said did not please those remaining; instead, to Eupeithes
466 they gave obedience, then rushed at once to put on their armor.
467 When they had clothed their bodies in bronze with its glittering brightness,
468 all in a body they gathered outside the broad-wayed city.
469 Foolishly then Eupeithes took his place as their leader;
470 he claimed he would avenge the murder committed against his own son, but
471 never would he return; he would meet his own fate in that place.
472 Then the goddess Athena spoke to Zeus the son of Kronos:
473 “Father of ours, son of Kronos, highest of all those who govern,
474 tell me, since I ask, what purpose you hide in your innermost spirit.
475 Will you bring evil war and the terrible clash of the war-hosts
476 further, or establish friendship between both of the parties?”
477 Zeus who gathers the clouds in answer addressed her, saying:
478 “Child of my heart, why do you question and ask me about this?
479 Did you yourself not devise this purpose and plan it already,
480 that on returning Odysseus surely would make those men pay?
481 Do as you wish; yet I will tell you the course that is fitting.
482 Since divine Odysseus has punished the suitors among them,
483 after they swear binding oaths, let him rule as their king there forever.
484 We in our turn shall forget the murder of sons and of brothers;
485 let that oblivion stand, and let them love one another
486 just as before; let wealth and peace be abundant among them.”
487 So after speaking, he spurred Athena, eager already,
488 and from the heights of Olympos she darted downward in flight there.
489 Then, when the men had put from themselves desire for the sweet food,
490 much-enduring divine Odysseus first addressed all the others:
491 “One of you go outside and see if those men are approaching.”
492 So he spoke; and a son of Dolios went as commanded,
493 stood on the threshold and saw the whole company drawing close to them.
494 Quickly he spoke to Odysseus then in winged words, saying:
495 “They are already near us; let us arm ourselves faster!”
496 So he spoke, and they rose and clothed themselves in their armor,
497 four men including Odysseus, with six of the sons of Dolios;
498 Also Laertes and Dolios put on their armor,
499 gray though they were, since need had made them fighters in battle.
500 When they had clothed their bodies in bronze with its glittering brightness,
501 They opened wide the doors and went out, Odysseus leading.
502 Near to them came the daughter of Zeus, gray-eyed Athena,
503 taking the bodily form and the very voice of Mentor.
504 Much-enduring divine Odysseus saw her and rejoiced in spirit;
505 quickly he spoke to Telemachus, his beloved son, saying:
506 “Now, Telemachus, you shall know for yourself as you enter the fighting,
507 where men do battle and where the greatest warriors are chosen,
508 not to disgrace the lineage of your fathers, men who before this
509 always excelled in courage and manhood across the whole earth.”
510 Then wise-minded Telemachus answered his father and told him:
511 “You will see, if you wish it, beloved father, that this heart
512 never shall shame the descent from you, as you have instructed.”
513 So he spoke; and Laertes rejoiced and lifted his voice there:
514 “What a day this has grown for me, beloved gods! I am joyful!
515 Son and grandson are striving together concerning their excellence.”
516 Standing beside him, the gray-eyed goddess Athena addressed him:
517 “Son of Arkeisias, far the most beloved of all my companions,
518 offer a prayer to gray-eyed Athena and Father Zeus;
519 quickly then poise and brandish and hurl your long-shadowed spear.”
520 So she spoke; then Pallas Athena breathed mighty power within him.
521 Then after praying aloud to the daughter of Zeus the almighty,
522 quickly he poised and brandished and hurled his long-shadowed spear,
523 striking Eupeithes directly through his bronze-cheeked helmet.
524 That could not keep back the spear; clear through it the bronze point traveled.
525 Thudding he fell, and the armor upon him clattered aloud there.
526 Then on the foremost fighters Odysseus fell with his bright son,
527 striking away with their swords and their double-edged spears in the battle.
528 Now they would all have been slain and denied any homeward returning,
529 had not Athena, the daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus,
530 shouted aloud in a voice that restrained all the people from fighting:
531 “Stop this agonizing warfare now, you Ithacan people,
532 so you may quickly be parted without any further bloodshed!”
533 So spoke Athena; and pallid fear took hold of the warriors.
534 Out of the hands of the terrified men their weapons went flying,
535 falling all over the earth when the goddess’s voice had commanded;
536 back toward the city they turned, for they longed to preserve their lives.
537 Much-enduring divine Odysseus shouted terribly,
538 gathered himself and swooped like an eagle that flies in the heavens.
539 Then the son of Kronos discharged his thunderbolt, smoking;
540 down it fell just in front of the gray-eyed daughter of mighty Zeus.
541 Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena addressed Odysseus:
542 “Son of Laertes, descended from Zeus, resourceful Odysseus,
543 hold yourself back now; cease from indiscriminate warfare,
544 lest broad-thundering Zeus, the son of Kronos, be angry against you.”
545 So spoke Athena; he willingly yielded, glad in his spirit.
546 Then for the days to come she established oaths for both parties,
547 Pallas Athena, the daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus,
548 taking the bodily form and the very voice of Mentor.
