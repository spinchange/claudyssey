# The Odyssey — Book XXI

*The bow of Odysseus; the trial of the axes.*

1 Then the goddess Athena, whose eyes shine brightly, implanted
2 deep in the spirit of Icarius’ daughter, thoughtful Penelope,
3 purpose to set the bow and the gray iron out for the suitors
4 there in Odysseus’ palace, a contest and prelude to slaughter.
5 Up the high staircase she went in the house that belonged to her husband,
6 taking the supple-curved key in the palm of her powerful hand there,
7 beautiful, fashioned of bronze with a handle of ivory fitted.
8 Off to the storeroom she went with the serving women attending,
9 farthest inside, where the treasures of her lord were lying,
10 bronze and gold together with iron that much toil had fashioned.
11 There lay the recurved bow and the quiver designed to hold arrows,
12 holding a multitude of arrows, the shafts that would make warriors groan loudly.
13 These were the gifts that a stranger gave him when they met in Lacedaemon,
14 Iphitus, son of Eurytus, one like the deathless immortals.
15 These two men encountered each other in Messenia,
16 there in the house belonging to thoughtful Ortilochus. Lord Odysseus
17 came to recover a debt that the whole community owed him:
18 men of Messenia carried away from the island of Ithaca
19 three hundred sheep in many-benched ships, together with all of the herdsmen.
20 For these Odysseus had traveled the long road as an envoy,
21 though he was young, for his father and other elders had sent him.
22 Iphitus, meanwhile, was seeking the mares that he lost there,
23 twelve of the females, with sturdy mules still nursing beneath them.
24 These were later to prove both the death and the doom that befell him,
25 once he had come to the mighty-hearted son of Zeus in his household,
26 Heracles, man who knew thoroughly all of the great deeds.
27 That man murdered him there, although he was a guest in his household,
28 pitiless wretch, with no reverence for the gods or the table
29 that he had set out before him; then afterward Heracles killed him,
30 keeping the hard-hoofed mares himself in his high-roofed palace.
31 Seeking those mares, Iphitus met Odysseus and gave him
32 that bow once carried by mighty Eurytus, who to his own son
33 left it behind to his son when he died in his high-roofed halls there.
34 Then Odysseus gave Iphitus a sharp sword and powerful spear,
35 starting their warm and intimate friendship; they never at table
36 knew one another, however, before Zeus’ son had killed
37 Iphitus, Eurytus’ son, one like the deathless immortals,
38 giver of that bow. Godlike Odysseus never would take it
39 when he went off to the war aboard the black-hulled vessels;
40 rather it stayed in the house as a memory left by the dear guest,
41 while he would carry it only at home in the land of his fathers.
42 When she, the brilliant among women, arrived at the storeroom,
43 there to the threshold fashioned of oak she came, which a craftsman
44 once had planed with expert skill and squared with a plumb line,
45 fitting the doorposts in it and setting the glittering doors there.
46 Straightaway she loosened the leather strap from its hook there,
47 inserted the key and lifted the bolts that held fast both the doors there,
48 aiming directly; they bellowed aloud like a bull in the meadow,
49 one that is grazing in pasture; so loudly the beautiful doors there
50 groaned when struck by the key, then quickly flew open before her.
51 Up to the high plank she stepped; and there stood the chests that were laden,
52 holding the fragrant garments that lay all folded within them.
53 Reaching above them, she lifted the bow from the peg where it hung there,
54 taking its glittering bow case as well, which enclosed it completely.
55 Sitting there on the floor, she laid it across her own dear knees and
56 wept with a piercing cry as she drew out the bow of her husband.
57 Then, when she had taken her fill of the many-teared lamentation,
58 off to the hall she went and the company of the arrogant suitors,
59 bearing the recurved bow in her hands and the quiver for arrows,
60 holding a multitude of arrows, the shafts that would make warriors groan loudly.
61 Behind her, attendants carried the chest in which all of the iron
62 lay in abundance with bronze, as prizes in the contest of her lord.
63 When she, the brilliant among women, arrived at the suitors,
64 there by the pillar that held the close-made roof she stood firmly,
65 holding the shining veil before either cheek as she waited.
66 One devoted attendant stood at each side beside her.
67 Straightaway she addressed all the suitors and spoke out among them:
68 “Listen to me, proud suitors, you men who beset this household,
69 eating and drinking continually here and never departing,
70 house of a man who has now been away for a long time; you have no other
71 pretext of speech that you ever have managed to make for your conduct,
72 only desire to marry me now and possess me as your woman.
73 Come, then, suitors, since this is the contest appearing before you.
74 I will set out the great bow that belongs to godlike Odysseus;
75 whoever most easily bends and strings up the bow in his two hands,
76 then sends an arrow through every one of the axes, all twelve of them,
77 I will follow that man and leave this house behind me,
78 home of my marriage, exceedingly beautiful, filled with my substance,
79 which I believe I will still recall, even seeing it only in dreamtime.”
80 So she spoke and told Eumaeus, the noble keeper of swine there,
81 both to set out the bow and the gray iron in front of the suitors.
82 Weeping, Eumaeus received them and set them before them;
83 off to one side the cowherd wept when he saw the bow of his master.
84 Then Antinous rebuked them, calling them out and addressing them:
85 “Foolish rustic men, concerned with the things of a single day only,
86 wretched pair, why let these tears run down, and disturb the
87 heart in the breast of the woman? Her spirit already without this
88 lies full of pain because she has lost the dear lord she had married.
89 Feast here sitting in silence, or go outside from the palace,
90 weeping away, but leave that bow where it is for the suitors,
91 contest difficult to overcome; for I do not believe this
92 beautifully polished bow can be easily bent into tension.
93 There is no man in the company now who is like the man that
94 Lord Odysseus was; I saw him myself, for I can
95 still recollect him, although I was only a child at the time then.”
96 So he spoke; but the heart in his breast was hoping to fasten
97 string to the bow and send an arrow through iron before him.
98 Yet he was destined himself to be first to taste of the arrow
99 sent from the hands of the blameless Odysseus, whom he dishonored
100 then as he sat in his halls and urged every one of his comrades.
101 Telemachus’ mighty and sacred strength spoke again to the gathered:
102 “Gods, how greatly Cronus’ son Zeus must have made me a madman!
103 My own mother, although she is thoughtful, tells me she plans to
104 follow another and leave this house that belongs to her husband;
105 yet I am laughing and taking delight in my witless spirit.
106 Come, then, suitors, since this is the contest appearing before you:
107 such a woman as this does not live today in Achaean
108 lands, not in sacred Pylos or Argos or even Mycenae,
109 neither in Ithaca’s island itself nor the dark-soiled mainland.
110 You know this for yourselves; what need to sing my mother’s praises?
111 Come now, do not draw things out with excuses, nor shrink from the bowstring
112 any more for a long time, so we may see what will happen.
113 Even I myself would be willing to try this bow before you;
114 if I should string it and send the arrow clean through the iron,
115 then my lady mother would not leave this house to my grieving,
116 going away with another while I remained here behind her,
117 since I would now be able to win the beautiful contests of Father.”
118 So he spoke and took the crimson mantle down from his shoulders;
119 springing upright, he also laid the sharp sword off his shoulder.
120 First he set out the axes, digging a single long trench for them,
121 one for them all, and made them true to a single plumb line;
122 packed all the earth around them; amazement seized all the suitors,
123 seeing how neatly he stood them, although he had never beheld them.
124 Then he went back to the threshold and tested the bow in his two hands.
125 Three times he shook it, straining with eagerness, trying to draw it;
126 three times he slackened his strength, although in his heart he was hoping
127 still to attach the string and send an arrow through iron.
128 Now he would have strung it by force as he drew it a fourth time,
129 but Odysseus signaled no with his head and restrained him though eager.
130 Telemachus’ mighty and sacred strength spoke again to the gathered:
131 “Gods, I will surely be worthless and weak from this time onward,
132 or I am younger than I thought and cannot yet trust my two hands to
133 fend off a man when somebody grows angry and first comes against me.
134 Come, all you men who surpass me in strength, by however much you
135 do so, test the bow and let us bring the contest to fullness.”
136 So he spoke and set the bow on the ground at his feet there,
137 leaning it up against closely fitted, smoothly polished boards there,
138 panels, and leaned the swift arrow against the beautiful bow horn.
139 Back once more he went and sat on the chair from which he had risen.
140 Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke to the suitors:
141 “Rise in succession from left to right, all you companions,
142 starting from that place where the man who pours our wine starts his serving.”
143 So spoke Antinous; all of them found his proposal pleasing.
144 Leodes rose first of the men, the son of Oenops,
145 one who inspected their sacrifices; beside the beautiful mixing bowl
146 always he sat, farthest in; reckless behavior was hateful
147 only to him, and he felt indignation at all of the suitors.
148 This man first took up the bow together with one of the swift shafts.
149 Then he went back to the threshold and tested the bow in his two hands,
150 yet could not string it, for first both hands grew tired from the drawing,
151 tender and still uncalloused; he spoke to the suitors and told them:
152 “Dear friends, I cannot string it; let one of the others now take it.
153 This bow here will deprive many excellent men of their living
154 spirit and life, for certainly it is better by far to be dead than
155 live and fail in the thing for whose sake we always have gathered
156 here in the palace together, awaiting it every day here.
157 At this time each man cherishes hope in his heart and desires to
158 marry Penelope, she who was married to lord Odysseus.
159 Yet when each man has tried this bow with his hands and beheld it,
160 then let him court another Achaean woman with beautiful clothing,
161 seeking to win her with bride-gifts; then let the woman herself marry
162 whoever gives her the most and whoever fate sends before her.”
163 So he spoke, then set the bow on the ground at his feet there,
164 leaning it up against closely fitted, smoothly polished boards there,
165 panels, and leaned the swift arrow against the beautiful bow horn.
166 Back once more he went and sat on the chair from which he had risen.
167 Then Antinous rebuked him, calling him out and addressing him:
168 “Leodes, what kind of word has escaped from the fence of your teeth now?
169 Dreadful and burdensome! I am resentful to hear you proclaim it,
170 saying this bow will deprive the best of the men of their living
171 spirit and life because you yourself do not have strength to string it.
172 Surely your lady mother did not give birth to a man who was able
173 both to be drawing the bow and to wield the swift arrows in battle;
174 some of the other proud suitors will string it in no time.”
175 So he spoke and commanded Melanthius, keeper of goats there:
176 “Come now, kindle a fire in the hall here, Melanthius,
177 then set beside it a large chair with a fleece spread over it.
178 Bring out a massive wheel of fat from the stores that we keep here,
179 so that we young men can warm the bow and rub it with tallow,
180 then try to string it and bring this contest at last to completion.”
181 So he spoke; Melanthius quickly rekindled the tireless fire,
182 then carried a large chair beside it and laid out a fleece there.
183 Out of the stores he brought them a massive wheel of tallow;
184 warming the bow with this, the young men tried it, but no one was able
185 then to attach the string: they lacked that strength by a great way.
186 Only Antinous still held back and godlike Eurymachus,
187 leaders among the suitors, by far the best of them all in their prowess.
188 Meanwhile, out of the house there went two men side by side there,
189 cowherd and swineherd belonging to godlike Odysseus;
190 after them godlike Odysseus himself came out of the household.
191 Then, when all three men were outside of the gates and the courtyard,
192 calling to them, he spoke with gentle words and addressed them:
193 “Cowherd and you, keeper of swine, shall I speak out a message,
194 or shall I hide it? My heart and spirit command me to tell you.
195 What kind of men would you be to defend Odysseus if he came here
196 suddenly, right here, if a god should bring the man to his household?
197 Would you defend the suitors, or would you fight for Odysseus?
198 Tell me exactly the thing that your hearts and your spirits command you.”
199 Then in response the man who was herdsman of cattle addressed him:
200 “Father Zeus, if only you would fulfill this prayer that I cherish,
201 so that the man might come and a god might conduct him among us;
202 then you would know what strength I possess in the force of my two hands.”
203 Even so did Eumaeus pray to every immortal
204 that many-minded Odysseus might come to his own house safely.
205 Then, when the king recognized the unerring thoughts of them both there,
206 once again he spoke out in answer to them and addressed them:
207 “Here he is now inside; I myself am Odysseus. After
208 suffering numerous evils, I come in the twentieth year to my homeland.
209 Now I can see that I come to the two of you, only, among all
210 servants who longed for my coming; from none of the others did I hear any
211 prayer that I might come once again to my home from my exile.
212 Now I will tell you the truth of exactly the thing that will happen:
213 if underneath my power a god strikes down these arrogant suitors,
214 I will bring wives to you both and grant you possessions in plenty,
215 houses constructed near mine; and then you will be to me always
216 brothers and comrades of Prince Telemachus, both of you always.
217 Come, I will show you another conspicuous sign for your knowledge,
218 so you can know me well and possess sure trust in your spirits:
219 here is the scar that the boar once made with his glistening white tusk,
220 when I went up to Parnassus with sons of Autolycus.”
221 So he spoke and pulled back the rags that concealed the enormous scar there.
222 Then, when the two men beheld it and understood every detail,
223 throwing their arms around thoughtful Odysseus, both began weeping,
224 kissing with love the head and the shoulders of their lord and master.
225 Even so did Odysseus kiss their heads and their two hands.
226 Now the light of the sun would have gone down upon them still grieving,
227 had not Odysseus held them back with his words and addressed them:
228 “Cease from your sobbing and wailing, lest somebody see us,
229 coming outside from the hall, then carry the news to the men there.
230 Each enter after another and not all together;
231 I will go first and then you, and let this sign be established:
232 all the rest of the men, as many proud suitors as gather,
233 will not allow you to give me the bow and the quiver of arrows.
234 You, Eumaeus, divinely noble, must carry the bow through the palace,
235 place it into my hands, then go to the women and tell them
236 they are to fasten the closely fitted doors of the hall there.
237 If any woman hears either a groaning or din from within here,
238 raised by the men in our walls, she must not come out from the workroom,
239 rather remain where she is by her work in complete quiet.
240 Philotius, divinely noble, to you I command that the courtyard
241 gate be locked with the key and its cable be quickly fastened.”
242 So he spoke and went into the comfortably settled household;
243 going again to his stool, he sat where he had arisen.
244 Into the house those two servants of godlike Odysseus followed.
245 Eurymachus now was handling the bow in his two hands,
246 warming it here and there with the blaze of the fire; but even
247 so he could not attach the string, and his proud heart groaned deeply.
248 Vexed with the labor, he spoke these words and addressed them directly:
249 “Gods, what sorrow I feel for myself and for all of the others!
250 Not for the marriage do I lament so much, though it pains me;
251 plenty of other Achaean women live, some on this same
252 island of sea-girt Ithaca, others in cities around us.
253 But if we fall this greatly short in the measure of strength that belonged
254 to godlike Odysseus, so none of us has the power to string his
255 bow, it will shame us even when later generations hear of it.”
256 Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke in response to him:
257 “Eurymachus, it will not be so, and you know it yourself too.
258 Now through the country the holy feast of this god is in progress;
259 who would be drawing a bow on this day? Set it down without worry.
260 Let the axes remain where they stand, every one of them upright,
261 since I believe nobody will carry away any one there,
262 entering the house belonging to Laertes’ son Odysseus.
263 Come now, let the wine-pourer first make offerings in our cups here,
264 so we can pour to the gods and put down the curved bow for tonight.
265 Then, in the morning, command Melanthius, keeper of goats there,
266 he is to bring us the goats that are finest in all of the goat herds,
267 so we can lay their thighbones before far-shooting Apollo,
268 test the bow once again and bring this contest at last to completion.”
269 So spoke Antinous; all of them found his proposal pleasing.
270 Heralds poured out water to wash all the hands of the suitors,
271 while young serving men filled the mixing bowls to the brim with their wine;
272 after the first drops in each cup, they served it to all in succession.
273 Then, when they had poured and drunk whatever their spirit desired,
274 resourceful lord Odysseus spoke with deception among them:
275 “Listen to me, all suitors of our widely renowned queen,
276 while I speak what the heart here within my chest has commanded.
277 Mostly I beg Eurymachus and godlike Antinous,
278 since he has spoken this word in its proper measure as well here,
279 now to stop trying the bow and entrust its outcome to the gods there.
280 In the morning a god will give his strength to the man whom he wishes.
281 Come, though, give me this finely polished bow, so that here in your presence
282 I may test both my hands and my strength, and discover if still there
283 lives any sinew like that which I had in the bending limbs of my young age,
284 or if my wandering life and neglect have already destroyed it.”
285 So he spoke; and all of them felt overpowering indignation,
286 fearing that he would attach the string to the beautifully polished bow there.
287 Then Antinous rebuked him, calling him out and addressing him:
288 “Wretched among all strangers, no sense lives within you, not even the slightest;
289 are you not content to sit here peacefully among us
290 noble men and feast, never lacking a thing in the banquet,
291 hearing our conversation and speech? No other man hears us,
292 none who is stranger and beggar, while listening close to our council.
293 Honey-sweet wine has wounded you, even as it has damaged
294 others when one drinks greedily, failing to measure his wine well.
295 Wine caused even the greatly renowned Eurytion the Centaur
296 madness inside the palace of high-hearted lord Peirithous,
297 when he came to the Lapiths; once he had maddened his spirit with wine there,
298 crazed as he was, he wrought ugly deeds in the house of Peirithous.
299 Sorrow took hold of the heroes; they sprang up and dragged him through the forecourt,
300 hauling him outward and cutting off both of his ears with the pitiless bronze there,
301 also his nose; and delusion took hold of the thoughts in his mind there,
302 so he went on, still bearing his ruin in his foolish spirit.
303 From that time there has been strife between men and the race of the Centaurs;
304 yet when he grew heavy with wine, the first evil he found was his own there.
305 So I predict an enormous affliction for you if you string up
306 this bow: nobody here in our country will treat you with kindness,
307 in our own country; aboard a black ship at once we shall put you,
308 bound for King Echetus, mutilator of all mortal people,
309 send you; from there you never will be saved. Sit peacefully drinking,
310 do not contend with the men who are younger than you in their lifetime.”
311 Thoughtful Penelope spoke to Antinous, answering:
312 “It is not fair, Antinous, nor is it right to deprive the
313 guests of Telemachus, anyone coming here to his household.
314 Do you expect if this stranger should string the great bow of Odysseus,
315 putting his trust in the force and the strength of his own hands,
316 he would conduct me away to his home and possess me as wife there?
317 Surely he does not cherish such hope in the heart in his body;
318 none of you feast in this place with grief in his heart for that reason,
319 since such a thing would neither be proper nor even be fitting.”
320 Eurymachus, son of Polybus, spoke in response to her:
321 “Daughter of Lord Icarius, circumspect Penelope,
322 we do not think this man will take you; the thing is not fitting.
323 Rather, we feel shame at the words of the men and the women,
324 lest at some time another, inferior man of Achaea, should say this:
325 ‘Men far inferior now are courting the wife of a blameless
326 man, and none has the strength to attach the string to his polished bow there.
327 Yet some other wandering beggar arrived and did it
328 easily, then sent the arrow straight through the line of the iron.’
329 So they will say, and then these things will become our reproach here.”
330 Thoughtful Penelope spoke to Eurymachus, answering:
331 “Eurymachus, never can men enjoy good fame in the country
332 while dishonoring and consuming the house that belongs to
333 an excellent man; why make this thing your fear of reproach?
334 This foreign guest is exceedingly tall and strongly constructed,
335 and he declares he is born in the line of an excellent father.
336 Come now, give him the beautifully polished bow, so we can see him.
337 This I will tell you now, and certainly it will be brought to fulfillment:
338 if he attaches the string and Apollo gives him the glory,
339 I will clothe him in mantle and tunic, beautiful garments,
340 give him a sharp-pointed spear to fend off the dogs and his foes too,
341 also a double-edged sword and sandals to fasten beneath his feet;
342 then I will send him wherever his heart and his spirit command him.”
343 Telemachus, full of his good sense, answered her, saying:
344 “Mother, no other Achaean has any greater right to the bow than
345 I have to give it away or deny it to anyone I choose here,
346 none of the men who wield command in the rugged country of Ithaca,
347 none of the men who rule in the islands off horse-grazed Elis.
348 Nobody there can employ his strength and compel me unwilling,
349 even if I would give the bow to the stranger outright for keeping.
350 Go to your room now and occupy yourself with your own proper duties,
351 loom and distaff, and order your serving women each to her labor;
352 each to go about her work; the bow is the business of all men
353 here, but above everyone mine, for I hold power in this household.”
354 She went back to her rooms, astonished at what had been spoken,
355 since she had taken her son’s well-balanced word to her spirit.
356 Then, when she came upstairs with the serving women attending,
357 there she wept for Odysseus, her husband, until sleep finally
358 fell sweet on her eyelids, bestowed by bright-eyed Pallas Athena.
359 Meanwhile the noble swineherd took up and carried the curved bow;
360 all of the suitors shouted at him throughout the palace.
361 Looking at one who stood at his side, one proud young man would speak this:
362 “Where are you carrying that curved bow, you accursed swineherd,
363 maddened and lost? Soon there by the pigs the swift dogs will eat you,
364 far from all humankind, dogs you yourself raised, if Apollo
365 favors us now together with all of the other immortal gods here.”
366 So they spoke; but Eumaeus set down the bow on the spot there,
367 fearing the numerous men who were shouting at him in the palace.
368 Telemachus, though, from across the room cried loudly and threatened:
369 “Father, carry the bow; you should not obey all these men here,
370 lest I, although I am younger, should chase you away to the farmland,
371 hitting with stones as you go, for I am the stronger in body.
372 Would that I were this much stronger in hands and physical power
373 than every one of the suitors, as many as gather here in the palace;
374 then very soon I would hatefully send some man on his homeward
375 way out of this house, for all of them plot what is evil.”
376 So he spoke, and all of the suitors laughed sweetly at him,
377 then relaxed their terrible anger against Telemachus.
378 Bearing the bow through the house, the swineherd came to Odysseus,
379 stood at his side and placed it into the hands of the thoughtful king there.
380 Then he called to the nurse Eurycleia and spoke to address her:
381 “Thoughtful Eurycleia, Telemachus gives you an order:
382 fasten the closely fitted doors of the hall and keep them closed there.
383 If any woman hears groaning or noise in the palace,
384 raised by the men in our walls, she must not come outside,
385 but must remain where she is by her task in complete quiet.”
386 So he spoke; and the word remained with her, wingless and silent.
387 Then she fastened the doors of the comfortably settled household.
388 Silently out of the house Philotius leapt to the courtyard,
389 then he securely fastened the gates of the strongly fenced courtyard.
390 There underneath the portico lay a papyrus cable belonging
391 to a curved ship; with this he bound the doors, then went back inside there.
392 Going again to his stool, he sat where he had arisen,
393 keeping his eyes on Odysseus, who now was handling the bow there,
394 turning it every way and testing it here and there for damage,
395 see if the worms had eaten the horns while the lord was away from his homeland.
396 Looking at one who stood at his side, each man would speak this:
397 “Surely this man is some expert admirer or robber of bows here;
398 either he keeps bows like this at his own house somewhere,
399 or he is eager himself to make one, so confidently now he
400 handles it here and there, this wandering beggar well versed in all evil.”
401 Then another proud young man in the company spoke out among them:
402 “May he encounter as much profit and good in his future
403 as he is ever likely to find strength to string up this bow here.”
404 So spoke the suitors; but meanwhile resourceful Odysseus,
405 once he had lifted the great bow and inspected it every way there,
406 just as a man who has knowledge of lyres and the art of the singer
407 easily stretches a string around the new peg he is fitting,
408 fastening tightly twisted sheep gut at each end of the string there,
409 so without any effort Odysseus strung the great bow there.
410 Taking the string in the grasp of his right hand, he tested its tension;
411 under his touch it sang beautifully, like the voice of a swallow.
412 Terrible anguish seized all the suitors, and every man’s skin
413 changed in its color; Zeus thundered mightily, showing a sign there.
414 Then long-enduring, godlike Odysseus felt great rejoicing
415 that the son of crooked-counseled Cronus had sent him a portent.
416 Next he took up the swift arrow that lay on the table beside him,
417 naked; the rest of the arrows lay in the hollow quiver,
418 those which the men of Achaea themselves would be testing in no time.
419 Laying the shaft on his forearm, he drew string and arrow-notches;
420 sitting right there on his stool, he loosed the arrow before him,
421 aiming directly; he missed not one of all axes before him,
422 starting from the first axe haft; it went through them and outward,
423 that bronze-heavy arrow; then he spoke out to Telemachus:
424 “Telemachus, this stranger who sits in your hall does not shame you:
425 I did not miss my mark, nor labor for long while I strung up
426 this bow. Still my strength and my vigor remain completely unshaken,
427 not as these suitors belittle and taunt me, treating me shamefully.
428 Now it is time to prepare the evening meal for Achaeans
429 here in the light, and afterward they can enjoy themselves otherwise,
430 dancing and playing the lyre, those ornaments proper for feasting.”
431 So he spoke and signaled by lifting his brows; then the well-loved
432 son of godlike Odysseus, Telemachus, girded his sharp sword
433 on him and laid his beloved hand on the spear there,
434 standing beside the chair in the blaze of his bronze armor.
