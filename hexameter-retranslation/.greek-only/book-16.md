# The Odyssey — Book XVI

*Telemachus returns to Eumaeus; Odysseus reveals himself; father and son make their plan.*

1 Meanwhile there in the hut Odysseus and the noble swineherd
2 kindled a fire with dawn and prepared their breakfast beside it,
3 sending the herdsmen away with the gathered droves of the swine herd.
4 Round Telemachus the clamorous dogs came fawning and wagging,
5 never once barking as he approached. Lord Odysseus noticed
6 both how the dogs were fawning and how the footfall came round him.
7 Straightway he spoke to Eumaeus and uttered these swift-winging phrases:
8 “Surely, Eumaeus, a friend or another familiar person
9 comes to your house, for the dogs do not bark but are fawning around him;
10 now I can hear the noise of his feet as he comes to the doorway.”
11 Scarcely the whole of the sentence was spoken before his beloved
12 son stood there in the entrance. Amazed, the swineherd sprang upright;
13 out of his hands fell all of the vessels with which he was working,
14 mixing the fire-bright wine. He went straight to meet his young master,
15 kissing his head and the beautiful light of his eyes and his two hands,
16 both of them; suddenly down from his eyes fell a warm, swelling teardrop.
17 Just as a father with love in his heart welcomes home his own dear son,
18 back from a faraway country at last in the tenth turning year there,
19 only and late-born, the cause of a multitude of sorrows and labors,
20 so then the noble swineherd embraced the godlike Telemachus,
21 kissing him everywhere, as though he had fled from the clutches of death there.
22 Weeping aloud in his sorrow, he spoke these swift-winging phrases:
23 “Here you have come, Telemachus, sweet light! I thought I would never
24 see you again after you sailed in your vessel to Pylos.
25 Come now, enter, dear child, that I may rejoice in my spirit,
26 looking upon you home and returned so newly from elsewhere.
27 Seldom indeed do you come to the fields and visit the herdsmen;
28 rather you stay in the city, for this has delighted your spirit,
29 looking upon the destructive mob of the arrogant suitors.”
30 Then in reply wise-minded Telemachus spoke to the swineherd:
31 “So it shall be, father; I came to this place for your own sake,
32 both that my eyes may see you and I may hear you reporting
33 whether my mother remains in the halls or another already
34 married her, while Odysseus’ bed, perhaps, in his absence
35 lies without anyone sleeping upon it, possessed by foul cobwebs.”
36 Then in his turn the swineherd, the leader of men, gave an answer:
37 “Yes, indeed, she remains with an enduring heart in your palace;
38 always in misery for her the nights and the days have been wasting,
39 as she continually sits in the halls and waters them with tears.”
40 Thus having spoken, he took the bronze-headed spear of Telemachus;
41 then the prince entered inside and crossed the threshold of stone there.
42 Seeing him come to his seat, his father Odysseus yielded;
43 but Telemachus stopped him from rising and spoke from his place there:
44 “Sit where you are, stranger; we shall discover another
45 seat in our own steading, with someone beside us to set it.”
46 Thus he spoke; Odysseus went back and sat. Then the swineherd
47 spread down green twigs as a mattress, and laid a fleece over them thickly;
48 there in that place the beloved son of Odysseus rested.
49 Next the swineherd placed platters of meat cuts before them,
50 roasted, which they had left when they ate on the previous evening.
51 Swiftly he heaped up loaves of the bread in the baskets beside them,
52 mixing the honey-sweet wine in a cup that was fashioned of ivy;
53 then he sat down across from the godlike lord Odysseus.
54 All of them reached out their hands to partake of the food set before them.
55 Soon as they cast off desire for the drinking and eating before them,
56 then Telemachus spoke to the noble keeper of swine there:
57 “Father, from where did this stranger arrive? In what manner did sailors
58 bring him to Ithaca? Who did they claim as their people and homeland?
59 Surely I cannot imagine he came to this place upon foot here.”
60 Then in reply, Eumaeus the swineherd, you spoke to your master:
61 “Therefore, my child, I shall tell you the whole of the truthful account now.
62 Out of the broad land of Crete he declares that he comes by his birthright;
63 he says he has wandered about through many a city of mortals,
64 driven astray, for a god had spun such a destiny for him.
65 Now from a ship belonging to Thesprotian men he has stolen
66 off and arrived at my steading; and I put him under your power.
67 Do as you wish with the man, for he claims to be here as your suppliant.”
68 Then in reply wise-minded Telemachus spoke to the swineherd:
69 “Truly, Eumaeus, this word that you uttered has wounded my spirit.
70 How can I possibly welcome the stranger at home in the palace?
71 I myself am young and do not yet trust in my hands’ strength,
72 beating a man away if he should be the first to attack me.
73 Deep in the heart of my mother a divided purpose is turning:
74 whether she stays where she is at my side and tends to the household,
75 showing regard for her husband’s bed and the voice of the people,
76 or if already she follows the best of Achaeans among those
77 courting her here in the halls, the man who gives most for her bride-price.
78 Yet since the stranger has come to the place of your habitation,
79 I shall clothe him in cloak and in tunic, both beautiful garments,
80 give him a double-edged sword for defense and sandals to walk in,
81 sending him onward wherever his heart and his spirit command him.
82 If you prefer, you can keep him and care for him here in the steading;
83 I shall send clothing here and the whole of the food he requires,
84 all he can eat, so he burdens neither yourself nor your comrades.
85 Yet I would never permit him to go to the palace and enter
86 there with the suitors; for violent outrage in them has no limit,
87 lest they make mock of the stranger, and bitter grief will possess me.
88 Hard is it even for one man strong in the onrush of battle
89 ever to carry a plan through many, when they are far stronger.”
90 Then the much-enduring, noble Odysseus spoke to Telemachus:
91 “Friend, since it surely is right I should make an answer myself here,
92 truly my own heart is consumed as I listen inside me,
93 hearing the way you say that the suitors contrive their presumption
94 there in the halls, though against your will, a man such as you are.
95 Tell me, are you willingly mastered? Or do all the people
96 hate you throughout the land, obeying a god-given oracle?
97 Or do you blame your brothers, on whom a man may depend when they battle,
98 even if some enormous contention arises among them?
99 Would I were young as I am in the purpose at work in my spirit,
100 or were the son of blameless Odysseus, or were that man himself here;
102 then may a stranger immediately sever my head from my shoulders,
103 if I should fail to become an affliction to every last suitor,
104 entering into the palace of Laertes’ son, Odysseus.
105 If they should master me there by the weight of their numbers against me,
106 I should prefer to be killed in the halls that belong to my household,
107 rather than live to look endlessly on at these infamous actions:
108 strangers battered and serving women throughout the beautiful palace
109 dragged here and there and assaulted in shameful and violent fashion,
110 wine being wantonly drained and the bread of the household devoured,
111 vainly, with never an end or a goal to the work they are doing.”
112 Then in reply wise-minded Telemachus spoke to the stranger:
113 “Therefore, stranger, I now shall explain to you everything truly.
114 None of the people together are hostile or angry against me;
115 nor do I blame any brothers, on whom a man may depend when they battle,
116 even if some enormous contention arises among them.
117 Cronus’ son has made every generation of our house single:
118 Arcesius fathered one only son, who was named Laertes;
119 then in his turn my grandfather fathered only Odysseus;
120 only myself he begot in the halls, then left without joying;
121 therefore now countless malicious men gather here in my household.
122 All of the nobles who rule in the islands surrounding our country,
123 both in Dulichium, Same, and forest-grown Zacynthus,
124 likewise all who hold power in rugged Ithaca’s borders,
125 all of them court my mother and ruin my house in the process.
126 She can neither refuse the detested marriage nor bring it
127 ever to settlement; meanwhile, eating, they lessen my household,
128 and they will very soon tear me limb from limb also.
129 Yet these matters in truth lie now on the knees of the godheads.
130 Father, go quickly now to prudent Penelope; tell her
131 I am alive and uninjured and I have returned here from Pylos.
132 I shall remain in this place, but you must return here to meet me,
133 once you have told her alone; let none of the other Achaeans
134 learn the report, for many among them are plotting to hurt me.”
135 Then in reply, Eumaeus the swineherd, you spoke to Telemachus:
136 “I understand and consider; your charge is addressed to a thinker.
137 Come, however, explain one thing to me fully and truly:
138 should I go on the same journey as messenger also to old lord
139 Laertes, unhappy man, who till lately, although he was mourning
140 greatly for lord Odysseus, still would oversee all the fieldwork,
141 eating and drinking at home with the slaves when his spirit commanded?
142 Now, however, from when you went in the vessel to Pylos,
143 people say that he has not eaten or taken his drink as aforetime,
144 nor overseen the work, but with groaning and wailing he sits there,
145 mourning, and round his bones his flesh is decaying and wasting.”
146 Then in reply wise-minded Telemachus spoke to the swineherd:
147 “Sad though it is, we shall leave him alone despite all of our sorrow.
148 If the entire choice could be taken by mortals themselves here,
149 first we should choose the day my father returns to his country.
150 Once you have carried the message, come straight back here; do not wander
151 over the fields to find him. But tell my mother to send her
152 woman who serves as the housekeeper out with all possible swiftness,
153 secretly; she can carry the news to the old man directly.”
154 Thus he spoke, rousing the swineherd, who took up his sandals
155 into his hands, tied them under his feet, and departed for town there.
156 Nor did Athena fail to perceive that Eumaeus the swineherd
157 left the steading; she now drew near in the bodily likeness
158 of a tall and beautiful woman accomplished in glorious handiwork.
159 There by the opposite doorway she stood and appeared to Odysseus;
160 neither Telemachus saw her in front nor perceived that she stood there,
161 since it is not to all human beings that gods show themselves plainly.
162 Only Odysseus and all the dogs saw Athena; not barking,
163 whining instead, they fled to the other side of the steading.
164 Then with her eyebrows she nodded; lord Odysseus noticed,
165 walked from the hut, going out by the towering wall of the courtyard,
166 stopped there before her, and gray-eyed Athena addressed him:
167 “Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus, master of many
168 stratagems, now tell your son what is true and no longer conceal it,
169 so that, devising together destruction and death for the suitors,
170 both of you go to the glorious city. I shall not be absent
171 long from you two, for I also am eager to enter the battle.”
172 Thus having spoken, Athena touched him once with her gold wand.
173 First she put round his chest a thoroughly clean mantle and tunic,
174 then made his body larger and brought back the bloom of his young years.
175 Dark was his color again and his cheeks grew firm and extended;
176 round his chin the beard became a deep blue-black darkness.
177 Having accomplished these things, she left once again, while Odysseus
178 went in the hut. His beloved son was struck with amazement;
179 frightened, he cast his eyes to the side, lest a god stand before him;
180 then, lifting his voice, he spoke these swift-winging phrases:
181 “Different now, stranger, you seem from the person you were a short while past.
182 Different clothing you wear, and the skin on your body is altered.
183 Surely you are one of the gods who inhabit the wide sky above us;
184 show us your favor, that we may make grateful sacrifice to you,
185 giving you skillfully fashioned gifts made of gold. O spare us.”
186 Then the much-enduring noble Odysseus spoke in his answer:
187 “I am no god; why do you compare me with those who are deathless?
188 No, I am your father, the cause for whose sake you are groaning and bearing
189 manifold griefs as you suffer the violence of arrogant menfolk.”
190 Thus having spoken, he kissed his son and let from his cheek fall
191 downward a tear to the earth; till then he had always restrained it.
192 But Telemachus, not yet persuaded this man was his father,
193 once more gave him an answer and spoke these words in his hearing:
194 “No, you are not Odysseus my father; a god is beguiling
195 me, so I groan in lamentation now with a still greater sorrow.
196 No mortal man could somehow accomplish this thing by devising
197 it with his mind alone, unless a god should approach him in person,
198 easily making him young at his will or an elderly person.
199 Truly, a moment ago you were old and were wearing vile garments;
200 now you resemble the gods who possess the broad reaches of heaven.”
201 Then Odysseus of manifold counsel addressed him in answer:
202 “My Telemachus, now that your loving father is here, it is not right
203 either to wonder beyond all measure or stare in amazement.
204 No other man Odysseus ever will come to you here now;
205 I am that man, after suffering evils and wandering widely;
206 I have come in the twentieth year to the land of my fathers.
207 All this work was performed by Athena, the giver of plunder;
208 she has the power to make me appear in whatever way she desires:
209 sometimes alike to a beggar, and then at another time like a
210 young man wearing beautiful clothing over his body.
211 Easy it is for the gods who possess the broad reaches of heaven
212 both to exalt any mortal or else to disfigure and harm him.”
213 Thus having spoken, he sat himself down; but the young Telemachus
214 flung his arms round his excellent father and wept in his sorrow,
215 shedding his tears; in the hearts of them both rose longing for mourning.
216 Shrill were the cries they uttered and louder than cries of the great birds,
217 sea-eagles or vultures with crooked talons, whose children
218 farmers have taken away before ever the young could be fledged;
219 so from beneath their brows they poured forth pitiful teardrops.
220 Even the light of the sun would have set as they sorrowed together,
221 had not Telemachus spoken at once to his own loving father:
222 “Father, what kind of a ship and what sailors have carried you hither,
223 bringing you into Ithaca? Who did they claim as their people?
224 Surely I cannot imagine that you have come here upon foot.”
225 Then the much-enduring, noble Odysseus spoke to Telemachus:
226 “Therefore, my child, I shall tell you the whole of the truthful account now.
227 Men of Phaeacia carried me, sailors renowned, who convey all
228 people who ever arrive in their country and stand in their presence.
229 Carrying me while I slept in the swift ship over the sea-swell,
230 they set me down in Ithaca, giving me glorious presents,
231 bronze and gold in abundance and finely woven apparel.
232 All of the gifts are lying in caves by the will of the godheads;
233 now I have come here under the guidance and counsel of Athena,
234 so we may plan the destruction and slaughter of all of our foemen.
235 Come then, number the suitors for me and explain them in full detail,
236 so I may know both how many there are and the kind of men they are;
237 then, after taking counsel within my own blameless spirit,
238 I shall consider whether the two of us can stand against them,
239 we two alone without others, or whether we need to seek helpers.”
240 Then in reply wise-minded Telemachus spoke to his father:
241 “Father, indeed I have always heard of your mighty renown there,
242 both as a spearman strong with your hands and a prudent deviser;
243 yet you have spoken too greatly; I stand here seized with amazement,
244 for never can two men contend against many powerful warriors.
245 Suitors do not number precisely ten or twice that number,
246 but many more; you will quickly learn their number among us.
247 Out of Dulichium come fifty and two of the chosen
248 young men, while six diligent serving men follow beside them;
249 out of Same are four and twenty men in their number;
250 out of Zacynthus come twenty Achaean youths in their number;
251 out of Ithaca twelve, all best of the men in the island.
252 Medon the herald is there with them, and the godlike singer,
253 also two serving men who are skilled in carving the meat there.
254 If we should meet all these when they are inside the palace,
255 I fear your vengeance for violence then will be bitter and dreadful.
256 Yet if you can consider some helper to stand in defense here,
257 think of the man who would eagerly help us with all of his spirit.”
258 Then the much-enduring noble Odysseus spoke to Telemachus:
259 “Therefore I now shall tell you, and you must consider and listen:
260 judge whether Athena together with Zeus the Father
261 will be enough for us two, or if I must think of another.”
262 Then in reply wise-minded Telemachus spoke to his father:
263 “Excellent surely are those two defenders whom you have spoken,
264 though they sit high in the clouds, for the two of them rule even other
265 men in their strength and likewise the gods who are deathless forever.”
266 Then the much-enduring noble Odysseus spoke to Telemachus:
267 “Surely the two will not stand far off for a long time around us,
268 once the force of the battle between us and all of the suitors
269 there in my palace is tested and judged by the power of Ares.
270 You must go to our home when the dawn shows her light in the morning,
271 mingling there with the suitors who carry their outrage too proudly;
272 later the swineherd will guide me along to the city behind you,
273 looking like nothing more than a wretched and elderly beggar.
274 If they dishonor me there in the house, let the heart in your bosom
275 suffer and bear it, although I am suffering evil before you,
276 even if they drag me out by the feet through the house to the doorway,
277 or if they pelt me with missiles; you must endure as you watch it.
278 Tell them, indeed, to cease from their folly and insolent actions,
279 speaking to each in mild words; but they will not be persuaded,
280 since the day allotted by destiny already stands near them.
281 Something else I shall tell you, and you must lay it inside you:
282 once Athena of many counsels inspires my spirit,
283 I shall nod with my head to you, and as soon as you notice,
284 take up every weapon of Ares that lies in the halls of the palace,
285 carry them all to the innermost part of the high upper chamber,
286 every last one; but if the suitors should question their absence,
287 cheat them with soothing words when they ask you and miss them, by saying:
288 ‘Far from the smoke I laid them away, for they no longer seemed like
289 arms which Odysseus left when he went long ago toward Troia;
290 rather, they all are defiled by the breath of the fire reaching round them.
291 Cronus’ son has put also a greater concern in my spirit,
292 lest, when the wine has possessed you and quarrels arise in your gathering,
293 you may wound one another, dishonoring feast and your courtship;
294 iron itself has a way of drawing a man to take hold of it.’
295 Only for us two leave behind two swords and a pair of
296 spears and two oxhide shields which our hands can take up in the battle,
297 so we may rush on the suitors and seize them; then the immortals,
298 Pallas Athena and Zeus of the counsels, will dazzle their senses.
299 Something else I shall tell you, and you must lay it inside you:
300 if in truth you are mine and the blood that we share is within you,
301 let no one hear that Odysseus now is inside of the household;
302 let neither Laertes know it, nor even the swineherd who serves us,
303 neither a man of the household nor even Penelope’s own self.
304 Only the two of us must discover the mind of the women;
305 also we might put some of the men who are servants on trial,
306 learning who honors and fears us within the heart of his bosom,
307 also the one who is careless and scorns a man such as you are.”
308 Then in reply his illustrious son made answer and told him:
309 “Father, I think you will learn the heart that is mine for yourself soon,
310 now and hereafter; for no heedless folly possesses me ever.
311 Yet I do not think that this plan will result in advantage
312 for the two of us; I urge you also to give it consideration.
313 Long and in vain you will go about putting each man to trial,
314 visiting farm-work, while suitors at ease in the palace
315 ravenously consume our possessions in pride without pity.
316 Yet I do urge you to learn who among all the women are servants
317 without guilt and who among them dishonor and scorn you.
318 As for the men across all the farms, I would not be willing
319 that we should test them now; let us labor at that later,
320 if you indeed know something, a sign from aegis-bearing Zeus.”
321 So in this manner they spoke these things to each other together.
322 Meanwhile, into Ithaca sailed the well-fashioned vessel
323 carrying from Pylos Telemachus and all of his comrades.
324 When at last they had come to the innermost part of the deep bay,
325 they drew up the black-hulled vessel onto the dry land,
326 while their impetuous servants carried their weapons away there;
327 then they carried the glorious gifts straight to Clytius’ household.
328 Meanwhile, they sent a herald ahead to the house of Odysseus,
329 bringing the news they would give to prudent Penelope, telling
330 how Telemachus stayed at the farm and commanded the vessel
331 onward to sail to the city, so that the strong-hearted lady
332 would not fear in her mind and shed the soft tears from her eyelids.
333 On the same errand the herald and noble swineherd encountered
334 one another, for both bore the same report to the woman.
335 When they arrived at the house of the godlike lord Odysseus,
336 standing among the serving women, the herald announced there:
337 “Queen, your beloved son has already returned from his journey.”
338 Standing close by, the swineherd spoke to Penelope also,
339 telling her all that her own dear son had commanded him tell her.
340 Soon as he told her the whole of the charge and had finished his message,
341 back to the swine herds he went, leaving fence and palace behind him.
342 All of the suitors grieved, and their hearts were cast down in dejection;
343 out of the house they went past the towering wall of the courtyard,
344 taking their seats there outside, in front of the doors of the palace.
345 First to begin was Eurymachus, son of Polybus, saying:
346 “Friends, an enormous exploit has been arrogantly accomplished
347 here by Telemachus, this journey; we said he would not see it finished.
348 Come then, let us launch the best black ship we can find in the harbor;
349 gather the fishermen in as her oarsmen, that they may swiftly
350 carry word out to those men to hasten at once toward homecoming.”
351 Scarcely the whole of the sentence was spoken when lord Amphinomus
352 turned from his place and saw the ship inside of the deep bay,
353 some of them furling the sails while others held oars in their two hands.
354 Sweetly he laughed out loud and addressed the companions around him:
355 “Let us not send any message now, for the men are inside here.
356 Either one of the gods has told them the news or they themselves saw their
357 vessel sail by, and although they pursued it they could not catch it.”
358 Thus he spoke; and rising, they went to the shore of the sea there.
359 Swiftly they drew up the black-hulled vessel onto the dry land,
360 while their impetuous servants carried their weapons away there.
361 All in a body they went to assembly, and would permit no
362 other to sit there with them, neither younger nor elderly townsman.
363 Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke in their hearing:
364 “Gods! How the gods have released this man from the grip of affliction!
365 Day after day our scouts sat high on the windy projections,
366 taking their turns without cease; at every descent of the sunlight,
367 never ashore did we spend the night, but over the sea-swell
368 always we sailed in the swift ship, waiting for radiant Dawn there,
369 lying in ambush for young Telemachus, meaning to seize and
370 kill him; but meanwhile a spirit had carried him safely to home shores.
371 Here, then, let us devise a miserable death for Telemachus,
372 lest he escape from us now; for I do not think that our business
373 ever can reach its end while this one remains here among us.
374 He himself has knowledge and wisdom in counsel and purpose;
375 people no longer display their full measure of favor toward us.
376 Come then, before he can gather Achaeans together and bring them
377 into assembly—for I do not think he will let the thing pass now,
378 but he will rise up in anger and tell all the people among us
379 how we were weaving his precipitous murder but failed to attain him;
380 once they have heard of our evil deeds, they will not give approval.
381 See that they do us no harm and drive us away from our country,
382 making us come to the people and country of some other nation.
383 Let us be first to seize him away from the city, out farming,
384 or on the road; and then let us take his living and holdings,
385 sharing it fairly among us, while giving his houses to his own
386 mother to keep and whatever man should make her his wedded wife.
387 Yet if this purpose displeases you, and you prefer that he keep on
388 living and holding entire the wealth that he has from his fathers,
389 let us no longer gather within and consume all his pleasant
390 property. Rather, from each of our houses let each man pursue her,
391 courting the woman with bride-gifts; then may she marry the suitor
392 giving the most, if he comes as the husband allotted by fortune.”
393 Thus he spoke, and all of the men fell silent together.
394 Then Amphinomus spoke in their midst and addressed the assembly,
395 splendid son of Nisus, the lord who descended from Aretias;
396 he from Dulichium, rich in its grain and its meadows, had come there,
397 leading the suitors, and more than the others he pleased Penelope
398 greatly in speech, for his mind was endowed with a good understanding.
399 Thinking for their good, he stood and addressed the assembly:
400 “Friends, I myself would not be willing to kill young Telemachus;
401 terrible surely it is to murder a kingly descendant.
402 First let us seek to discover the counsels and will of the godheads.
403 If the ordinances spoken by Zeus the mighty approve it,
404 I shall kill him myself and command all the others to help me;
405 yet if the gods turn us away, I command that you cease from the purpose.”
406 Thus Amphinomus spoke, and his counsel delighted the others.
407 Straightway then they arose and went to the house of Odysseus;
408 coming inside, they sat themselves down on the well-polished benches.
409 Meanwhile, prudent Penelope thought of another intention:
410 she would appear to the suitors who carried their insolent outrage.
411 She had learned of the death being planned for her son in the palace;
412 Medon the herald had told her the plan, for he heard all their counsels.
413 Into the hall she went with her serving women beside her.
414 When the most noble of women arrived where the suitors were gathered,
415 there by the pillar that held up the closely constructed roof-tree
416 she stood, holding the glistening veil before both her cheeks there.
417 Then she rebuked Antinous, calling him by name as she told him:
418 “Arrogant, evil deviser Antinous! People have called you
419 best among all of your peers in the land of Ithaca’s people,
420 both in counsel and speech; but you have not lived up to that praise.
421 Madman, why are you weaving the death and the doom of Telemachus,
422 caring nothing for suppliants, whose witness is Zeus the Father?
423 It is not holy for anyone here to devise evil against others.
424 Do you not know how your father came fleeing to this place among us,
425 fearing the people? Their anger was great and was fiercely enkindled,
426 since with pirates of Taphos he followed and brought sorrow on
427 the Thesprotians, who were friends allied with us; they desired
428 to kill him and tear the dear life from the depths of his body,
429 and to consume all the great, heartening wealth he had gathered;
430 yet Odysseus held them back and restrained them, eager though they were.
431 Now you devour that man’s house without payment, you court his own wife and
432 murder his son, while you cause me greatly to grieve in my spirit.
433 I command you to stop and command all the others to cease now.”
434 Then in reply Eurymachus, son of Polybus, spoke to Penelope:
435 “O daughter of Icarius, Penelope prudent and thoughtful,
436 take heart; do not let any of these things weigh down on your thinking.
437 No such man is alive now, nor will he exist or be born here,
438 one who will lay violent hands on your son Telemachus,
439 while I remain alive and look on the earth with my two eyes.
440 Thus I declare to you, and the word will surely be finished:
441 straightway his dark-flowing blood will flow out around my own spearhead,
442 since the city-destroyer Odysseus in former times often
443 sat me upon his knees, put fire-roasted meat in my two hands,
444 and held the red wine out to me there when I sat in the palace.
445 Therefore Telemachus is by far the dearest to me of all men;
446 never do I command him to tremble at death from the suitors.
447 Death from a god is a thing that no human being can flee from.”
448 Thus he encouraged her, while he fashioned destruction against her son there.
449 Up to the glorious rooms on the uppermost floor she ascended;
450 then she lamented Odysseus, the husband she loved, till Athena,
451 gray-eyed goddess, poured sweet sleep on her eyelids and stilled her.
452 Evening brought the noble swineherd to lord Odysseus
453 and to his son; they readied the meal in orderly fashion,
454 slaughtering one yearling pig. But Athena, approaching,
455 came near and stood by Odysseus, Laertes’ son;
456 struck him with her wand, made him once again an old man,
457 and put pitiful clothes on his body, lest ever the swineherd should know him,
458 seeing him clearly, and go with the news to the prudent Penelope,
459 unable to hold back the thing in the heart of his bosom.
460 First of the men Telemachus spoke to the swineherd, saying:
461 “Here you have come, Eumaeus. What news do they tell in the city?
462 Have the overbearing suitors already come in from their ambush,
463 or do they still keep watch for me as I travel back homeward?”
464 Then in reply, Eumaeus the swineherd, you spoke to Telemachus:
465 “I had no care to inquire into these things or question the people
466 while I went through the city; my spirit commanded me quickly,
467 once I had carried the message, to hurry back here from the palace.
468 Yet a swift messenger met me who came from the crew of your vessel,
469 herald who first had announced the report to your mother before me.
470 Something else I can tell you, because I beheld it myself there.
471 High above town, where the hill of Hermes is standing, I journeyed
472 onward, when I saw a swift ship running down toward our harbor;
473 many men were aboard it, crowded together inside her;
474 heavily laden with shields and with double-edged spears she was sailing.
475 I imagined that these were the men, but I cannot know surely.”
476 Thus he spoke; the sacrosanct strength of Telemachus smiled then,
477 looking at his father but keeping the glance from the eyes of the swineherd.
478 Soon as they ceased from the work and the feast lay ready before them,
479 all of them feasted, and no heart lacked its equitable portion.
480 Soon as they cast off desire for the drinking and eating before them,
481 then they remembered the bed and took hold of the gift of their slumber.
