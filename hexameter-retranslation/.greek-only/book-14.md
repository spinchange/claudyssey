# The Odyssey — Book XIV

*Odysseus comes to the loyal swineherd Eumaeus.*

1 Leaving the harbor, he climbed the rough trail upward and inland,
2 over the wooded terrain, through the heights where Athena had told him
3 he would discover the excellent swineherd, who more than the others
4 cared for the wealth of the house that godlike Odysseus had gathered.
5 There in the forecourt he found him seated; a lofty enclosure
6 stood there, built on a spot commanding a view of the country,
7 handsome and spacious and circular. This the swineherd had founded
8 wholly himself for the pigs while his master was absent and wandering,
9 without his mistress’s help or the help of the old man Laertes,
10 using enormous dragged stones, and he crowned it with wild-pear branches.
11 Outside, this way and that, he drove a continuous paling,
12 closely and thickly set, from the black heartwood he had split out of oak trunks.
13 Inside the yard he constructed twelve pens for housing the pig herds,
14 close to each other, their sleeping places; and into each pigpen
15 fifty pigs sleeping low on the ground were penned in each pigsty,
16 all of them breeding sows; but the boars slept outside of the main yard,
17 fewer by far, for the godlike suitors diminished their number,
18 eating them: always the swineherd sent them the best of his fattened
19 young pigs, carefully chosen from all of the herd for their table.
20 There were three hundred and sixty boars in the herd that remained there.
21 Close by, four dogs kept sleeping, resembling beasts in their fierceness,
22 dogs that the swineherd had bred himself, a leader among men.
23 He was fitting a pair of sandals around his own feet,
24 cutting a fine-colored oxhide. His fellow herdsmen had scattered,
25 each on a different errand, accompanying separate herds of the pigs,
26 three of the four; but he sent the fourth unwillingly cityward,
27 forced to conduct a pig to the arrogant suitors’ banquet,
28 so they could slaughter the beast and sate their desire upon swineflesh.
29 Suddenly bark-loving dogs caught sight of Odysseus approaching.
30 All of them charged him at once with a clamor; but wary Odysseus
31 quietly sat on the ground, and the staff fell away from his fingers.
32 There by his own farmstead he would have suffered a shameful disaster,
33 had not the swineherd run hard on his swift feet after the watchdogs,
34 darting out through the gateway; the leather fell out of his fingers.
35 Shouting, he drove all the dogs, each one in a different direction,
36 pelting them thickly with stones. Then he spoke to his lord, though he knew not:
37 “Old man, a moment ago my dogs came near to destroying
38 you on the spot, and you would have covered my name with dishonor.
39 Even without that, the gods have granted me sorrows and mourning.
40 Grieving and weeping here for a master as godlike as any,
41 I sit tending the fattened pigs to be eaten by others,
42 while he, perhaps in need of a meal, is wandering somewhere,
43 through the cities and countries of men who speak foreign languages,
44 if he is still alive and beholds the brightness of sunlight.
45 Come now, follow me into the hut, old man, so you also,
46 after your heart has had its fill of bread and of wine there,
47 may tell where you come from and all the sorrows that you have endured.”
48 So having spoken, the noble swineherd led him inside there,
49 brought him in, seated him, and spread thick brushwood beneath him,
50 then spread over it skin from a shaggy wild goat for a bed-cover,
51 his own large, densely tufted sleeping hide. Odysseus rejoiced then,
52 glad of the welcome he gave, and he spoke aloud and addressed him:
53 “May Zeus grant you, my host, and the other immortal gods with him,
54 all you desire the most, for receiving me here with such kindness.”
55 Then in reply, swineherd Eumaeus, you spoke and addressed him:
56 “Stranger, it is not right for me, though a poorer man came here,
57 ever to slight any guest; all beggars and strangers are Zeus’s.
58 and the gift that is ours may be small but is welcome and precious;
59 this is the portion that falls to slaves, for such is their custom,
60 always in fear whenever their masters possess the dominion,
61 masters still young. For the gods have fastened the homeward day of my master,
62 who would have loved me well and bestowed possessions upon me,
64 house and a plot of my own and a wife much sought after in marriage,
63 all that a generous lord will grant to a servant who serves him,
65 one who has labored hard, when a god gives increase to his labor,
66 just as the labor I stay with here has increased and has prospered.
67 Greatly my lord would have blessed me if he had grown old here among us.
68 No, he is lost! Would to heaven that Helen’s whole breed had been ruined,
69 brought to its knees, since she loosened the knees of so many good men there.
70 He too departed, defending the honor of lord Agamemnon,
71 bound for Ilios, famed for horses, to battle the Trojans.”
72 So he spoke, briskly tucking his tunic under his waistband,
73 then went off to the sties where the tribes of his pigs had been penned there.
74 Taking two from the pens, he brought and slaughtered them both there,
75 singed them, cut them apart, and then on the spits he impaled them.
76 Roasting the whole, he carried and set it before Odysseus,
77 hot on the spits themselves, and scattered white barley meal on it.
78 Then in a bowl of ivy he mixed honey-sweet wine for drinking,
79 sat down opposite him, and urged him aloud as he spoke there:
80 “Eat what the servants have here, old stranger, our young pigs only;
81 as for the fattened hogs, those suitors consume them and gorge them,
82 keeping no watch in their hearts for divine indignation or pity.
83 Blessed gods have no pleasure in pitiless deeds and oppression;
84 justice and orderly works among mortals are what they honor.
85 Even hostile and lawless men who descend on the country
86 of foreign people, when Zeus has granted them plunder to carry,
87 load up their ships and depart to return again homeward in safety;
88 even in them a powerful fear of vengeance will settle.
89 These men surely know something, or heard some voice of a god tell
90 of his miserable death; they refuse an honorable courtship,
91 yet will not go to their homes, but remain here easy and idle,
92 savaging all his possessions in violence, never with mercy.
93 Every night and day that proceeds from the hands of the high god,
94 never just one victim is slaughtered, nor merely a pair here;
95 arrogant hands draw off his wine and diminish it wantonly.
96 Boundless indeed was his wealth, for no other hero possessed such,
97 no one upon the dark-soiled mainland, and no one in Ithaca;
98 not even twenty men altogether could muster such riches.
99 Listen, and I shall tell you the number his wealth had amounted to:
100 twelve great herds on the mainland, as many flocks of the sheep there,
101 as many droves of pigs, and as many broad herds of the she-goats,
102 tended alike by hired foreigners and his own herdsmen.
103 Here on the island eleven far-ranging herds of the she-goats, all of them,
104 pasture the outermost ground, and excellent men oversee them.
105 Each of the men drives one goat down every day for the suitors,
106 whichever he sees is the best of the well-fed goats he is tending.
107 I am the one who watches and guards these pigs that you see here,
108 sorting the finest one out and sending it off to the suitors.”
109 So he spoke. Odysseus earnestly ate the meat and drank wine there,
110 ravenously, saying nothing, and planted disaster for suitors.
111 When he had dined and strengthened his inward heart with the meal there,
112 Eumaeus filled up and handed to him the cup he was using,
113 brimming with wine. He accepted and felt his spirit delighted,
114 then he addressed the swineherd aloud with the following winged words:
115 “Friend, who purchased you once with the wealth that he had for his household,
116 he who was rich and powerful, the man you have just been describing?
117 You say he died in defense of the honor of lord Agamemnon.
118 Tell me, for perhaps I might know of a man such as this one.
119 Zeus in the heavens knows, and all the immortal gods with him,
120 whether I saw him and could bring news; I have wandered through many lands.”
121 Then in reply spoke the swineherd, a leader among all the workmen:
122 “Old man, no wanderer ever could come and announce that man’s coming,
123 then make his wife and dear son believe the report he was bringing.
124 Wanderers needy of care tell lies without purpose or conscience,
125 never desiring to give an account that is truthful and honest.
126 Anyone wandering into the land and people of Ithaca
127 goes to my mistress and tells her deceptive stories and fables.
128 Kindly she welcomes and loves him, then asks him of every small detail,
129 while, as she mourns, the tears drop down from the lids of her eyes there,
130 as is the custom and right of a wife when her husband has perished.
131 Soon enough even you, old man, might fashion a story,
132 if someone gave you a mantle and tunic to serve you as clothing.
133 As for Odysseus, swift dogs and birds must already have torn all
134 skin from the bones of his corpse, and the spirit has left him forever;
135 or else fish in the sea ate him, and his bones on the mainland
136 lie in the sand of the shore beneath a great drift that enfolds them.
137 So he has perished somewhere, leaving sorrow behind for his loved ones,
138 all of us, most of all me; for however far I may travel,
139 never again shall I find such a gentle and kindly master,
140 not if I came once more to the home of my father and mother,
141 where I was born at the first, and where they themselves reared me.
142 Even for them I do not mourn now as greatly, although I
143 long to behold them again with my eyes in the land of my fathers;
144 longing for absent Odysseus takes possession and wastes me.
145 Stranger, I am ashamed to name him, though he is not present;
146 he loved me beyond all others and cared in his heart for my welfare.
147 Though he is far from here, I address him as ‘brother beloved.’”
148 Then much-enduring, godlike Odysseus answered Eumaeus:
149 “Friend, since you utterly say no, refusing to think he is coming,
150 and your heart will always remain unbelieving within you,
151 I shall not make you the promise lightly, but bind it with oath-words:
152 homeward Odysseus comes. Let the fee for the good news be given
153 instantly, after the man has returned to his own halls and household:
154 clothe me in tunic and mantle, beautiful garments together;
155 desperate though I may be, I would never accept them beforehand.
156 Hateful to me as the gates of Hades is that man forever
157 who, surrendering to his need and poverty, tells lies and deceives you.
158 Zeus be my witness first among gods, and this table of strangers,
159 also the hearth of blameless Odysseus, to which I have come here:
160 every single thing I am telling you now will be brought to completion.
161 In this very turning of seasons Odysseus comes here,
162 while one moon is waning and while the succeeding moon rises;
163 home he returns and will punish whoever dishonors his household,
164 treating his wife with contempt and his glorious son with dishonor.”
165 Then in reply, swineherd Eumaeus, you spoke and addressed him:
166 “Old man, neither shall I pay you a good-news reward here,
167 nor will Odysseus ever return to his home. Go on calmly,
168 drink, and let us remember some other things now; do not force me
169 ever to think about this: the heart in the depths of my breast here
170 aches whenever a man recalls my excellent master.
171 Let us indeed put aside your oath. May Odysseus only
172 come as I wish in my heart, and as circumspect Penelope wishes,
173 also the aged Laertes and godlike Telemachus with them.
174 Now I am mourning inconsolably over the son Odysseus
175 fathered, Telemachus; gods had reared him alike to a sapling,
176 and I said that among men he would never prove worse than his dear father,
177 marvelous both in the form and the bodily beauty he carried.
178 But some immortal has harmed the good understanding within him,
179 or else a man has done it: he went to hear news of his father,
180 traveling on to sacred Pylos; but arrogant suitors
181 ambush his road as he journeys home, so that utterly nameless
182 out of Ithaca vanishes lineage of godlike Arcesius.
183 But let us leave him there, whether captured or able to flee them,
184 if perhaps Zeus son of Cronus extends his hand to defend him.
185 Come now, old man, tell your own afflictions and sorrow,
186 and tell this to me truly as well, so I clearly may know it:
187 who are you, whence among men? Where are your city and parents?
188 What kind of ship carried you? By what means did sailors convey you
189 hither to Ithaca? What was the land they claimed as their homeland?
190 Surely I do not imagine you came to this island on foot here.”
191 Then the resourceful lord Odysseus answered the swineherd:
192 “All these things I shall tell you now with exactness and candor.
193 Would that the two of us had provisions enough for a long time,
194 also sweet wine, remaining inside of the hut and enjoying
195 feasting in peace, while others outside attended their labors;
196 easily then, though I spoke for the course of an entire year here,
197 I could not make an end of recounting the griefs of my spirit,
198 all the sufferings I have borne by the will of immortals.
199 I claim descent from the broad land Crete and its people,
200 born as the son of a prosperous man; and numerous other
201 sons had been born and were reared in his palace, a number of children,
202 legitimate sons of his wife; but the mother who bore me was purchased,
203 kept as his concubine. Still, equal honor with trueborn children
204 Castor son of Hylax gave me—the father whose blood I inherit.
205 Castor in those days lived like a god among Cretan people,
206 honored for happiness, wealth, and the excellence shown by his sons there.
207 Then the death-spirits came and carried him off at his ending,
208 down to the house of Hades; his high-hearted children divided
209 all of his livelihood, casting the lots for their several portions.
210 Small was the share they gave me, and they allotted a dwelling.
211 Still, by my merit I won for a wife the child of a rich man,
212 lord of a wealth of land, for I was neither worthless nor craven,
213 never a fugitive out of war; but all of that now has departed.
214 Still, from the stubble you see, you can judge what the harvest had once been,
215 though great suffering now besets me and weighs on me sorely.
216 Truly Athena and Ares had given me courage in battle,
217 also the power to break men; whenever I chose for an ambush
218 all of our finest men, sowing evils among our foemen,
219 never did death stand out to the eye of my high-hearted spirit.
220 Always I bounded in front of the rest and took with my spearpoint
221 any hostile man whose feet were slower than mine there.
222 Such was my being in war; but toil was never my pleasure,
223 nor did I care for the management nourishing splendid children.
224 No, my delight was always in ships fitted out with their oarlocks,
225 fighting and wars and well-polished javelins, arrows in volleys—
226 grievous things that are terrifying to everyone else in the world here.
227 Those were the things I loved, which some god set deep in my nature;
228 different men find joy in the doing of different labors.
229 Before the sons of Achaea ever had landed at Troia,
230 nine times I led war bands and ships that were swift on the seaway
231 out against foreign people, and great wealth fell to my portion.
232 Out of the plunder I chose what pleased me; much more was allotted
233 afterward. Quickly my house increased, and so among Cretans
234 I became both a man to be feared and a man to be honored.
235 But when far-thundering Zeus devised the abominable journey,
236 that which loosened the knees of so many men in the battle,
237 then they commanded illustrious Idomeneus and me with him
238 both to conduct our ships to Ilios; no way appeared then
239 we could refuse, so strongly the people’s hard clamor constrained us.
240 There for nine years we Achaean sons kept fighting the Trojans;
241 then in the tenth, after sacking the city of Priam, we journeyed
242 home with the ships, but a god scattered all the Achaeans.
243 Yet for unhappy me Zeus counseled nothing but evils.
244 One single month I remained, taking joy in my children,
245 joy in my wedded wife and possessions. Afterward, yearning
246 urged me to sail far southward and make my way into Egypt,
247 once I had fitted the ships with companions as godlike as any.
248 Nine ships I fitted; the company gathered together swiftly.
249 Six whole days afterward my faithful companions continued
250 feasting; and I provided a wealth of sacrificial victims,
251 both for the gods to receive and themselves to prepare for the banquet.
252 Then on the seventh we boarded and sailed from the broad land of Crete there,
253 borne by a North Wind fair and fresh, blowing steadily onward,
254 easily, just as though we moved on the flow of a river.
255 None of my ships took harm, but unharmed and untouched by illness
256 there we sat, while the wind and the helmsmen steadily steered them.
257 On the fifth day we came to the river of beautiful Egypt;
258 there in the Egyptian river I moored all my balanced vessels.
259 Then I commanded my trusted companions to stay by the ships there,
260 close at their sides to watch and preserve them, while I urged our scoutsmen
261 onward to take up their posts on the places of highest observance.
262 Yielding instead to arrogance, trusting only their war strength,
263 swiftly they ravaged the beautiful fields of Egyptian people,
264 carrying off all the wives and the innocent children in bondage,
265 killing the men. Soon cries of alarm reached the city.
266 Hearing the shouting, they came with the first appearance of morning;
267 all of the plain filled up with footmen and horses in numbers,
268 bronze flashing brightly; and Zeus, who delights in the thunder,
269 cast a disastrous panic among my men, and not one man
270 dared stand facing the foe, for destruction stood everywhere round them.
271 Many of us they killed on the spot with the pitiless bronze there;
272 others they carried alive to labor for them under compulsion.
273 Then father Zeus himself put this resolution within me—
274 would that I died and fulfilled my doom on the soil of the country,
275 there in the land of Egypt, since more distress lay before me—
276 instantly taking the well-made helmet away from my forehead,
277 shield from my shoulders, I flung the spear from my hand to the ground there;
278 then I approached the king’s own chariot, meeting it head-on,
279 seized his knees and kissed them. He saved me and pitied my sorrow,
280 seated me there in his car and bore me home as I wept on.
281 Many indeed of his men made rushes at me with their ash-spears,
282 longing to kill me because they were terribly angry against me,
283 but he kept them away; he revered the wrath of the high god,
284 Zeus of the stranger, who most of all hates pitiless actions.
285 There for seven years I remained in the land of the people,
286 gathering many possessions from them, for all of them gave me.
287 But when the eighth returning year came on in its season,
288 then a Phoenician came, a man well versed in deception,
289 greedy for gain, who had done much evil among human people.
290 He persuaded and carried me off with him, till together we entered
291 Phoenicia, where his dwelling and his possessions were lying.
292 There in his house I stayed for a year till the seasons were rounded.
293 But when the months and the days had reached their appointed completion,
294 after the year had turned on itself and the seasons were coming,
295 then for Libya he had me board his seafaring vessel,
296 planning a lie: I was there to help convey his cargo,
297 but he would sell me there and obtain a limitless purchase.
298 Though I suspected, I followed aboard under force of compulsion.
299 Onward she ran with the North Wind, beautiful, fresh, and unbroken,
300 over mid-Crete, and Zeus was designing destruction against them.
301 But when at last we had left Crete and no other country
302 showed to the eye, but only the sky and the sea were around us,
303 then son of Cronus planted a dark-blue cloud on the heavens,
304 over the hollow ship, and the sea went dark underneath it.
305 Zeus together thundered and struck the ship with his lightning;
306 all of her shivered and reeled when struck by the bolt of the high god,
307 filling with sulfur; her crewmen fell from the ship into water.
308 Like black crows they were carried among the waves round the black hull;
309 thus did the god take homecoming away from them all there.
310 Yet Zeus himself, although I carried my heart full of sorrow,
311 placed in my two hands the towering mast of the dark-prowed
312 ship, so that even yet I might escape from disaster.
313 Twining myself around it, I rode on the murderous windstorms.
314 Nine days I drifted; the tenth came on with the darkness of nighttime,
315 when a great rolling wave brought me to Thesprotian country.
316 There Pheidon, ruler and king of the Thesprotians, received me,
317 freely, demanding no price; for his own dear son, on arriving,
318 found me oppressed by exposure and weariness, led me homeward,
319 raising me up by the hand till we came to the halls of his father.
320 Round me he wrapped both a mantle and tunic, garments for clothing.
321 There I heard news of Odysseus. Pheidon asserted he hosted
322 him as a friend and honored him while he went to his homeland.
323 He showed me all of the property gathered there by Odysseus,
324 bronze and gold in abundance and iron exceedingly well wrought.
325 Riches enough to support his heirs to the tenth generation,
326 so great were the treasures the lord had stored in the king’s halls.
327 Pheidon said he had gone to Dodona, intending to listen,
328 out of the lofty crown of the oak, for the counsel of great Zeus,
329 how he should come again to the fertile country of Ithaca,
330 after so long an absence, in public or hidden from all men.
331 Pheidon swore it to me in his house while pouring libations:
332 now the ship was launched and companions were fitted and ready,
333 those who would carry Odysseus home to the land of his fathers.
334 But he dispatched me first, for a seagoing ship was departing,
335 manned by Thesprotian men for Dulichium, rich in its grainland.
336 Pheidon commanded the men to convey me with care to Acastus,
337 king of that place, but they took evil pleasure in planning against me,
338 meaning to plunge me still deeper in sorrow and utter disaster.
339 But when the seafaring ship had gone a long way from the mainland,
340 straightway they plotted to bring the day of enslavement upon me.
341 First they stripped off my mantle and tunic, the clothing I wore there,
342 cast these wretched rags around me and gave me a tunic,
343 torn into pieces, the very ones you can see with your own eyes.
344 Evening came as they reached the fields of far-visible Ithaca.
345 There in the well-benched ship they bound me hard to the timbers,
346 using a strongly twisted rope, while they disembarked there,
347 hurriedly taking their supper beside the strand of the seashore.
348 But the immortals themselves with ease unbent all my binding.
349 Covering over my head and my face with the rag I was wearing,
350 down by the polished stern-rope I slid, drawing close to the seawater,
351 then breast-deep in it rowed with both of my hands as I swam there;
352 swiftly I made my escape outside of the reach of them all there.
353 There I came up where a thicket stood in the flower-rich woodland,
354 curled up and lay there. They roamed around with a terrible wailing,
355 searching; but it did not appear it would profit them further
356 still to continue the search, so they went back again and departed,
357 boarded the hollow ship. But the gods themselves hid me from sight there,
358 easily, leading and bringing me close to the stead of a good man,
359 one who is skilled in his work, for my portion is still to keep living.”
360 Then you replied, swineherd Eumaeus, speaking your answer:
361 “Ah, poor stranger, you truly have stirred the heart in my body,
362 telling me all the things you have suffered and places you wandered.
363 Yet what you say of Odysseus seems neither orderly nor true;
364 nothing you tell will persuade me. Why should a man such as you are
365 idly tell falsehoods like these? I know very well for myself now
366 what fate attends my master’s return: all gods must have hated
367 him beyond measure, since they did not strike him down among Trojans,
368 nor in the arms of his friends when he finished and folded the warfare.
369 Then the united Achaeans would all have constructed his grave-mound,
370 and he would have won for his son great fame among those born after.
371 Now the Harpies have snatched him ingloriously, leaving no tidings.
372 I keep away from the town, living here with my pigs in the country;
373 never I go to the city unless discerning Penelope
374 urges me there when some message has come from a faraway quarter.
375 There the people sit round it and question each detail of rumor,
376 both those grieving because of the lord who is long gone and absent,
377 also those taking delight as they eat his wealth without payment.
378 As for myself, I take no pleasure in asking and probing,
379 since an Aetolian man once deceived my heart with a story,
380 one who had killed a man and had wandered over wide countries,
381 coming at last to my home; and I welcomed him warmly and cherished.
382 He said that among the Cretans he saw Odysseus with Idomeneus,
383 mending the ships that the hurricane blasts had shattered together.
384 Then he declared he would come in the summer or harvest-time season,
385 bringing a wealth of treasure, with comrades as godlike as any.
386 You too, old man of sorrows, whom some god led to my dwelling,
387 do not attempt to win my favor or charm me with falsehoods.
388 That is not why I shall honor and love you here in my homestead,
389 but from my fear of Zeus of the stranger and pity upon you.”
390 Then the resourceful lord Odysseus answered Eumaeus:
391 “Surely the heart in your breast is exceedingly slow to believe me,
392 since I have not made you yield or persuaded you even by swearing.
393 Come now, let us arrange an agreement; afterward, both our
394 witnesses shall be the gods who possess the expanse of Olympus.
395 If your lord should return to this house and enter his homeland,
396 dress me in tunic and mantle and send me onward to travel
397 into Dulichium, there where I long in my heart to be going.
398 But if your master does not come home again as I tell you,
399 set your slaves upon me and cast me down from a high cliff,
400 so that the next poor beggar will shun the practice of lying.”
401 Then in reply the noble swineherd spoke and addressed him:
402 “Stranger, fine fame and virtue indeed I would win among people,
403 both from this moment onward and all through the ages that follow,
404 if after leading you into my hut and giving you guest-gifts,
405 afterward I should kill you and take the dear life from your body!
406 Freely indeed I could afterward pray to Zeus son of Cronus.
407 Now it is supper time; I wish my companions were home here
408 soon, so that in the hut we might make a delectable supper.”
409 Thus they were speaking together and answering one another.
410 Close to them now came the swine and the men who attended the pig herds.
411 First they enclosed all the females inside their customary sties there,
412 settling them down for the night; an immeasurable clamor
413 rose from the swine being penned. Then the noble swineherd commanded:
414 “Bring in the finest pig, so I may offer it up for the stranger,
415 one who has come from afar; and we ourselves also shall profit,
416 we who have suffered so long for the sake of these white-tusked creatures,
417 while other men consume all our labor and pay us no wages.”
418 So he spoke, splitting firewood then with the pitiless bronze blade;
419 they led in an exceedingly fat five-year-old boar there.
420 Then they stood him beside the hearth; and the excellent swineherd
421 did not forget the immortal gods, for his mind was upright.
422 Taking the first-cut hairs from the white-tusked boar, he threw them
423 into the fire and prayed to all of the gods who are deathless,
424 asking that wise Odysseus come to his own home again there.
425 Raising a billet of oak he had left from his cleaving, he struck him;
426 life left the boar. Then they slit his throat and singed him,
427 swiftly dismembered the carcass. The swineherd set raw portions,
428 taking the first from every limb, in a wrapping of rich fat;
429 some in the fire he cast after sprinkling barley meal on them.
430 Cutting the rest, they fastened the pieces on spits for the roasting,
431 carefully roasted them all, then drew them off of the spit-rods,
432 heaping them closely together on boards. Then the excellent swineherd
433 rose to divide them—for righteous measure was known to his judgment.
434 Cutting, he portioned the whole into seven equal divisions.
435 One he set for the Nymphs and for Hermes, the son of Maia,
436 praying; to each of the others he gave an appropriate portion.
437 Then with the long, unbroken chine he honored Odysseus,
438 taken from the white-tusked boar, and gladdened the inward heart of his master.
439 Then the resourceful lord Odysseus spoke and addressed him:
440 “Would you were dear to father Zeus, Eumaeus, as you are
441 dear now to me, for you honor me well though I am as I am here.”
442 Then, Eumaeus the swineherd, you spoke this answer before him:
443 “Eat now, stranger, and take your delight in the portions before you,
444 such as is present; a god will grant one thing and withhold one,
445 all as he wills in his heart, for a god can accomplish all things.”
446 So he spoke, then offered the firstlings to gods everlasting;
447 pouring a libation of bright wine, he set it in the hands of
448 city-destroying Odysseus, who sat beside his own portion.
449 Bread was distributed there by Mesaulius, whom the swineherd
450 purchased himself alone while his master was absent and wandering,
451 without the aid of the mistress or aid of the old man Laertes;
452 from the Taphians he bought him, paying from his own possessions.
453 All of them reached their hands to the good things lying before them.
454 When they had put aside desire for drinking and eating,
455 then Mesaulius removed their bread, and the others, replenished
456 fully with bread and meat, made haste to lie down for slumber.
457 Night came over them, moonless and foul; Zeus rained without ceasing
458 all through the darkness, and wet West Wind blew strongly forever.
459 Then spoke Odysseus, testing the swineherd carefully, whether
460 he might strip off his mantle and give it to him, or command some
461 other companion to do so, because of his care for the stranger:
462 “Listen to me, Eumaeus, and all of you other companions.
463 Boasting a little, I shall tell a story; foolish wine drives me,
464 wine that has urged even men of exceeding wisdom to singing,
465 caused them to laugh in a tender way and has sent them to dancing,
466 even let fly some word that was better left unexpressed there.
467 But since I have cried aloud at the first, I shall not conceal it.
468 Would I were young in this way and the strength in my limbs were enduring,
469 as on the night we prepared and led an ambush below Troy.
470 Lord Odysseus led us along with Atreus’ son Menelaus;
471 I was the third commander with them, for they had commanded.
472 But when at last we came near the city and high wall of Troia,
473 we in the dense brushwood surrounding the city lay hidden,
474 down in the reeds and the marsh, crouched low underneath our armor.
475 Then came a bitter night, since the North Wind had fallen upon us,
476 freezing; and overhead the snow came thick as the hoarfrost,
477 bitterly cold, and ice crust formed all around on our shields there.
478 All of the other men had mantles and tunics for cover,
479 sleeping in comfort, their shoulders enveloped under the bucklers.
480 But when I came with my comrades, I left my mantle behind me,
481 foolishly, since I had not supposed I would suffer from cold there,
482 but I had followed with only my shield and my glittering waist-guard.
483 But when the night was a third part spent and the stars had passed over,
484 then I addressed Odysseus there, who was lying beside me,
485 nudging his arm with my elbow; he listened to me in a moment:
486 ‘Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus, master of cunning,
487 I shall not long be among the living; the winter is crushing
488 me, for I have no mantle; a spirit deceived me, and so I
489 came with only my tunic. Escape is no longer before me.’
490 So I spoke, and he framed this scheme in the depths of his spirit,
491 being a man so able alike in planning and warfare.
492 Speaking in low-pitched voice, he addressed me then with an answer:
493 ‘Silence now, so no other Achaean can hear you among us.’
494 So he spoke, then propped his head on an elbow and called out:
495 ‘Listen, my friends, a dream came over me here from a god’s hand.
496 We have come much too great a distance away from the vessels.
497 Would there were someone to tell Agamemnon, shepherd of people,
498 whether he might send more from the ships to reinforce us.’
499 So he spoke. Then Thoas rose, son of lord Andraemon,
500 swiftly, and laid his purple-colored mantle beside him,
501 then set off at a run for the ships; and I in his garment
502 happily lay there till gold-throned Dawn brought light to the heavens.
503 Would I were young in this way and the strength in my limbs were enduring;
504 someone here at the swineherds’ stead might give me a mantle,
505 both out of friendship and out of respect for a worthy man here.
506 Now they dishonor me since I have wretched clothes on my body.”
507 Then, swineherd Eumaeus, you answered his speech with these words:
508 “Old man, faultless indeed is the tale you have told and recounted;
509 not one profitless word beyond proper measure was spoken.
510 So you shall lack neither clothing nor any other provision
511 proper to give a suffering suppliant when one encounters,
512 now; but at morning you must wrap your own rags again round you.
513 We do not have many cloaks nor changes of tunics among us,
514 here to be worn, but only a single garment for each man.
515 But when the dear son of Odysseus comes home among us,
516 he will provide you with tunic and mantle, garments for clothing,
517 send you wherever the heart and spirit within you command you.”
518 So having spoken, he sprang up and laid out a bed near the fireside,
519 casting on top of it hides from the sheep and the goats of the farmstead.
520 There Odysseus lay; and he cast a mantle upon him,
521 densely woven and large, which he kept as a spare one beside him,
522 always to wear whenever a terrible storm should arise there.
523 So in that place Odysseus slept, and there beside him
524 slept all the youthful men; but the swineherd took no pleasure
525 sleeping apart from the pigs in that place, away from his charges.
526 Outside he went, equipping himself, and Odysseus rejoiced then,
527 glad that he cared so much for the wealth of his long-absent master.
528 First he cast his sharp sword around his powerful shoulders,
529 then put on a mantle defending against wind, densely woven;
530 over it all he took the hide of a large and well-fattened goat there,
531 then took a pointed spear, his defense against dogs and against men.
532 Then he went out to lie where all of the white-tusked porkers
533 slept underneath an overhung rock, in shelter from North Wind.
