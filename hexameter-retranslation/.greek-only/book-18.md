# The Odyssey — Book XVIII

*The fight with Irus; Penelope appears before the suitors.*

1 Then there approached a beggar who begged from all of the people,
2 roaming throughout all Ithaca’s town, and famous for ravenous
3 hunger, continually eating and drinking; he had neither sinew
4 nor any strength, though the bulk of his visible body was massive.
5 Arnaeus had been his name, for his lady mother had named him
6 that from his birth; but the young men all used to call the man Irus,
7 since he would carry a message whenever somebody commanded.
8 Coming upon Odysseus now, he tried to expel him from his own
9 household, and berating him spoke these winged words and addressed him:
10 “Yield, old man, from the doorway, before you are dragged by the foot out.
11 Do you not see how everyone there gives a wink in my favor,
12 urging me now to drag you? For all that, I feel some shame still.
13 Rise, then, before our quarrel turns to a contest of hands here.”
14 Looking at him from under his brow, resourceful Odysseus answered:
15 “Strange man, I do you no harm here, neither in deed nor in speaking,
16 nor do I grudge what another may give you, though you take plenty.
17 This threshold here has room enough for the two of us beggars;
18 you have no need to begrudge another the goods that belong to
19 others. You seem like a wandering man, as I am; but only
20 gods can bestow prosperity. Do not challenge me overmuch with your hands,
21 or, old though I am, I will soil both your breast and your two lips
22 crimson with blood. Then greater would be my peace on tomorrow,
23 since I do not think you would come back here at any time after,
24 entering again the palace of Laertes’ son Odysseus.”
25 Irus, the wandering beggar, was angry and answered Odysseus:
26 “Gods, how the shameless glutton can prattle, his speech tripping lightly,
27 just like an old crone tending an oven! I would devise some evil against him,
28 striking with both of my hands and knocking all of his teeth out,
29 down from his jaws like the teeth of a sow that devours the grain harvest.
30 Gird yourself now, so all these men can tell us apart when
31 we go to battle; but how can you fight with a much younger man here?”
32 So they there at the front of the tall doors glared at each other,
33 standing upon the polished threshold, bristling fiercely.
34 Antinous’ mighty and sacred strength soon perceived them;
35 laughing with pleasure, he spoke to the suitors and called them together:
36 “Dear friends, nothing like this has ever occurred to us so far,
37 such is the sport that some god has brought to the house for our pleasure.
38 Irus and this new stranger are wrangling now with each other,
39 eager to fight with fists; come, let us bring them together.”
40 So he spoke; they all sprang up together with laughter,
41 gathering around the two beggars dressed in their miserable clothing.
42 Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke to the suitors:
43 “Listen to me, proud suitors, that I may tell you a proposal.
44 Goat stomachs here lie waiting beside the fire, and we have
45 filled them with fat and blood and laid them away for our supper.
46 Whichever beggar wins and proves himself best in the contest,
47 let him rise up himself and select the one that he wishes;
48 always from now on he will share our feasting, nor shall we allow any
49 other beggar inside to mingle with us and petition.”
50 So spoke Antinous; all of them found his proposal pleasing.
51 Resourceful lord Odysseus spoke with deception among them:
52 “Friends, there is no way for an old man to fight with a younger
53 man when misery breaks him; and yet my belly within me
54 urges me wickedly on, to be mastered by blows in the contest.
55 Come now, all of you swear me a powerful oath and assurance:
56 no one in favor of Irus shall lift a heavy hand against me
57 and strike in wantonness, forcing me down with his strength to assist him.”
58 So he spoke, and all of them swore the oath he demanded.
59 Then, when they had sworn and brought the oath to fulfillment,
60 Telemachus’ mighty and sacred strength spoke again to the gathered:
61 “Stranger, if your heart and the bold spirit within you are urging
62 you to drive this man off, then fear no other Achaean.
63 Whoever strikes you must fight against many men all together;
64 I am your host, and the kings who approve me are here to support you,
65 both Antinous and Eurymachus, each one thoughtful in counsel.”
66 So he spoke; and all approved him. Odysseus meanwhile
67 girded the rags round his loins and revealed the shape of his thighs there,
68 beautiful, massive; broad shoulders appeared on the hero,
69 wide was the chest and powerful the arms; meanwhile Athena,
70 standing nearby, made larger the limbs of the shepherd of peoples.
71 All of the overbearing suitors marveled beyond measure;
72 looking at one who stood at his side, each man would speak this:
73 “Irus will soon be Irus no longer, with trouble upon him,
74 such is the powerful thigh that the old man displays from his tatters.”
75 So they spoke, and the spirit of Irus was terribly shaken.
76 Nevertheless, the serving men girded and led him by force there,
77 frightened; the flesh on his limbs was trembling all over.
78 Then Antinous rebuked him, calling him out and addressing him:
79 “Now, you ox-gulleted fool, would you never had lived or been born here,
80 if you tremble at this man so and are fearfully stricken,
81 though he is old and afflicted with hardship that fell to his portion.
82 This I will tell you, and certainly it will be brought to fulfillment:
83 if this stranger defeats you and proves himself best in the contest,
84 I will throw you into a black ship, sending you over
85 onto the mainland, straight to King Echetus, mutilator of mortals.
86 He will cut off your nose and your ears with the pitiless bronze there,
87 tear off your genitals too, and give them to dogs to be eaten raw.”
88 So he spoke, and trembling took hold of the man’s limbs more strongly.
89 Into the middle they brought him; both men lifted their two hands.
90 Long-enduring, godlike Odysseus pondered his choices:
91 whether to strike such a blow that the life would abandon the fallen
92 man where he lay, or hit him lightly and stretch him out on the ground there.
93 Thinking it over, he judged that this was the better decision:
94 lightly to strike, lest the watching Achaeans should recognize him.
95 Then, when their hands were raised, Irus struck at his right shoulder;
96 under the ear Odysseus struck him in turn on the neck and
97 crushed the bones inward; immediately crimson blood filled his mouth there.
98 Down in the dust he fell with a moan, and his teeth struck together,
99 kicking the earth with his feet; meanwhile the proud suitors all there
100 raised up their hands and nearly died laughing. Odysseus meanwhile
101 seized him by one foot and dragged him through the forecourt into the courtyard,
102 over the porch to its doorway; he propped him against the enclosing
103 wall of the court as he sat, then thrust the staff in his hand there.
104 Calling to him, he spoke these winged words and addressed him:
105 “Sit where you are now, driving the pigs and the dogs from the courtyard.
106 Do not attempt to be ruler of strangers and beggars,
107 wretched yourself, lest you meet with something still worse than your beating.”
108 Over his shoulders then he threw the shameful provision
109 sack, full of holes and tatters; its strap was a twist of coarse cord.
110 Back to the threshold he went and seated himself; and the suitors
111 entered the hall again with sweet laughter, welcoming him warmly:
112 “May Zeus grant you, stranger, and all of the other immortals,
113 all you desire the most, whatever is dear to your spirit,
114 since you put an end to that insatiable beggar’s
115 begging among us; soon we will send him away to the mainland,
116 straight to King Echetus, mutilator of all mortal people.”
117 So they spoke; lord Odysseus rejoiced in the word as an omen.
118 Then Antinous set a huge goat stomach beside him,
119 filled with fat and blood; Amphinomus lifted and brought him
120 two loaves of bread from the basket and set them beside him.
121 Greeting him with a golden cup, he spoke out and addressed him:
122 “Hail, old father and stranger, and may you have prosperity
123 later, although at this time you are held in many afflictions.”
124 Resourceful lord Odysseus answered Amphinomus, saying:
125 “Amphinomus, you truly appear to me thoughtful and prudent,
126 sprung from a father like yours, since I heard the excellent fame of
127 Nisus of Dulichium, who is said to be noble and wealthy.
128 You are declared to be born of the man, and appear to be gentle.
129 Therefore now I will tell you; pay heed to my words and attend me:
130 Earth does not nourish anything feebler than the human
131 being, of all that breathe on the earth and move over ground there.
132 For a man never believes he will suffer an evil hereafter,
133 while the gods grant him excellence still and his knees have their vigor.
134 Yet when the blessed gods bring woeful days to fulfillment,
135 then, although unwilling, he bears them with steadfast spirit.
136 Such is the mind of all humankind that inhabit the earth here,
137 changing according to each day the father of gods and men brings.
138 Once I too was destined to prosper among my companions,
139 yet I committed much recklessness, yielding to violence and power,
140 putting my trust in my father and all of my brothers.
141 Therefore let no man at any time be wholly lawless,
142 rather in silence accept every gift that the deities give him.
143 Such are the reckless designs I can see these suitors devising,
144 wasting the wealth and treating the wife with scorn and dishonor,
145 wife of a man whom I say will not remain far from his loved ones
146 and from the land of his fathers for long; he is very near you.
147 May some power conduct you home, and may you not meet him
148 when he comes back again to the dear native land of his fathers,
149 for I believe there will not be a bloodless parting between them,
150 those suitors and him, when he passes beneath the roof of his palace.”
151 So he spoke, poured an offering, then drank the honey-sweet wine.
152 put the cup once again in the hands of the marshal of people.
153 Then Amphinomus walked through the house with sorrow heavy in his heart,
154 shaking his head, for his spirit foresaw the disaster before him.
155 Yet not even so did he flee his doom, for Athena bound him
156 also to fall underneath Telemachus’ hands and his powerful spearhead.
157 Back once more he went and sat on the chair from which he had risen.
158 Then the goddess Athena, whose eyes shine brightly, implanted
159 deep in the spirit of Icarius’ daughter, thoughtful Penelope,
160 purpose to show herself to the suitors, that she might stir up
161 all of the suitors’ hearts most strongly and win even greater
162 honor than ever before from her husband and son in the household.
163 Laughing an idle laugh, she spoke to her servant and named her:
164 “Eurynome, my heart has come to desire, as never before now,
165 that I appear to the suitors, although I detest them entirely;
166 I could give to my child as well some words that are better
167 for him, not always to mix with the overbearing suitors,
168 men who speak well to his face but plot only evil behind him.”
169 Then Eurynome, keeper of stores, in answer addressed her:
170 “Yes indeed, dear child, all your words have been spoken in measure.
171 Go now, speak to your son, and do not conceal your intention,
172 after you wash your body and rub some oil on your two cheeks.
173 Do not go as you are with your face completely disfigured
174 under the tears; it is worse to go mourning forever without cease.
175 Now your son has reached that age, the one whom you always
176 prayed above all to the deathless gods you might see with a beard grown.”
177 Thoughtful Penelope spoke to the housekeeper, answering:
178 “Eurynome, do not counsel me so, although you are caring,
179 telling me now to wash my body and rub it with olive oil;
180 all the gods who possess Olympus destroyed every splendor
181 mine when that man went away in the hollow seagoing vessels.
182 Go now, tell both Autonoe and Hippodameia
183 they are to come and stand at my side when I enter the palace.
184 I will not go among men alone, for I feel shame to do so.”
185 So she spoke, and the aged woman went out through the great hall,
186 telling the serving women and urging them swiftly to join her.
187 Thereupon bright-eyed Athena conceived yet another intention:
188 over Icarius’ daughter she poured down sweetness of slumber.
189 Leaning backward, Penelope slept, and all of the joints in
190 her body relaxed on the couch; meanwhile the brilliant goddess
191 gave her immortal gifts, so the watching Achaeans would marvel.
192 First with ambrosial beauty she cleansed the beautiful features,
193 using the kind of ointment that Aphrodite, beautifully garlanded,
194 rubs on herself when she goes to the lovely dance of the Graces.
195 She made Penelope taller to see and fuller in figure,
196 even made her whiter than ivory newly cut by the saw-blade.
197 Having accomplished all, the brilliant goddess departed.
198 White-armed serving women came in from the hall there,
199 bringing a noise as they entered, and sweetness of slumber released her.
200 Wiping her cheeks with her hands, she spoke these words and addressed them:
201 “Strange how a gentle sleep has enclosed me, despite all my sorrow.
202 Would that Artemis, holy goddess, would now in this manner
203 give me a death just as gentle, that I might no longer in sorrow
204 waste all my life in the heartache of longing after my dear lord’s
205 excellence of every kind, for he far surpassed the Achaeans.”
206 So she spoke and descended the glittering upper chamber,
207 not alone, for two of her serving women accompanied her.
208 When she, the brilliant among women, arrived at the suitors,
209 there by the pillar that held the close-made roof she stood firmly,
210 holding the shining veil before either cheek as she waited.
211 One devoted attendant stood at each side beside her.
212 There the suitors’ knees went slack, and desire enchanted their spirit;
213 all of them prayed to lie down beside her upon their bedclothes.
214 Then she addressed Telemachus, her own beloved son, saying:
215 “Telemachus, no longer are your mind and your thoughts firmly founded.
216 Even while still but a child, you turned shrewd plans in your spirit;
217 now, when you are fully grown and have reached the measure of young manhood,
218 so that a stranger might say you were born as the son of a prosperous father,
219 looking upon your height and your beauty, though knowing you only,
220 neither your mind nor your purpose is proper and rightly proportioned.
221 Think what a thing has occurred in the halls at this moment,
222 since you permitted the stranger to suffer this shameful abuse here.
223 What if a stranger sitting as guest in the halls of our household
224 suffered such pain as this from a brutal and violent dragging?
225 Yours would the shame and reproach be among all humanity.”
226 Telemachus, full of his good sense, answered her, saying:
227 “Mother, I do not fault you for being enraged at this outrage.
228 Yet in my own heart I understand and know every matter,
229 both what is good and worse; but before I was still only childish.
230 Still I am not capable now of deciding all things with prudence;
231 these men seated around me strike my thoughts from one side and another,
232 plotting me evil, while no one comes to assist or defend me.
233 Yet the fight that the stranger and Irus fought did not happen
234 by the suitors’ will, and the stranger prevailed in his power.
235 Father Zeus, Athena and lord Apollo, if only
236 suitors here in our palace now could be beaten in that way,
237 nodding their heads in defeat, some out in the court of the household,
238 others inside the hall, with the limbs of them each one loosened,
239 just as Irus now by the doors that open into the courtyard
240 sits there nodding his head like a man who is deeply besotted with wine,
241 unable to stand upright on his feet or return to his household,
242 homeward, wherever that was, since all of his dear limbs had loosened.”
243 Thus did the two there talk over these things with one another.
244 Eurymachus then spoke out to Penelope, saying:
245 “Daughter of Lord Icarius, circumspect Penelope,
246 if all Achaeans throughout Iasian Argos could see you,
247 many more suitors would feast in the halls of your house every morning,
248 since you surpass other women by far, above all in the fullness
249 of beauty and stature and balanced thought held within you.”
250 Thoughtful Penelope spoke in response to Eurymachus, saying:
251 “Eurymachus, my excellence, beauty of form, and my figure
252 all were destroyed by the deathless gods when the Argives ascended
253 into Ilium, and with them went my husband Odysseus.
254 If he returned and cared for the goods and the life of my household,
255 so would my fame become much greater and finer among you.
256 Now I grieve, for a power has driven such evils upon me.
257 Surely the time when he went away from the land of his fathers,
258 holding my right hand there at the wrist, he spoke and addressed me:
259 ‘Wife, I do not believe that every Achaean in greaves will
260 come back safely and free from harm out of Troy to his homeland,
261 since they say that the Trojans too are men who are fighters,
262 both with a spear and a bowstring, showering arrows, and riders
263 mounted on swift-footed horses, who very quickly determine
264 any great quarrel arising in warfare that levels all people.
265 Therefore I do not know if a god will return me or I am
266 destined to fall in Troy; let all things here be your concern now.
267 Keep my father and mother in mind here within the palace,
268 just as you do at this time, or more while I am away from them.
269 Then, when you see that the child has grown and a beard clothes his two cheeks,
270 marry whatever man you desire, and leave your household behind you.’
271 Such were his words, and now everything is coming to fullness.
272 Night will arrive when hateful marriage encounters the woman
273 wretched and ruined—me, whose prosperity Zeus has wholly taken away.
274 Yet there is one cruel grief that comes to my heart and my spirit:
275 this was never the custom of suitors in times before this,
276 those who desire to court a noble woman, the daughter
277 born of a wealthy man, and compete against one another;
278 these men themselves bring cattle and powerful sheep from their homesteads,
279 making a feast for the maiden’s friends and bestowing magnificent presents;
280 gifts; they never devour another’s goods without payment.”
281 So she spoke; long-enduring, godlike Odysseus rejoiced then,
282 since she drew out their gifts while beguiling each one of their spirits
283 with gentle words, though the mind within her intended another.
284 Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke to Penelope:
285 “Daughter of Lord Icarius, circumspect Penelope,
286 any gift an Achaean is willing to carry before you,
287 take it, since it is not becoming to turn down a gift that is offered.
288 We will not go to our work, nor anywhere else from the household,
289 until you marry whichever Achaean appears to be noblest.”
290 So spoke Antinous; all of them found his proposal pleasing.
291 Each sent a herald away to carry his gift from his household.
292 Antinous’ herald returned with a beautiful broad robe,
293 richly embroidered, and fastened upon it were twelve golden
294 brooches, each of them fitted with finely fashioned, curved clasps.
295 Eurymachus’ man straightaway brought an elaborate necklace,
296 fashioned of gold strung together with amber, shining like sunlight.
297 Two of Eurydamas’ servants carried a pair of his earrings,
298 triple-clustered like mulberries; wonderful grace flashed around them.
299 Out from the house of Peisander, the son of Lord Polyctor,
300 one of his serving men carried a splendid ornament worn at the neckline.
301 Each other man of Achaea brought his own beautiful present.
302 Then the brilliant among women went up to the upper chamber,
303 while her serving women accompanied her, carrying the splendid gifts there.
304 Then the men turned toward the dance and the lovely song of the minstrel,
305 taking their pleasure and waiting for evening to come to the household.
306 Black evening fell on them while they continued to take their pleasure.
307 Straightaway they stood three braziers up in the hall of the palace,
308 so they would furnish them light, and about them they piled the dry kindling,
309 long since seasoned and parched, newly split into billets with bronze there,
310 mixing in torches among it; by turns they tended the firelight,
311 serving women of long-enduring Odysseus; but then to the women
312 Zeus-born, resourceful Odysseus spoke these words for himself there:
313 “Serving women of lord Odysseus, long gone away now,
314 go to the inner rooms of the house, where the honored queen is.
315 Whirl your spindles beside her and cheer your queen as she sits there,
316 seated within her room, or card out the wool with your hands there.
317 I will myself provide all these men with the light of the fire.
318 Even if all of them wish to await fair-throned Dawn in the palace,
319 they will not outlast me, for I am a man of long patience.”
320 So he spoke; but the women laughed and looked at each other.
321 Beautiful-cheeked Melantho rebuked Odysseus shamefully,
322 she whom Dolios had fathered, while Penelope cared for the woman,
323 raising her just like a child and giving her playthings.
324 Yet not even so did she share in the grief of Penelope;
325 rather she lay with Eurymachus often and gave him her love there.
326 She now abused Odysseus with words of contempt and addressed him:
327 “Wretched stranger, your mind has surely been struck out of order:
328 you do not want to go to sleep at some blacksmith’s dwelling
329 or at a public shelter, but babble so much in this place here,
330 boldly before these numerous men, and never afraid in your spirit.
331 Surely the wine has got your thoughts, or your mind is
332 always the same as it is at this time, when you talk windy nonsense.
333 Are you beside yourself because you defeated the wandering Irus?
334 Take care that no man better than Irus should suddenly rise here,
335 one who with both of his powerful hands will batter your head there,
336 drive you away from the house, and bespatter your body with bloodshed.”
337 Looking at her from under his brow, resourceful Odysseus answered:
338 “Soon I will go to Telemachus, bitch, and report what you tell me,
339 so he can cut you apart on the spot, carving limb after limb off.”
340 So he spoke and frightened the women away with his harsh words.
341 Through the house they went, and the limbs underneath each woman
342 loosened in terror, for all believed that he spoke with the truth there.
343 Odysseus stood by the braziers, keeping the firelight
344 bright, while he watched every man; but the heart in his breast was
345 turning to other designs in his mind, all destined for fullness.
346 Nor did Athena allow the arrogant suitors entirely
347 to refrain from heart-wounding insults, in order that even more deeply
348 grief might enter the heart of Laertes’ son Odysseus.
349 Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, began speaking among them,
350 mocking Odysseus and raising laughter among his companions:
351 “Listen to me, all suitors of our widely renowned queen,
352 while I speak what the heart here within my chest has commanded.
353 Not without divine aid did this man reach the household of Odysseus;
354 certainly I think that the blaze of the torches comes out of him,
355 down from his head, since not even a little hair grows upon it.”
356 So he spoke; and at once he addressed Odysseus, sacker of cities:
357 “Stranger, would you be willing to work for wages, if I should hire you,
358 there at the farthest edge of my field? Your pay will be ample:
359 gathering stones for a wall and planting trees that grow lofty?
360 There I would furnish abundant grain, an unfailing provision,
361 clothing to wear, and sandals that cover and bind both your feet there.
362 But since all of the work you have learned is harmful, you will not be willing
363 ever to go to a task; you would rather beg through the country,
364 seeking to get enough food for your bottomless belly.”
365 Resourceful lord Odysseus answered Eurymachus, saying:
366 “Eurymachus, if only the two of us had a contest
367 working in springtime, when all the days grow long in the country,
368 out in the meadow, and I had a well-curved sickle to work with,
369 you had one like it as well, so we could be tested in labor,
370 fasting until the dark of the evening, with plentiful grass there;
371 or if there were cattle, the finest of all, for us two to be driving,
372 tawny and massive and both filled full of the grass they had eaten,
373 equal in age and in burden, whose strength had not been diminished,
374 and a four-acre field whose clods would yield to the plowshare:
375 there you would see if I cut a furrow unbroken before me.
376 Or if Cronus’ son stirred battle from somewhere today here,
377 and if I had a shield and a pair of spears for the fighting,
378 also a helmet all bronze and fitted around both my temples:
379 then you would see me among the foremost line of the fighters,
380 nor would you taunt me again with the ravenous belly I carry.
381 But you are greatly abusive, and cruel is the mind you are keeping;
382 you think yourself to be someone important and powerful only
383 since you associate with few men, none of them good or courageous.
384 If Odysseus came and arrived at the native land of his fathers,
385 quickly the doors you behold, though they are exceedingly broad now,
386 would become narrow for you as you fled from the porch to the outside.”
387 So he spoke; Eurymachus grew still more enraged in his spirit,
388 looking at him from under his brow, and addressed him with winged words:
389 “Wretch, I will soon bring evil upon you for what you are saying,
390 bold as you speak before these numerous men, and never afraid in
391 spirit. Surely the wine has got your thoughts, or your mind is
392 always the same as it is at this time, when you talk windy nonsense.
393 Are you beside yourself because you defeated the wandering Irus?”
394 So he spoke, and his hand took hold of a stool; but Odysseus
395 sat himself down by the knees of Amphinomus of Dulichium,
396 frightened of Eurymachus; he hurled it and struck the wine-pourer’s hand,
397 right hand; the jug fell down to the ground with a loud brazen ringing,
398 while the man cried out and fell on his back in the dust there.
399 Then all the suitors made uproar throughout the shadowy palace;
400 looking at one who stood at his side, each man would speak this:
401 “Would that the wandering stranger had died in another country
402 before he came here, so he would not have caused such an uproar.
403 Now we are wrangling over these beggars, and there will be no
404 pleasure in excellent feasting, for evil and baseness are winning.”
405 Telemachus’ mighty and sacred strength spoke again to the gathered:
406 “Strange men, you have gone mad and no longer conceal in your spirit
407 food or the wine you are drinking; some god must be driving you onward.
408 Feast here well, then go to your houses and lie down to slumber,
409 whenever your spirit commands; I drive no man from the palace.”
410 So he spoke; and all of them bit their lips with their teeth there,
411 marveling at Telemachus speaking so boldly among them.
412 Then Amphinomus opened his speech and addressed all the suitors,
413 glorious son of Nisus and grandson of Lord Aretias:
414 “Friends, no man should take offense with hostile answers
415 when what has been spoken is just, nor turn it to violent anger.
416 Do not assault this wandering stranger or any other
417 servant living here in the house of godlike Odysseus.
418 Come now, let the wine-pourer first make offerings in our cups here,
419 so we can pour to the gods and go to our homes for our slumber.
420 Leave this stranger here in the halls that belong to Odysseus,
421 making him Telemachus’ charge; it is his dear house he has entered.”
422 So he spoke, and the word he had given was pleasing to all there.
423 Hero Mulius mixed up wine in a bowl for the company,
424 herald from Dulichium, serving as man to Amphinomus;
425 Standing beside each man in order, he poured it among them;
426 they made libation to all the blessed gods and drank honey-sweet wine.
427 Then, when they had poured and drunk whatever their spirit desired,
428 each man went to his own house, intending to lie down in slumber.
