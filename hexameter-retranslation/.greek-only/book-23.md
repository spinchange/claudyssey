# The Odyssey — Book XXIII

*Penelope and Odysseus know one another again; at dawn he goes to Laertes.*

1 Up to the upper room went the old nurse, laughing in triumph,
2 eager to tell her mistress her own dear husband was home now.
3 Strength filled her knees as she ran, and her feet kept outstripping each other.
4 Over Penelope’s head she stood, and addressed her directly:
5 “Wake, Penelope, dear child, so that you may behold now
6 with your own eyes what you longed for through all of your lifetime.
7 Home is Odysseus; home he has come, though late in returning.
8 He killed the arrogant suitors who preyed on his own household,
9 causing it grief as they ate his wealth and oppressed his son there.”
10 Then wise Penelope spoke in reply to the nurse who had told her:
11 Nurse, dear mother, the gods have made you mad, for they can
12 make even the wisest person senseless and empty of judgment,
13 then set one who is foolish again on the path toward wisdom.
14 They have deranged you; before this your mind was steady.
15 Why do you mock me now, when my heart is burdened with sorrow,
16 telling this senseless tale and awakening me from my slumber,
17 sweet sleep, which held me fast and covered my eyelids around me?
18 Never have I slept thus, not once from the day when Odysseus
19 sailed to behold Accursed Ilion, whose name should be nameless.
20 Go then, descend once more, and return to the hall of the palace.
21 Had any one of the other women who serve and attend me
22 come with this news to me here and rouse me out of my slumber,
23 grimly and swiftly I would have sent her away from my presence,
24 back to the hall below; but old age has given you license.”
25 Then the beloved nurse Eurycleia spoke in reply to her:
26 “No, dear child, I do not mock you; I tell you the truth now.
27 Home is Odysseus; home he has come, as surely as I say—
28 that stranger whom everyone held in contempt in the palace.
29 Long had Telemachus known that his father was there in the household,
30 but in his prudence concealed every plan his father had fashioned,
31 till he could punish the violent pride of the overbearing.”
32 So she spoke; Penelope rejoiced and sprang from her bed then,
33 clung to the aged woman, and tears streamed down from her eyelids;
34 raising her voice, she addressed her then with the following winged words:
35 “Come then, dear mother, and tell me the whole truth without any falsehood.
36 If he has truly returned to his home, as surely as you say,
37 how did he lay his hands upon all of the shameless suitors,
38 one man alone, while all of them stayed together within here?”
39 Then the beloved nurse Eurycleia spoke in reply to her:
40 “Nothing I saw and nothing I learned; I heard only groaning
41 from those being killed. We sat at the back of the strong-fashioned chambers,
42 stricken with fear, while the tightly fitted doors held us prisoned,
43 until at last your son called me away from the great hall,
44 Telemachus; for his father had sent him there to summon me.
45 There I found Odysseus standing among all the slaughtered bodies,
46 standing; around him they lay on the hard, unyielding ground there,
47 heaped on each other. Your heart would have warmed, if only you had seen him.
48 Now they are lying beside the doors that open to the courtyard,
50 all in a heap, while he purifies his beautiful palace,
51 kindling a great fire. He sent me here to summon you.
52 Follow me now, so rejoicing may rise for the two of you both,
53 deep in your own dear hearts, after all the suffering you have borne.
54 Now at last your desire, so long deferred, is accomplished:
55 home to his hearth he has come alive and has found you beside it,
56 found his son in the palace; the suitors who wronged him so cruelly—
57 all of them he has punished at last in his own house.”
58 Then wise Penelope spoke in reply to the nurse who had told her:
59 “Nurse, dear mother, do not exult too loudly in triumph.
60 You know how gladly we all would welcome him into the palace,
61 most of all I and the son whom we two brought into the world here.
62 Yet this report you have brought cannot possibly be the true one;
63 one of the gods everlasting has killed the glorious suitors,
64 angered to see their heart-rending pride and their wicked behavior.
65 Never did they show honor to anyone born upon earth here,
66 neither the good nor the bad, whoever might come and approach them.
67 Thus their own recklessness brought them to grief; but Odysseus
68 lost his return from Achaea afar, and he himself perished.”
69 Then the beloved nurse Eurycleia spoke in reply to her:
70 “Child, what a word has escaped from the barrier formed by your teeth now!
71 Here is your husband at home by the hearth, and yet you deny that
72 ever he will return; your heart forever remains full of disbelief.
73 Come then, I shall give you another unmistakable token:
74 there is the scar a boar once dealt him with one of its white tusks.
75 Washing his feet, I knew it and wanted to tell you directly,
76 but he caught me and covered my mouth with his powerful hands then,
77 would not allow me to speak, for his mind was rich in devices.
78 Follow me now; I will pledge my own life as the price of my promise:
79 if I deceive you, then kill me with death in its pitiless fashion.”
80 Then wise Penelope spoke in reply to the nurse who had told her:
81 “Nurse, dear mother, the plans of the gods who continue forever
82 are hard for you to unravel, however extensive your knowledge.
83 Still we shall go to my son, so that I may see for myself there
84 all of the suitors dead and the man who slaughtered the suitors.”
85 So she descended the stair, while many thoughts crowded her heart there:
86 thoughts came thronging: whether to question her husband at distance,
87 or to stand close beside him, to kiss his head and take his hands there.
88 After she entered the hall and crossed the threshold of stonework,
89 she sat down opposite lord Odysseus, there in the firelight,
90 close by the other wall. He sat beside the tall pillar,
91 looking down as he waited to see whether she would address him,
92 his strong-willed wife, now that she beheld him before her own eyes.
93 Long she sat without speaking; astonishment entered her spirit.
94 Sometimes she looked upon him directly, full in the face there;
95 sometimes she failed to know him because of the wretched clothes there.
96 Then Telemachus chided his mother and spoke out directly:
97 “Mother—cruel mother, whose heart is utterly hardened—
98 why do you hold yourself far from my father, and never sit near him,
99 asking him questions and testing him closely with word after word here?
100 No other woman would keep such an obstinate heart and stand distant
101 thus from a husband who, after suffering terrible hardships,
102 came in the twentieth year to the land of his fathers again here.
103 Yet always the heart in your breast is much harder than stone is.”
104 Then wise Penelope spoke in reply to Telemachus’ chiding:
105 “Child, the heart in my breast is struck with utter amazement.
106 Neither can I address him in speech nor ask him a question,
107 nor can I look in his face outright. But if this is truly
108 lord Odysseus, at home once more, then surely we two shall
109 know one another more surely; for we have signs between us,
110 hidden away from others, which only we two know.”
111 So she spoke, and the much-enduring lord Odysseus smiled then;
112 swiftly he turned to Telemachus, speaking the following winged words:
113 “Son Telemachus, leave your mother alone in the palace,
114 testing me as she will; she soon will recognize better.
115 Now I am foul with grime, with miserable clothes on my body;
116 therefore she shows me dishonor and will not say I am that man.
117 We, though, must now consider the way that all may go best here.
118 Even a man who has killed but one in the midst of the people,
119 even if few avengers remain behind to pursue him,
120 that man flees from his kindred, abandons the land of his fathers.
121 We have destroyed the city’s bulwark, those who were noblest,
122 young men in Ithaca; therefore ponder the trouble before us.”
123 Then wise Telemachus answered his father directly:
124 “You yourself must decide it, dear father; they say that your counsel
125 stands as the best among men, and no other mortal among them
126 ever could strive with you in the art of devising a purpose.
127 Eagerly we shall follow together, nor do I imagine
128 we shall be wanting in courage, so far as our strength can sustain us.”
129 Then resourceful Odysseus answered his son and addressed him:
130 “Therefore I shall tell you the course that appears to be best now.
131 First you must wash yourselves and put clean tunics upon you;
132 bid all the maids in the palace to choose out garments and dress too.
133 Then let the godlike singer, holding his clear-sounding lyre,
134 lead us all in the measures that lovers of dancing delight in,
135 so that whoever outside may hear will think it a wedding,
136 whether he walks down the road or dwells in the houses around us.
137 Let no broad rumor of slaughter sweep through the town any sooner—
138 news of the suitors’ death—before we can all have departed,
139 out to our many-treed farm; when we have arrived there together,
140 we shall consider whatever advantage the Olympian grants us.”
141 So he spoke; they heard him and readily did as he ordered.
142 First, then, all of them bathed and put clean tunics upon them;
143 women arrayed themselves, while the godlike singer had taken
144 up his hollow lyre and awakened in them a longing
145 both for the sweetness of song and the loveliness found in the dancing.
146 Loudly the great house echoed around with the stamping of footsteps,
147 men who were dancing in play and women with beautiful girdles.
148 Thus would somebody say who heard from outside of the household:
149 “Surely a man has married the queen whom many were wooing.
150 Cruel woman! For her lawful husband she could not endure
151 guarding his great house safely and always until he returned home.”
152 So they would speak, but none of them knew what had happened within there.
153 Meanwhile, inside his own house, great-hearted Odysseus
154 was bathed and rubbed with oil by Eurynome the stewardess,
155 draping a beautiful mantle around him and clothing him in a tunic.
156 Then from above on his head great beauty was lavished by Athena,
157 making him taller to see and broader in all of his stature;
158 down from his head she sent thick curls, like the flower of hyacinth.
159 Just as a man overlays silver with gold poured around it,
160 one who is skilled, whom Hephaestus and Pallas Athena instructed
161 in every art, and who finishes works that are full of enchantment,
162 so she poured grace all around his head and his shoulders together.
163 Out of the bath he came, in form like the gods everlasting;
164 back he went to the chair from which he had risen before this,
165 facing his wife once more, and addressed her directly before him:
166 “Strange woman, beyond all others among womankind, you
167 were given an unyielding heart by the gods of Olympus.
168 No other woman would keep such an obstinate heart and stand distant
169 thus from a husband who, after suffering terrible hardships,
170 came in the twentieth year to the land of his fathers again here.
171 Come then, nurse, spread bedding for me, so I may lie down
172 here by myself; for surely her heart is iron within her.”
173 Then wise Penelope answered the words her husband had spoken:
174 “Strange man, I neither exalt myself nor scorn you,
175 nor am I greatly amazed; I know very well how you looked
176 as you went out from Ithaca, boarding the long-oared vessel.
177 Come then, Eurycleia; make ready his strong-built bedstead
178 outside the well-built chamber—the room he constructed himself there.
179 When you have set the sturdy bed there, lay bedding upon it,
180 fleeces and blankets and finely woven coverlets, shining.”
181 Thus did she speak, putting her husband to proof; but Odysseus,
182 deeply troubled, addressed his worthy and faithful-minded bedmate:
183 “Woman, the word you have spoken pains the heart within me!
184 Who has removed my bed? The labor would prove to be difficult,
185 even for one who was skilled, unless some god had come there,
186 willing to move it with ease and set it down in another place.
187 No living mortal man, not even one blooming in young strength,
188 lightly could lever it out, for a powerful token lies hidden
189 in that well-fashioned bed, which I labored at—no one else did.
190 Inside the courtyard enclosure an olive grew, long in its foliage,
191 flowering in its prime, and thick as a pillar in girth there.
192 Round it I built up the chamber until I had fully completed it,
193 fitting the stones close-set, and covered it well with a roof there,
194 adding the close-set doors that were tightly fitted together.
195 Then I lopped off the long-leaved crown of the olive above it,
196 trimmed the trunk upward from its root, working the bronze round about it,
197 well and expertly, and trued it all with the plumb line,
198 shaped it into a bedpost and bored all its surface with augers.
199 Starting with this, I fashioned the bed till I brought it to completion,
200 ornamenting it all with gold and silver and ivory;
201 then I stretched oxhide thongs through the frame, made bright with the crimson.
202 Thus I reveal this token to you; but this I do not know:
203 whether my bed is firm in its place, dear wife, or whether
204 someone has moved it elsewhere, cutting beneath the olive.”
205 So he spoke; her knees gave way where she stood, and her heart melted,
206 knowing the steadfast tokens Odysseus now had revealed to her.
207 Bursting in tears, she ran straight forward, and cast both her arms then
208 round Odysseus’ neck, kissed his head, and addressed him directly:
209 “Do not be angry, Odysseus; in all other matters
210 you were the wisest of men. The gods have assigned us our sorrow;
211 they were unwilling that we should remain with each other together,
212 taking delight in our youth and reaching the threshold of old age.
213 Do not be angry with me for this now, nor resent me
214 since I did not embrace you at once when I first looked upon you.
215 Always the heart in my dear breast shuddered within me,
216 fearing that some mortal might come and deceive me with speeches;
217 for many there are who devise evil gains in their scheming.
218 Never would Argive Helen, the daughter begotten of Zeus,
219 have lain in love and bed with a man from a foreign people,
220 if she had known that the warlike sons of Achaea were destined
221 once more to carry her home to the dear land of her fathers.
222 Surely a god incited the shameful deed she committed;
223 never before had she harbored delusion within her spirit—
224 that ruinous madness from which our sorrow first came upon us.
225 Now, since you have recounted the unmistakable tokens
226 known from our bed, which no other mortal ever has witnessed—
227 only the two of us and one handmaid alone in the household,
228 Actoris, whom my father bestowed when I came to your dwelling,
229 she who guarded for us the doors of the well-built chamber—
230 now you persuade my heart, though stern and unyielding within me.”
231 So she spoke, and in him awakened more longing for weeping;
232 weeping, he held the wife he loved, both faithful and discerning.
233 Just as land is welcome when it appears to men who are swimming,
234 whose well-fashioned ship Poseidon amid the sea waters
235 shattered, when driven along by furious wind and the hard wave;
236 only a few have escaped from the gray sea, making for dry land;
237 swimming, with heavy salt encrusted all over their bodies;
238 gladly they step on the earth, having fled from the evil that chased them—
239 welcome to her was her husband at last as she gazed on his presence;
240 never entirely did her white arms release from his neck there.
241 Dawn with her rose-red fingers would find them still there lamenting,
242 had not the goddess Athena, gray-eyed, devised something other.
243 Long she held back the night at its uttermost edge, while Dawn
244 golden-throned she restrained by Ocean, nor let her yoke horses,
245 swift-footed bearers of light to humankind in their going,
246 Lampos and Phaethon, stallions who carry the goddess of morning.
247 Then resourceful Odysseus spoke to his wife and addressed her:
248 “Woman, we have not yet reached the end of all our trials;
249 still there is measureless labor beyond, a hardship remaining,
250 mighty and hard, and all of it I must bring to completion.
251 Thus did the spirit of Teiresias prophesy to me
252 on that day when I went inside the house of Hades,
253 seeking a homeward return for myself and all of my comrades.
254 Come then, let us go to our bed, dear wife, so that we may
255 sleep there now and take our pleasure in sweetness of slumber.”
256 Then wise Penelope spoke in reply to her husband:
257 “Your bed will always be ready whenever your heart may desire it,
258 now that the gods have caused you at last to make your arrival
259 home to this well-fashioned house and the land of your fathers.
260 Yet since you spoke of the trial, and a god put the thought in your spirit,
261 come, tell me what it will be; for later I think I shall hear it,
262 and to discover it now will surely be nothing the worse.”
263 Then resourceful Odysseus answered his wife and addressed her:
264 “Strange woman, why do you press and urge me so strongly
265 now to recount it? I shall tell you and hide nothing from you.
266 Yet your heart will not rejoice in it; neither am I myself
267 glad, since he ordered me onward through many cities of mortals,
268 going with a well-fitted oar held fast in my hands there,
269 until I come to a people who have no knowledge at all of the sea,
270 men who eat no food that is seasoned or mingled with sea salt;
271 neither do they know ships whose cheeks are painted with crimson,
272 nor well-fitted oars, which serve as the wings of the vessels.
273 This unmistakable sign he gave me; I shall not conceal it:
274 when another traveler happens to meet me along the roadway
275 and says I bear a winnowing fan on my glorious shoulder,
276 then he commanded me to plant my oar upright in the earth there,
277 offering beautiful victims to lord Poseidon the ruler—
278 one ram, one strong bull, and a boar that mounts upon sows there—
279 then to return to my home and offer holy hecatombs
280 unto the gods everlasting who hold the breadth of the heavens,
281 all in their proper order. My death shall come from the sea then,
282 gentle and painless, the sort that will strike me down when it finds me
283 worn out beneath a rich old age; and round me the people
284 all will prosper. He said these things would all be accomplished.”
285 Then wise Penelope spoke in reply to her husband:
286 “If the immortals make your old age more noble and happy,
287 hope remains for you then to escape from all of your troubles.”
288 Thus they spoke to one another in words of this fashion.
289 Meanwhile, both Eurynome and the nurse made ready the bed there,
290 piling it high with soft robes under the light of the torches.
291 After they busily spread and prepared the strong-fashioned bedstead,
292 off to her sleeping-place went the old nurse, back to her quarters;
293 but Eurynome, handmaid who tended the chamber, led them
294 toward their bed, as she carried a torch in her hands to direct them.
295 After she brought them inside their chamber, she went back; and they then,
296 joyful in heart, approached the old rite of their bed there together.
297 But Telemachus now, with the oxherd and also the swineherd,
298 stopped their feet from the dancing and ordered the women to cease too;
299 they themselves laid down to sleep throughout the shadowy palace.
300 When the two had taken delight in the pleasure of lovemaking,
301 then they delighted in stories, which each one told to the other:
302 she told all she had borne in her house, the glorious woman,
303 forced to behold the destructive mob of men who were suitors,
304 who, for her sake, numbers of cattle and vigorous strong sheep
305 slaughtered them; wine by the jarful was drawn from the storage vessels.
306 Zeus-born Odysseus told of the numberless woes he had brought on
307 other men, and the many he suffered himself in his hardship;
308 all he recounted; she listened in joy, and never did slumber
309 fall on her eyelids until he had brought every tale to completion.
310 First he began with the way he subdued the Cicones, and thereafter
311 came to the fertile fields of the men who feed on the lotus;
312 what the Cyclops did, and how he exacted the penalty from him
313 for his courageous comrades, whom he ate without pity;
314 how he came to Aeolus, who welcomed him gladly and warmly
315 sent him away, though fate did not yet let him reach his own homeland,
316 but once again a tempest seized him and carried him outward,
317 over fish-filled seas, while he groaned with the weight of his sorrow;
318 how he came to Telepylus, city of Laestrygonians,
319 those who destroyed his ships and his well-greaved comrades together,
320 all but Odysseus, who fled in his own black ship there alone then;
321 then he recounted the intricate guile and devices of Circe,
322 how he descended inside the mold-dark dwelling of Hades,
323 there to consult the spirit of Theban Teiresias,
324 going in his many-oared ship; and there he saw all of his comrades,
325 also his mother, who bore him and nursed him when he was an infant;
326 how he heard the voices of Sirens singing intensely;
327 how he had reached the Wandering Rocks and terrible Charybdis,
328 Scylla as well, whom men had never escaped from unharmed there;
329 how his companions slaughtered the cattle belonging to Helios;
330 how the swift ship was struck by a thunderbolt, smoking and blazing,
331 hurled by high-thundering Zeus; and his noble companions had perished,
332 all of them there together, while he escaped from the death-dooms;
333 how he came to Ogygia’s isle and the nymph Calypso,
334 who detained him there, desiring to have him for husband,
335 fed him inside her hollow caves and always declared she
336 would make him deathless and ageless through all of his lifetime;
337 still she could never persuade the heart in his breast to accept it;
338 how after numberless toils he reached the people of Phaeacia,
339 who from the heart showed honor to him as though to a god there,
340 sending him home in a ship to the dear land of his fathers,
341 giving him bronze in abundance and gold and garments in plenty.
342 This was the final word that he spoke, before the sweetness of slumber
343 leapt on him, loosening his limbs and the cares from the heart of his spirit.
344 Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena devised something other.
345 Once she had judged in her heart that lord Odysseus now had
346 taken delight in his wife’s bed and likewise delighted in slumber,
347 straight from Ocean she roused the golden-throned early-born Dawn,
348 so she would bring light to mortals; Odysseus in turn awakened,
349 rising from soft bed, and gave this word of command to his bedmate:
350 “Woman, already we have been sated with many ordeals,
351 both of us—you here enduring the grief of my homecoming,
352 weeping; but Zeus and the other gods with sorrow oppressed me,
353 holding me, eager to come, far away from the land of my fathers.
354 Now that the two of us both have reached our beloved bedstead,
355 care for the possessions that belong to me here in the palace.
356 As for the flocks the arrogant suitors have greatly diminished,
357 many I shall plunder myself; still others the lords of Achaea
358 shall give me, until all the folds of my flocks are replenished.
359 Now I shall go to the many-treed farm in the country,
360 there to behold my noble father, who grieves for me deeply.
361 Wife, I give you these orders, though you are prudent already:
362 rumor at once will go with the sun as it rises above us—
363 news of the suitors’ death, whom I killed in the halls of the palace.
364 Go to the upper room with the women who serve and attend you;
365 sit there and neither look upon anyone nor question.”
366 Then he clothed his shoulders round in his beautiful armor,
367 roused up Telemachus, roused both the oxherd and swineherd,
368 bidding them all take hold of the warlike gear in their hands there.
369 None disobeyed him; they clothed themselves in the bronze of their armor,
370 opened the doors and strode out, Odysseus leading before them.
371 Daylight already shone on the earth, but the goddess Athena
372 covered them over with night and led them swiftly out of the city.
