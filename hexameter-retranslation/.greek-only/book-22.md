# The Odyssey — Book XXII

*Odysseus reveals himself and destroys the suitors; the disloyal servants are punished.*

1 Then resourceful Odysseus stripped off the rags from his body,
2 leapt on the broad threshold, holding the bow and his quiver
3 filled with arrows, and poured out the swift-flying shafts at his feet there;
4 then among all of the suitors together he spoke and addressed them:
5 “Now this difficult contest has reached its appointed completion.
6 Now I shall see if I strike one target no man has ever
7 struck yet, granting that I may succeed and Apollo give glory.”
8 Speaking, he aimed the bitter arrow directly at lord Antinous.
9 He was just lifting a beautiful cup to his lips at the moment,
10 golden and two-handled, already turning it there in his fingers,
11 ready to drink of the wine; not a thought of his death filled his spirit.
12 Who among men could imagine that, there among many at dinner,
13 one man alone, though exceedingly strong, would bring down upon him
14 evil death and the dark fate destined to overtake him?
15 Odysseus aimed at his throat and struck it directly with an arrow;
16 straight through the soft neck went the point of the shaft on its journey.
17 Sideways he sagged; from his hand the cup fell away as he was smitten;
18 instantly out through his nostrils a thick jet of blood came
19 flowing, the blood of a man; with his foot he thrust off the table
20 swiftly and struck it away, spilling all of the food on the ground there;
21 bread and the roasted meats were befouled. Then all of the suitors
22 raised an uproar throughout the hall when they witnessed him falling;
23 out of their chairs they sprang, and they raged through the whole of the chamber,
24 looking on every side at the finely constructed walls there;
25 nowhere was any shield or a mighty spear they could seize on.
26 Then they reviled Odysseus there with the words of their anger:
27 “Stranger, your shooting at men brings evil; no other athletic
28 contests will you attempt; now utter destruction is certain.
29 For you have murdered the man who by far was the best of the young men
30 here in Ithaca; therefore the vultures will eat you among us.”
31 Thus every man kept speaking, for each thought he had not meant the killing;
32 fools, they never perceived what they saw: that the bonds of destruction
33 now had been fastened securely around them, every one of them.
34 Looking from under his brows, resourceful Odysseus answered:
35 “Dogs, you believed I would never return to my own home here
36 out of the land of the Trojans, because you were wasting my household,
37 forcing the women who served in my house to lie down beside you,
38 courting my wife while I still was alive and breathing among you,
39 fearing neither the gods who possess the broad reaches of heaven,
40 nor that indignation from men would arise in the days still to follow.
41 Now the bonds of destruction are fastened around every one of you.”
42 Thus did he speak; and pale fear seized all of the suitors together;
43 each of them looked all around for a way to escape sheer destruction.
44 Only Eurymachus answered the king with the words that now follow:
45 “If you indeed are Odysseus of Ithaca, home from your journey,
46 right are the words you have spoken of all that Achaeans accomplished,
47 many outrageous deeds in the palace and many in farmlands.
48 Yet the man who occasioned them all already lies murdered,
49 lord Antinous; he was the one who began these transgressions,
50 not from the need or desire he felt to possess your wife in marriage,
51 rather from other designs that the son of Cronus prevented:
52 that he himself might be king throughout all the well-founded country
53 here in Ithaca, after he killed your son in an ambush.
54 Now he is slain as his fate required; but spare your own people.
55 Later, collecting throughout the land, we will make full repayment,
56 all that was drunk and devoured by us in the halls of your palace;
57 each man bringing a price worth twenty oxen in payment,
58 bronze together with gold we will pay, until your spirit
59 softens; until then none can blame you for bearing your anger.”
60 Looking from under his brows, resourceful Odysseus answered:
61 “Lord Eurymachus, though you should give me all that you have from your fathers,
62 all that is yours at present, and add whatever else from another place,
63 even so I would never restrain my hands from the work of the slaughter
64 till all the suitors have paid for the whole of their sinful transgression.
65 Now there is set before you either to fight me in battle
66 or to escape, if one can avoid both death and his dark fate.
67 Yet I imagine that no man here will escape sheer destruction.”
68 Thus did he speak; and the knees and the hearts of them there were loosened.
69 Then Eurymachus spoke once more to the rest of the suitors:
70 “Friends, for this man will never restrain his invincible two hands;
71 now that he holds the well-polished bow and the quiver beside him,
72 shooting from smooth threshold until he has killed all of our number;
73 therefore remember the joy and the fury of battle before us.
74 Draw all your swords and hold up tables against the assault
75 of death-swift arrows; then everyone rush at the man there together,
76 all in one mass, if we thrust him away from threshold and doorway;
77 then go up through the city; the cry would be raised very quickly.
78 Then this man will have shot what surely will be his last arrow.”
79 Speaking, he drew from his scabbard the sharply whetted sword there,
80 bronze, honed on both edges, and sprang at Odysseus directly,
81 shouting a terrible cry; at the same time noble Odysseus
82 sent out the arrow and struck at his chest by the side of the nipple,
83 planting the swift shaft deep in his liver; the sword from his hand then
84 fell to the ground; and doubled across the table before him,
85 down he fell, pouring food all over the earth from the dishes,
86 also the two-handled cup; with his forehead he battered the earth there,
87 anguished in spirit, while both of his feet kicked hard at the chair there,
88 shaking it under him; mist was scattered over his eyes there.
89 Then Amphinomus rushed to face renowned Odysseus directly,
90 springing against him, and drew out the sharp-pointed sword he was carrying,
91 hoping the king might give way from the doors; but Telemachus caught him
92 first from behind with a bronze-shod spear, striking the center
93 between his shoulders, and drove it completely through to his breastbone.
94 Down he fell with a thud, and he struck the earth with his forehead.
95 Telemachus sprang away, leaving the long-shadowed spear there,
96 fixed in Amphinomus; he feared that one of the Achaeans
97 might run up while he pulled out the long spear and stab him,
98 slashing with sword, or strike him as he bent over forward.
99 Running away from the corpse, he came rapidly back to his father,
100 standing close by him there and addressing him now with winged language:
101 “Father, now I will bring you a shield and a couple of lances,
102 also a helmet of bronze that is fitted around both your temples;
103 I will put armor on when I come, and give to the swineherd
104 arms, and the oxherd as well; it is better that all should be armored.”
105 Then resourceful Odysseus answered his son in this manner:
106 “Bring them while running, as long as I still have arrows to guard me,
107 lest they dislodge me from here at the doors while I stand here alone now.”
108 Thus did he speak; Telemachus listened well to his dear father,
109 went on his way to the chamber in which the illustrious arms lay.
110 Out of it four great shields he selected and eight sturdy lances,
111 also four helmets of bronze with their horsehair crests nodding above them;
112 carrying these, he swiftly returned once again to his father.
113 First of them all he himself put bronze on his body;
114 likewise both of the servants put on their beautiful armor,
115 standing around the resourceful Odysseus, cunning in counsel.
116 Then for as long as the arrows remained to defend him, Odysseus
117 struck one suitor after another in his own palace,
118 aiming unerringly; close to each other the dead men were falling.
119 But when the arrows at last had failed the lord who was shooting,
120 then against one doorpost of the well-built hall he leaned his bow,
121 setting it upright and leaning against the resplendent doorwall.
122 Then he surrounded his shoulders with one great four-layered shield there,
123 placed on his powerful head a well-fashioned helmet of bronze there,
124 crested with horsehair; above it the terrible horsehair plume nodded.
125 Then he took up a pair of powerful bronze-headed lances.
126 There was a postern door in the strongly constructed wall there,
127 there at the farthest end, near the threshold of the well-standing palace,
128 passageway led to an alley; well-fitting planks closed the entry.
129 Noble Odysseus told the illustrious swineherd to watch it,
130 standing close by the door, for there was a single approach there.
131 Then Agelaus spoke, declaring his counsel to all men:
132 “Friends, can none of us climb through the postern door and give warning
133 unto the people outside, that the cry may be raised very quickly?
134 Then this man will have shot what surely will be his last arrow.”
135 Then Melanthius, keeper of goats, made answer among them:
136 “No way exists, Zeus-nurtured Agelaus; terribly near are
137 doors of the courtyard, and narrow and difficult is the alley’s
138 mouth; a single courageous man could hold off everyone from it.
139 Come, however, and I will bring armor to cover your bodies
140 out of the chamber; inside, I believe, and nowhere else did
141 lord Odysseus and his glorious son lay all of the weapons.”
142 Speaking, Melanthius, keeper of goats, went up through the palace
143 up through the narrow ways of the great hall into Odysseus’ chamber.
144 Twelve great shields he selected from there, and as many lances,
145 also as many bronze helmets crested with horsehair above them;
146 carrying these, he swiftly returned and presented them all to the suitors.
147 Then were the knees and the heart of Odysseus utterly loosened,
148 seeing them cover their bodies with armor and hold in their two hands
149 long spears brandished aloft; mighty labor appeared before him.
150 Quickly he spoke to Telemachus then with the following winged words:
151 “Surely, Telemachus, one of the women who serve in our palace
152 urges an evil war against us, or this is Melanthius.”
153 Then in his prudence Telemachus answered directly before him:
154 “Father, I myself made this mistake, and no one beside me
155 carries the blame; for I left the strongly constructed door of the chamber
156 standing ajar; and their watchman was better than ours in the palace.
157 Go now, noble Eumaeus, and shut fast the door of the chamber;
158 find out whether in fact one of the women is doing these things,
159 or, as I myself think, Dolios’ son Melanthius.”
160 Thus they were speaking these words with one another there in the palace.
161 Back once again to the chamber Melanthius, keeper of goats, went,
162 seeking to bring out beautiful arms; but the noble swineherd
163 noticed and quickly addressed Odysseus, who stood there beside him:
164 “Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus of manifold cunning,
165 there is the destructive man whom we ourselves were suspecting;
166 he is returning again to the chamber; but give me an answer:
167 shall I kill him, if somehow I prove myself stronger in combat,
168 or shall I bring him to you, that he may pay all of the many
169 insolent crimes that this man has devised in the halls of your palace?”
170 Then resourceful Odysseus answered the swineherd in this way:
171 “I and my son Telemachus here will contain all the haughty
172 suitors within the great hall, though exceedingly eager for battle;
173 you two twist both his feet and his hands up behind his body,
174 cast him into the chamber and lash its boards closed behind him;
175 attach a plaited rope to his body and bind it securely,
176 haul him along the high column and bring him close to the roof beams,
177 so he lives on for long and suffers the pain of affliction.”
178 Thus did he speak; and the two men readily heard and obeyed him,
179 going into the chamber, unseen by the man who was in it.
180 He was searching for arms in the innermost part of the chamber;
181 they stood still on both sides of the doorway, waiting upon him.
182 When Melanthius, keeper of goats, stepped over the threshold,
183 holding a beautiful helmet of bronze in one of his two hands,
184 while in the other he carried an ancient shield crusted with mildew,
185 shield of the hero Laertes, which he had borne as a young man;
186 now it had long lain there, and the seams of the straps had been loosened;
187 then the two men sprang upon him and seized him and dragged him inside there
188 by the hair, throwing him down on the floor with his heart full of anguish;
189 bound both his feet and his hands in a painful and heart-breaking bondage,
190 twisting them well up behind him, completely, just as commanded
191 there by Laertes’ son, long-suffering noble Odysseus;
192 fastened a plaited rope to his body and bound it securely,
193 hauled him along the high column and brought him close to the roof beams.
194 Mocking him there, you addressed him now, Eumaeus the swineherd:
195 “Now then, Melanthius, you will keep vigil carefully all night,
196 lying here in a soft bed, just as befits such a creature;
197 nor will the early-born goddess approaching from Ocean’s river
198 take you unaware, gold-throned Dawn, when the hour comes for driving
199 goats to the palace for suitors, preparing their feast in the great hall.”
200 Thus he was left where he hung, stretched out in the deadly entanglement;
201 they put armor upon them and fastened the shining door of the chamber,
202 going to shrewd Odysseus there, the resourceful planner.
203 There they were standing and breathing their fury, the men on the threshold;
204 four were they, while within stood opponents both many and valiant.
205 Close to them came Zeus’ daughter, the goddess Athena,
206 taking the likeness of Mentor in body as well as in voice there.
207 Seeing her there, Odysseus rejoiced and addressed her:
208 “Mentor, avert this ruin! Remember your own dear companion,
209 all of the good I have done you; for you are equal in age to me.”
210 Thus did he speak, supposing she was Athena, rouser of armies.
211 Meanwhile the suitors shouted at her from the depths of the palace;
212 first to revile her was lord Agelaus, the son of Damastor:
213 “Mentor, let not Odysseus here with his words now persuade you
214 into defending him here and fighting against all the suitors.
215 For I believe that our purpose will certainly reach its completion:
216 after we kill these men, both the son and his father together,
217 you will be slain thereafter with them for the deeds you are eager
218 here to perform in the palace; your head shall pay for your outrage.
219 Then when our bronze has robbed the strength from every one of your bodies,
220 all possessions of yours, both those indoors and those beyond doors,
221 we will mix with Odysseus’ wealth; and we will not suffer
222 sons of your body to live in your halls, nor suffer your daughters
223 or your faithful wife to go freely throughout Ithaca’s city.”
224 Thus did he speak; and Athena was much more wrathful in spirit;
225 then she reviled Odysseus there with the words of her anger:
226 “Now no longer, Odysseus, firm is your courage or battle-strength,
227 such as you showed when for Helen, white-armed and high-born,
228 nine years long you battled the Trojans always without rest,
229 killing many men in the terrible labor of warfare,
230 and through your counsel the broad-streeted city of Priam was taken.
231 How then, now that you reach your own house and all your possessions,
232 do you lament that you lack the courage to face all the suitors?
233 Come, dear man, stand close beside me and witness my labor,
234 so you may know what manner of man among hostile opponents
235 Mentor, the son of Alcimus, is in repaying your kindness.”
236 Thus she spoke, but she had not granted decisive victory,
237 rather she tested the courage and battle strength that were dwelling
238 both in Odysseus and in the king’s glorious son there.
239 Then she flew up to a smoke-darkened beam of the hall there,
240 perching upon it, appearing in form like a swallow before them.
241 Then Agelaus, the son of Damastor, roused all the suitors,
242 lord Eurynomus also, Amphimedon, and Demoptolemus,
243 lord Peisander, the son of Polyctor, and warlike Polybus;
244 these were by far the very best of the suitors in valor,
245 all who remained still alive and were fighting there for their lives;
246 all of the others the bow and the crowded arrows had conquered.
247 Then Agelaus spoke, declaring his counsel to all men:
248 “Friends, now surely this man will restrain his invincible two hands.
249 Mentor has gone away, after speaking his empty vauntings;
250 only these men are left at the foremost doors of the palace.
251 Therefore do not cast all of your long spears together,
252 rather let six throw first, if somehow Zeus should be willing
253 that Odysseus be struck and glory be won by our party.
254 None of the others will matter to us when this man has fallen.”
255 Thus he spoke; and the six cast spears as the man had commanded,
256 eager to strike; but Athena rendered every one useless.
257 One of their weapons had struck the post of the well-built great hall;
258 another had struck the door that was strongly constructed and fitted;
259 in the wall had fallen another man’s ash spear, heavy with bronze.
260 But when they all had avoided the spears of the suitors,
261 long-suffering noble Odysseus began speaking among them:
262 “Friends, I would say it is time now for us in our own turn
263 to cast at the suitors’ throng, who furiously strive
264 to kill and despoil us, adding this to all their former evils.”
265 Thus did he speak; and the four cast all of the sharp-pointed lances,
266 aiming directly: Odysseus struck Demoptolemus,
267 Telemachus Euryades, and the swineherd Elatus;
268 Peisander was slain by Philoetius, keeper of cattle.
269 All of the stricken together bit hard at the measureless flooring;
270 then all the suitors withdrew to the innermost part of the chamber.
271 Odysseus’ men sprang forward and pulled their spears from the corpses.
272 Once again all the suitors cast out their sharp-pointed lances,
273 eager to strike; but Athena rendered most of them useless.
274 One of their weapons struck the doorpost of the well-standing palace,
275 one of them struck the strongly constructed door of the great hall,
276 one man’s ashwood spear, heavy with bronze, fell into the wall there.
277 But Amphimedon struck Telemachus there on the hand at the wrist;
278 grazing, the bronze only marred the outermost surface of skin there.
279 Then at Eumaeus Ctesippus cast his long spear over the shield;
280 it grazed his shoulder, flew onward, and fell to the ground there.
281 Then all the men with resourceful Odysseus, cunning in counsel,
282 hurled their sharp-pointed lances again at the crowd of the suitors.
283 There once again Odysseus, sacker of cities, struck Eurydamas,
284 Telemachus Amphimedon, and the swineherd Polybus;
285 then Ctesippus was struck by the man who was keeper of cattle,
286 hit in the breast; and he vaunted aloud and addressed him in triumph:
287 “Son of Polytherses, lover of insults, never surrender
288 wholly to foolishness, speaking great words, but entrust to the godheads
289 anything spoken; for surely the gods are much stronger than mortals.
290 This is a guest-gift returned for the hoof that once you presented
291 unto godlike Odysseus here as he roamed through his household.”
292 Thus did the keeper of spiral-horned cattle speak; but Odysseus
293 wounded Damastor’s son at close quarters there with his long spear.
294 Telemachus wounded Leocritus, son of Euenor,
295 driving the spear through the middle of his flank and the bronze through his body;
296 forward he fell, and he struck the earth with the whole of his forehead.
297 Then at that moment Athena held up the man-destroying aegis,
298 high from the roof; and the hearts of the suitors were panic-struck.
299 Then they fled through the great hall like cattle in herds that are driven
300 when a flickering gadfly attacks and drives them to frenzy
301 there in the season of spring, when the days grow long in the sunlight.
302 Then like vultures with crooked talons and beaks curving inward,
303 coming out of the mountains and pouncing straight upon birds;
304 the birds in the plain speed onward, cowering under the cloudbanks,
305 vultures destroy them by pouncing; no valor can give them protection,
306 none can escape, and the hunters rejoice in capturing quarry;
307 so they rushed at the suitors throughout the great hall of the palace,
308 striking them everywhere round; and foul groaning arose there
309 as heads were smitten, and all of the floor seethed over with bloodshed.
310 Then Leodes ran to Odysseus and clasped both his knees,
311 begging for mercy and speaking to him with the following winged words:
312 “I embrace your knees, Odysseus; respect me and pity me!
313 “Never, I say, did I wrong any woman who lived in the palace,
314 either by word or reckless deed; indeed I restrained all the others,
315 the other suitors whenever any one acted in such fashion.
316 Yet they would never obey me and keep their hands away from their evil;
317 therefore because of their reckless transgressions they met their vile death.
318 Yet among them, though I was the diviner and did no evil,
319 I shall lie; afterward there is no gratitude for doing good things.”
320 Looking from under his brows, resourceful Odysseus answered:
321 “If you boast you were here as diviner of sacrifice for them,
322 many times in the halls you must have prayed to the godheads
323 that the goal of my sweet homecoming might stay far from me,
324 while my beloved wife should follow you, bearing your children.
325 Therefore you will not escape the painful death that awaits you.”
326 Thus did he speak and take up the sword in his powerful hand there,
327 lying where Agelaus had flung it away on the ground
328 when he was killed; with this he struck through the midst of his neck there;
329 even while he was speaking, his head was mingled with dust there.
330 Still the bard, the son of Terpes, was trying to flee the black death,
331 Phemius, who for the suitors had sung by their forceful compulsion.
332 There he stood with the clear-toned lyre held fast in his two hands,
333 close to the postern door; and his spirit considered two courses:
334 whether to slip from the hall and go toward great Zeus’ altar,
335 sitting at that well-built shrine of the courtyard, where so often
336 both old Laertes and lord Odysseus burned thighbones of cattle,
337 or instead to rush at Odysseus, taking his knees in entreaty.
338 Thus as he pondered, this seemed to him much the better decision:
339 taking the knees of Laertes’ son, Odysseus, firmly.
340 Setting the hollow lyre down there on the ground as he stood there,
341 midway between the great mixing bowl and silver-studded chair there,
342 he himself rushed up to Odysseus, clasping his two knees,
343 begging for mercy and speaking to him with the following winged words:
344 “I embrace your knees, Odysseus; respect me and pity me!
345 You yourself afterward will suffer sorrow, if ever you kill a singer
346 who sings alike for the gods and the mortal-born people.
347 I am self-taught, and within me a god has implanted the pathways
348 of every kind; I am fit to sing beside you as I would sing
349 to a god; therefore do not desire to cut through my throat here.
350 Telemachus himself, your beloved son, could tell you
351 how I was never willing and never from need of my own came
352 into your household to sing for the suitors at all of their dinners;
353 rather they, being both stronger and many more, brought me by force here.”
354 Thus did he speak; and the sacred strength of Telemachus heard him;
355 swiftly he spoke to his father, who stood beside him, and told him:
356 “Hold back; do not strike this innocent man with the bronze sword.
357 We will preserve the herald Medon too, who was always
358 caring for me in our palace in those days when I was a child there,
359 if Philoetius or the swineherd has not already killed him,
360 and if he has not met you as you raged through the palace.”
361 Thus did he speak; and Medon, a man of discernment, had heard him,
362 crouching beneath a chair; around him a newly stripped ox-hide
363 lay as a cloak, as he tried to escape black death and destruction.
364 Quickly he rose from beneath the chair and slipped out of the ox-hide,
365 rushed up to Telemachus, clasping his knees with his two hands,
366 begging for mercy and speaking to him with the following winged words:
367 “Dear one, here I am! Hold back, and tell your own father
368 not to destroy me with sharp bronze, overpowering me utterly,
369 angry against the suitors, the men who wasted his treasures
370 here in the halls and in folly gave no consideration to you.”
371 Smiling at him, resourceful Odysseus answered in this way:
372 “Courage, for this man already has rescued and saved you,
373 so you may know in your heart, and tell to another hereafter,
374 how doing good is much better by far than doing an evil.
375 Go from the hall and sit down outside in the open air,
376 out from the slaughter, there in the courtyard, you and the singer,
377 while I labor throughout the house at whatever is needed.”
378 Thus did he speak; and the two men went out together from the great hall,
379 taking their seats at the altar of mighty Zeus in the courtyard,
380 looking around upon all sides, always expecting their slaughter.
381 Then Odysseus surveyed his house, to discover if someone
382 still was alive and hiding away to escape black death and destruction.
383 All of the men he beheld there in blood and in dust now,
384 fallen in heaps, like fish that are gathered by seafaring fishers,
385 hauled to a hollow shore after drawing them out of the gray sea
386 in a net full of many small holes; and all of the fishes,
387 longing for waves of the salt sea, lie strewn over sand there;
388 there the resplendent sun takes life away from their bodies.
389 So at that hour the suitors lay heaped one upon another.
390 Then resourceful Odysseus spoke to his son Telemachus:
391 “Come now, Telemachus; call my nurse Eurycleia,
392 so I may tell her a word that my spirit desires to utter.”
393 Thus did he speak; Telemachus listened well to his dear father,
394 rattled the door and addressed Eurycleia, the nurse of the household:
395 “Come here, rise, old woman born long ago, who are keeper
396 over the serving women who live in the halls of our palace.
397 Come here; my father is calling for you, that he may address you.”
398 Thus did he speak, but the word remained without wings for the old nurse.
399 Opening doors of the well-inhabited palace before her,
400 on she went, while Telemachus there was leading before her.
401 Then she discovered Odysseus standing among the dead bodies,
402 covered with blood and with gore, and resembling one of the lions
403 coming away after eating an ox that had grazed in the country;
404 all of the lion’s chest and the cheeks on both sides of his muzzle
405 drip with blood, and the sight of the beast is a terrible thing there;
406 so was Odysseus covered with blood on his feet and his two hands.
407 When she beheld all the corpses there and immeasurable bloodshed,
408 forward she started to raise a cry of triumph at such a great labor;
409 but Odysseus held her and stopped her, though she was eager,
410 then he raised up his voice and addressed her with words that were winged there:
411 “Rejoice in your heart, old woman; restrain yourself and do not cry triumph.
412 Glorying over the dead men never is holy or righteous.
413 Fate from the gods has conquered these men, and their cruel transgressions;
414 never did they show honor to any earth-dwelling mortal,
415 neither a wicked nor good man, whoever arrived among them;
416 therefore because of their reckless transgressions they met their vile death.
417 Come now, list all the women for me who live here within doors,
418 those who dishonor me here and those who remain without guilt here.”
419 Then his beloved nurse Eurycleia spoke to him in answer:
420 “Therefore, my child, I will tell you all of the truth of the matter.
421 Fifty women who serve you live in the halls of your palace,
422 some of them taught by ourselves to accomplish the work of the household,
423 carding the wool and enduring the yoke of their own life in bondage.
424 Twelve among all of these had entered the ways of dishonor,
425 showing respect to neither myself nor Penelope ever.
426 Telemachus only was lately grown, and his mother would never
427 suffer the youth to command these women who serve in the household.
428 Come now, however, and I will ascend to the beautiful upper rooms;
429 tell your wife, whom a god sent sleeping upon her.”
430 Then resourceful Odysseus answered the nurse in this manner:
431 “Do not awaken her yet; but call all the women to come here,
432 those who earlier plotted the acts of their shame in the palace.”
433 Thus he spoke, and the old nurse went through the length of the palace,
434 bearing the message to women and urgently bidding them gather.
435 Then he called Telemachus, the oxherd, and also the swineherd
436 close to his side and addressed them all with the following winged words:
437 “Begin now carrying bodies, and order the women to help you.
438 Afterward clean all the beautiful chairs and the tables
439 using clean water and sponges pierced with a multitude of holes.
440 Then when you all have put the whole house in its proper arrangement,
441 lead all the serving women outside from the well-standing palace,
442 midway between the round house and the strong wall that circles the courtyard,
443 strike them with long-edged swords, until from every last woman
444 you have taken her life and they all have forgotten Aphrodite,
445 the love they enjoyed with the suitors, secretly lying beside them.”
446 Thus did he speak; and the women arrived there, gathered together,
447 terribly wailing and shedding warm tears down over their faces.
448 First of all, therefore, they carried the bodies of all the dead suitors,
449 setting them under the portico there of the well-fenced courtyard,
450 leaning the corpses together; Odysseus himself gave orders,
451 sternly compelling their speed; and the women were forced to remove them.
452 After this they cleaned all the beautiful chairs and the tables,
453 using clean water and sponges pierced with a multitude of holes.
454 Then Telemachus, the keeper of cattle, and the swineherd together
455 scraped with their shovels the floor of the strongly constructed great palace;
456 serving women carried the scrapings and set them beyond doors.
457 Then when they all had put the great hall in its proper arrangement,
458 leading the serving women outside from the well-standing palace,
459 midway between the round house and the strong wall that circles the courtyard,
460 there in a narrow place they confined them, from which none could flee there.
461 Then in his prudence Telemachus started speaking among them:
462 “Never would I grant these women a death that is clean and untainted,
463 women who poured out reproaches on top of my head in this household
464 and on the head of my mother, then slept at the side of the suitors.”
465 Thus did he speak, and he took the cable of one dark-prowed vessel,
466 fastening one end to a great column and circling the round house,
467 stretching it high overhead, so no woman’s feet reached the flooring.
468 Just as the long-winged thrushes or doves in their swift flight
469 strike on a snare that stands there securely set in a thicket,
470 eager to enter their roost, but a hateful bed receives them,
471 so these women held all their heads there, one beside another;
472 nooses lay round every neck, that their death be the most lamentable.
473 Briefly their two feet struggled, but not for a very long time.
474 Then they brought Melanthius out through the forecourt and courtyard;
475 cut both his nose and his ears away with the pitiless bronze there,
476 tore out his genitals, feeding them raw to the dogs for devouring,
477 cut off his two hands and feet in the wrath and rage of their spirits.
478 Then when the men had washed both their hands and their feet in the water,
479 into Odysseus’ house they returned; all the labor was ended.
480 Then to his own beloved nurse Eurycleia Odysseus spoke out:
481 “Bring me sulfur, old woman, the cure of corruption, and bring fire,
482 so I may sulfur the great hall; you are to bid Penelope
483 come here now with the women attendants who wait on her closely;
484 urgently call every servant woman who lives in the household.”
485 Then his beloved nurse Eurycleia spoke to him in answer:
486 “Surely, my child, you have spoken of all these things as is fitting.
487 Come now, and I will bring clothes for you—a cloak and a tunic;
488 do not stand in the rags with your broad two shoulders enveloped
489 here in the hall; for it would be a shameful thing to behold you.”
490 Then resourceful Odysseus answered the nurse in this manner:
491 “First of all, let there now be fire for me in the palace.”
492 Thus did he speak; and his dear nurse Eurycleia obeyed him,
493 bringing the fire and the sulfur; and noble Odysseus
494 carefully sulfur-smoked both the great hall, the house, and the courtyard.
495 Then the old woman went back through the beautiful house of Odysseus,
496 bearing the message to women and urgently bidding them gather.
497 Out of the palace they came, each carrying a torch in her two hands.
498 Gathering round, they embraced and welcomed Odysseus home there,
499 kissing his head and his shoulders, embracing him lovingly;
500 taking his hands; then sweet longing took hold of Odysseus
501 for weeping and groaning; in spirit he knew every one of the women.
