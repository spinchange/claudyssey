# The Odyssey — Book XIX

*The weapons are hidden; Penelope questions the stranger; Eurycleia recognizes Odysseus.*

1 Meanwhile noble Odysseus was left in the hall of the palace,
2 planning together with Pallas Athena destruction for suitors.
3 Straightway he spoke to Telemachus, uttering swift-winging phrases:
4 “My Telemachus, all of the weapons of Ares must now be
5 laid up inside; but whenever the suitors should miss them and question,
6 cheat them with soothing words when they ask you, and tell them the reason:
7 ‘Far from the smoke I laid them away, for they no longer seemed like
8 arms which Odysseus left when he went long ago toward Troia;
9 rather, they all are defiled by the breath of the fire reaching round them.
10 Also a spirit has planted this greater concern in my thinking,
11 lest, when the wine has possessed you and quarrels arise in your gathering,
12 you may wound one another, dishonoring feast and your courtship;
13 iron itself has a way of drawing a man to take hold of it.’”
14 Thus he spoke; and Telemachus heeded his own loving father.
15 Calling his nurse Eurycleia out of the chamber, he told her:
16 “Nurse, come now and confine all the serving women in the palace,
17 till I have carried the beautiful weapons belonging to father
18 into the storeroom; smoke in the house has been spoiling them, left here
19 uncared for, since father departed, when I was still only an infant.
20 Now I intend to put them away, where fire-smoke cannot attain them.”
21 Then his devoted nurse Eurycleia addressed him in answer:
22 “If only, child, you would ever take up such prudent attention,
23 caring for all of the house and guarding the whole of its substance.
24 Come, however, and tell me who follows and carries the lamplight?
25 You would not let serving women go forward, although they could light you.”
26 Then in reply wise-minded Telemachus spoke to his nurse there:
27 “This stranger; I will not suffer the idle man who partakes of
28 my grain allowance, even though he has come from a faraway country.”
29 Thus he spoke; but the word had no winged flight in the old nurse;
30 rather, she barred all the doors of the strongly inhabited palace.
31 Then both Odysseus and his illustrious son sprang upright,
32 carrying helmets and boss-covered shields to the innermost chamber,
33 also the sharp-bladed spears. Before them Pallas Athena,
34 holding a lamp made of gold, created a beautiful radiance.
35 Then Telemachus suddenly spoke to his own loving father:
36 “Father, indeed, with my eyes I behold an enormous wonder:
37 all of the walls of the palace and beautiful beams that divide it,
38 rafters of fir and the columns that tower on high in the household
39 shine to my eyes like the light of a fire that is blazing.
40 Surely a god is inside, one of those who possess the broad heaven.”
41 Then Odysseus of manifold counsel addressed him in answer:
42 “Silence; restrain your own thought and do not make any inquiry.
43 Such is the practice of gods who possess the Olympian dwellings.
44 Go now and lie down; I shall remain here behind you,
45 so I may question the serving women and stir up your mother;
46 mourning for me, she will ask me about every thing that has happened.”
47 Thus he spoke; and Telemachus went through the hall of the palace,
48 walking away to his chamber beneath the light of the firebrands,
49 where he had lain in the past whenever sweet sleep had approached him.
50 There he lay also that night and awaited the radiant Dawn there.
51 Meanwhile noble Odysseus was left in the hall of the palace,
52 planning together with Pallas Athena destruction for suitors.
53 Out of her chamber prudent Penelope came to the great hall,
54 like to Artemis there or the golden goddess of love, Aphrodite.
55 Close to the fire for her they set the chair where she rested,
56 curved, ornamented with ivory work and with silver; the craftsman
57 Icmalius once had made it and fitted a stool to its lower
58 frame, growing out of the chair; a great fleece had been laid over all of it.
59 There in the chair the prudent Penelope settled and rested.
60 White-armed serving women arrived from the hall of the palace.
61 They removed the abundant bread and the tables beside it,
62 also the cups from which the imperious men had been drinking.
63 Onto the floor they cast all the fire from out of the braziers,
64 heaping abundant wood on the braziers for light and for warmth.
65 Then Melantho rebuked Odysseus yet a second time, saying:
66 “Stranger, will you even now keep troubling us into the nighttime,
67 wandering over the house and spying upon all the women?
68 Get yourself out through the door, wretch, and profit from feasting,
69 lest very soon you are struck with a firebrand and forced through the doorway.”
70 Looking from under his brows, Odysseus of many devices answered:
71 “Foolish woman, why do you fasten upon me with anger?
72 Is it because I am filthy and wear evil clothes on my body,
73 begging throughout the land? My necessity presses me onward.
74 Such is the nature of beggars and men who are homeless and wandering.
75 Once I myself had a house and a place among humankind also,
76 wealthy and prosperous, often I gave to a wanderer freely,
77 whatever kind he might be and whatever the need that had brought him.
78 Servants I had without number and manifold other possessions,
79 all by which people live well and are commonly counted as wealthy.
80 Zeus, Cronus’ son, stripped all these away; such was surely his pleasure.
81 Therefore beware, woman, lest you one day may lose every
82 grace and beauty by which you excel over all serving women,
83 lest your mistress become angry with you and treat you with harshness,
84 or Odysseus come—for a portion of hope is remaining.
85 Even if he has died in this fashion and has no return home,
86 now there is such a son, by the favor of lordly Apollo,
87 young Telemachus; no woman here in the halls can escape him,
88 doing her reckless deeds, for he is no longer so little.”
89 Thus he spoke; and prudent Penelope heard all his answer.
90 Then she rebuked her attendant and called her by name as she told her:
91 “Surely, you bold-faced dog without shame, you do not escape me,
92 doing an infamous deed you will wipe on your own head.
93 All things you knew well, for you heard from me in my own words
94 how I intended to question the stranger here in my palace
95 after my husband, for I am oppressed by continual sorrow.”
96 Thus she spoke, and now she addressed Eurynome the housekeeper:
97 “Bring, Eurynome, a chair here and lay a fleece over it,
98 so he may sit and address me, and listen in turn to my answer,
99 this man, the stranger; for I am desiring to question him closely.”
100 Thus she spoke; Eurynome carried and speedily set down
101 there a well-polished chair and laid a great fleece over it also.
102 There much-enduring noble Odysseus sat and was settled.
103 First of the two to begin was prudent Penelope, saying:
104 “Stranger, this question is first, and I myself mean to ask it:
105 who are you, whence among men? Where is your city and parentage?”
106 Then Odysseus of manifold counsel addressed her in answer:
107 “Lady, no mortal man on the limitless earth could reproach you;
108 truly your fame has risen as high as the broad reaches of heaven,
109 like the fame of a blameless and excellent king who is god-fearing,
110 reigning among many powerful men in a numerous people,
111 holding justice aloft; then the dark earth bears up its bounty,
112 wheat and barley, while trees are heavily laden with fruit there,
113 sheep give birth without failing, and seas provide fish in abundance,
114 all from his excellent rule, and the people beneath him are prospering.
115 Therefore ask me now any other thing in your household,
116 but do not ask me about my own race and the land of my fathers,
117 lest you should fill my spirit more fully with bitter affliction
118 when I remember; for I am a man of continual mourning.
119 Neither is it right in the house of another to sit in lamenting,
120 weeping, since endless grieving continually makes the thing worse there,
121 lest one serving woman resent me, or you do so yourself, and
122 say I am swimming in tears with a mind that is heavy from wine here.”
123 Then in her turn the prudent Penelope gave him an answer:
124 “Stranger, my excellence, beauty, and bodily stature together
125 gods have destroyed from the time the Argives embarked for the city
126 Ilium, and with them Odysseus, my husband, departed.
127 If that man had returned to protect and attend to my lifetime,
128 greater and fairer by far would my fame and my glory have risen.
129 Now I am grieving; the spirit has driven such evils upon me.
130 All of the nobles who rule in the islands surrounding our country,
131 both in Dulichium, Same, and forest-grown Zacynthus,
132 likewise all who inhabit our clear-seen island of Ithaca,
133 court me against my own will and wear out the whole of the household.
134 Therefore I pay no regard to strangers or men who are suppliants,
135 neither to heralds, the men whose work is the public’s employment;
136 rather, desiring Odysseus, I waste the dear heart in my bosom.
137 They press marriage upon me; but I wind up my coils of deception.
138 First, some spirit inspired my mind with the thought of a great shroud,
139 setting the huge loom up in the palace and weaving upon it,
140 delicate work of broad measure; and straightway I spoke to the suitors:
141 ‘Young men courting me now, since noble Odysseus has perished,
142 wait, although you are pressing my marriage, until I have finished
143 weaving this shroud—lest all of my spinning be wasted and fruitless—
144 burial cloth for the hero Laertes, to have when a deadly
145 fate lays hold of the man in the long and painful hour of his dying,
146 lest one Achaean woman among all the people reproach me,
147 should a man with so many possessions lie dead without winding.’
148 Thus I spoke, and their proud hearts consented and yielded within them.
149 Thereafter all through the day I would weave at the great loom,
150 but in the night I unraveled the work when I set torches near me.
151 Thus for three years I secretly fooled and persuaded Achaeans.
152 Yet when the fourth year came and the seasons were passing upon me,
153 while the moons waned away and a great many days were accomplished,
154 then through the aid of my shameless maids who cared nothing about me,
155 they came and caught me at work and rebuked me with words of compulsion.
156 Thus I finished the shroud, unwilling, constrained by necessity.
157 Now I can neither escape the marriage nor find any other
158 stratagem; greatly my parents are pressing that I should be married,
159 while my son is angered to see them consume his possessions,
160 knowing it all, for already he is a man who is fully
161 able to care for a house, to whom Zeus gives glory and honor.
162 Yet even so, tell me of your own birth and the place you have come from.
163 You were not born of a long-fabled oak tree or out of a boulder.”
164 Then Odysseus of manifold counsel addressed her in answer:
165 “Revered wife of Laertes’ son Odysseus, lady,
166 will you not cease from questioning me of my birth and my bloodline?
167 Yet I shall tell you, although you will give me additional sorrows
168 greater than those I possess, as is usual whenever a person
169 stays far away from the land of his fathers as long as I have done,
170 roaming about through many a city of mortals and suffering hardship.
171 Nevertheless I shall answer the things you inquire and question.
172 Crete is a country that lies in the midst of the wine-darkened ocean,
173 beautiful, fertile, and sea-girt, and people are living
174 there in a number beyond all counting, in ninety great cities.
175 Mixed are their languages, each with the others; Achaeans are living
176 there, great-hearted Eteocretans, and Cydonian people,
177 Dorians divided in three tribes and noble Pelasgians.
178 There among all of the cities is Knossos, the great town, where Minos
179 ruled in nine-year terms as the confidant of mighty Zeus there,
180 father of my own father, the great-hearted hero Deucalion.
181 Deucalion fathered both me and Idomeneus the ruler;
182 but he had gone in the curving ships to the city of Ilium,
183 going beside the sons of Atreus. Aethon is my famous name here;
184 I was the younger by birth, while he was the elder and better.
185 There I beheld Odysseus once and presented him guest-gifts.
186 For to the land of Crete a violent wind had compelled him,
187 driving him out of his road toward Troia beyond the Malean
188 cape, and it set him at Amnisus, there where Eileithyia’s
189 cave stands over difficult harbors; he barely escaped from the storm-winds.
190 Straightway, going up to the town, he asked for Idomeneus,
191 saying he was his cherished and honored guest-friend among men.
192 Yet it was already the tenth or the eleventh day since Idomeneus
193 left in the curving vessels to journey to Ilium’s country.
194 Therefore I led Odysseus home to my house and received him,
195 treating him well and attentively out of our plentiful substance.
196 Both for the man and the other companions who followed beside him,
197 barley and fire-bright wine I collected as gifts from the people,
198 also the oxen for sacrifice, filling their hearts with abundance.
199 There the noble Achaeans remained for a total of twelve days,
200 held by a powerful northern wind which would not let a person
201 stand upright even on land, for some hostile spirit had raised it.
202 Then on the thirteenth day the wind fell, and they sailed from our harbor.”
203 Thus as he spoke, he fashioned his manifold lies like the truth there.
204 Penelope listened, and tears ran downward; her fair skin was melting.
205 Even as snow melts high on the loftiest peaks of the mountains,
206 snow which the eastern wind thaws after Zephyrus pours it in drifts there,
207 and as it melts the rivers are filled with its waters and rushing,
208 so were her beautiful cheeks dissolved in the stream of her teardrops,
209 weeping for her own husband, although he was sitting beside her.
210 Deep in his spirit Odysseus pitied his wife as she mourned him;
211 yet his eyes stood firm between eyelids, like horn or like iron,
212 never once trembling; with cunning he hid all his tears from the woman.
213 When she had taken her fill of the lamentation of many tears,
214 once more giving an answer, she spoke these words in his hearing:
215 “Stranger, I think that I now will put you at last to the testing,
216 whether in truth he was there with his godlike companions beside him,
217 and you received my husband in your halls, as you say in your story.
218 Tell me the kind of apparel he wore around his own body,
219 what he himself was like and the comrades who followed beside him.”
220 Then Odysseus of manifold counsel addressed her in answer:
221 “Lady, it is difficult now after we have been parted so long to
222 tell you; for this is already the twentieth year since Odysseus
223 went from my country that day and departed away from my homeland.
224 Yet I shall tell you the way that my heart represents him before me.
225 Noble Odysseus wore a great cloak of wool colored purple,
226 folded in two; and upon it a fastening fashioned of gold had
227 two matching tubes, while marvelous art was displayed on its outer face:
228 held in the forepaws of a dog was a dappled fawn that was struggling,
229 clutched in its jaws; and all would marvel at how it was fashioned,
230 both made of gold, yet the dog was strangling the fawn it had captured,
231 while with its feet the fawn struggled and yearned to escape from its captor.
232 Also I noticed the shining tunic that lay on his body,
233 fine as the delicate skin that encircles a dried garden onion;
234 so very soft was the garment and bright as the face of the sunlight.
235 Truly a great many women gazed in amazement upon him.
236 Something else I shall tell you, and you must lay it inside you:
237 I cannot know if Odysseus wore those clothes in his household,
238 or if one of his comrades had given them when he embarked on his swift ship,
239 or perhaps even a host, for Odysseus had many who loved him;
240 few of Achaea’s people, indeed, were equal to that man.
241 I myself gave him a bronze-bladed sword and a beautiful double
242 mantle of purple, together with one long tunic with borders,
243 sending him forth with honor aboard his well-benched vessel.
244 Also a herald a little more elderly than Odysseus
245 followed beside him; and I shall describe how the herald appeared there:
246 round in the shoulders and dark in complexion, with closely curled hair locks,
247 Eurybates was his name; Odysseus honored him over
248 all of his other companions because their thoughts were in concert.”
249 Thus he spoke, and a longing for lamentation arose even stronger
250 when she discovered the tokens which steadfast Odysseus had told her.
251 When she had taken her fill of the lamentation of many tears,
252 then in her turn giving answer, she spoke these words in his hearing:
253 “Stranger, though pitiful in my eyes when you first came before me,
254 now in my palace you shall be beloved and honored among us.
255 I myself gave him the garments exactly as you have described them,
256 folding them out of the chamber; I added the glittering brooch there,
257 meant as adornment for him; but I shall not welcome him ever
258 homeward returning again to the land of the fathers he cherished.
259 Therefore Odysseus went by an evil fate in the hollow
260 vessel to look on accursed Ilium, never to be named.”
261 Then Odysseus of manifold counsel addressed her in answer:
262 “Revered wife of Laertes’ son Odysseus, lady,
263 cease now from ruining all of the beautiful skin on your body,
264 melting the heart in your breast as you mourn for your husband. I cannot
265 blame you, for any woman will mourn when she loses a husband,
266 wedded to her when a maiden, to whom she has borne loving children,
267 let alone lord Odysseus, said to resemble the godheads.
268 Yet you must cease from your mourning and listen to what I shall tell you,
269 since I shall give an exact account and conceal nothing from you:
270 I have already heard of Odysseus’ journey returning,
271 close by, there in the fertile land of Thesprotian people,
272 living; and many and excellent treasures he carries along now,
273 begging them out through the land. Yet his faithful band of companions
274 and his hollow-built vessel he lost on the wine-darkened ocean,
275 coming away from the isle of Thrinacia; Zeus and the Sun-god
276 both were enraged at him, for his comrades had slaughtered the cattle.
277 All of the men were destroyed in the sea of the manifold breakers;
278 him on the keel of his ship a wave threw out on the dry land,
279 into the land of Phaeacian people, a race near the godheads.
280 These men honored him there from the depths of their hearts like a godhead,
281 gave him innumerable gifts, and wished to convey him themselves back
282 home without injury. Long ago would Odysseus be here now,
283 but in his spirit it seemed more profitable far to go onward,
284 gathering property as he traversed the broad-open country;
285 so far beyond all mortals Odysseus knows how to seek his
286 manifold profit, and no other man among mortals could rival him.
287 Thus did Pheidon, the king of Thesprotian people, inform me;
288 also he swore in my presence, as wine was poured in his household,
289 how they had hauled down a ship and prepared all the comrades to carry
290 lord Odysseus home to the land of the fathers he cherished.
291 Yet he sent me away first, because a vessel was passing,
292 manned by Thesprotian men, to Dulichium, rich in its grain-fields.
293 He showed me all the property which Odysseus gathered together;
294 even another man to the tenth generation could live from
295 all of the treasure that lay for Odysseus there in the king’s halls.
296 Pheidon said that he went to Dodona, intending to listen
297 out of the high-crested oak of Zeus to the counsel of godhead,
298 how he should go back home to the land of the fathers he cherished,
299 after so long an absence, whether in secret or open.
300 Thus he is safe and already will come; he is very close by here,
301 nor from his loved ones and land of his fathers will he still be absent
302 any long while; nevertheless I give you my oath now.
303 First among witnesses Zeus, best and highest of all gods,
304 then the hearth of blameless Odysseus which I have come to:
305 everything surely will come to fulfillment just as I tell you.
306 Here in the course of this very year will Odysseus come home,
307 as one month wanes and the following month is beginning.”
308 Then in her turn the prudent Penelope gave him an answer:
309 “Would that this word you have spoken, O stranger, might reach its fulfillment!
310 Soon you would know many gifts and much loving friendship from me here,
311 so that whoever encountered you afterward called you a blessed man.
312 Yet in the heart of my bosom I think that events will fall this way:
313 neither Odysseus now will return, nor will you obtain an
314 escort, because there are no such masters as once in the household,
315 such as Odysseus was among men, if indeed he once lived here,
316 honored strangers receiving and sending them on from the household.
317 Yet, serving women, wash him and lay down a bed for the stranger,
318 bedsteads and mantles and beautiful rugs for his covering also,
319 so that, kept thoroughly warm, he may reach gold-throned Dawn in the morning.
320 Very early at daybreak, wash him and rub him with oil there,
321 so he may sit by Telemachus here and attend to his dinner,
322 seated inside the hall; and worse for the man among suitors
323 who might distress this stranger with heart-devouring affliction;
324 he will accomplish no further work here, though bitterly angered.
325 How shall you learn of me, stranger, whether among all the women
326 I stand out for my mind and for prudent and thoughtful devising,
327 if, while filthy and clothed in rags, you should feast in the palace?
328 Human beings are creatures whose lives are exceedingly fleeting.
329 Anyone harsh in himself and acquainted with deeds that are cruel,
330 all mortal people call curses upon him in future,
331 while he is living; and after he dies they mock him together.
332 Anyone blameless himself and acquainted with blameless behavior,
333 strangers carry his wide-spreading fame as they go through the whole of
334 humankind, and many people pronounce him noble and worthy.”
335 Then Odysseus of manifold counsel addressed her in answer:
336 “Revered wife of Laertes’ son Odysseus, lady,
337 truly, both cloaks and the beautiful rugs have been hateful to me from
338 when I first left behind me the snow-covered mountains of Crete-land,
339 going aboard my ship with its long row of oars for the journey.
340 I shall lie here, as before I have passed many nights without sleeping;
341 many the nights I have lain in a shameful and comfortless bed here,
342 waiting in misery all through the night for the well-throned Dawn-goddess.
343 Nor does the washing of feet bring any delight to my spirit;
344 no woman ever will lay any hand on these feet that are mine here,
345 none among all of the women who serve in the halls of your palace,
346 unless there is some old woman, devoted and skilled in good service,
347 one who has suffered as many afflictions in spirit as I have;
348 her I would never begrudge laying hands on the feet that are mine here.”
349 Then in her turn the prudent Penelope gave him an answer:
350 “Stranger and friend, no man so prudent among all of the foreign
351 guests from afar whom I cherished has ever arrived at my household,
352 since you speak every prudent thing with such knowledge and clear understanding.
353 I have an elderly woman whose heart knows many devices;
354 she was the woman who nourished and tenderly cared for that poor man,
355 taking the infant in both of her arms when his mother first bore him.
356 She shall wash your feet, although she is weak from her great age.
357 Come now and rise to your feet, prudent Eurycleia;
358 wash the man who is equal in age to your lord; and perhaps now Odysseus
359 has such feet as the stranger and such hands also upon him,
360 since in misfortune mortals grow old with exceeding swiftness.”
361 Thus did she speak; but the old nurse covered her face with her two hands,
362 pouring hot tears from her eyes, and spoke this word of lamenting:
363 “Ah, I am helpless for you, my child! Surely Zeus beyond all
364 human beings has hated you, though your heart was afraid of the godheads.
365 Never before did a mortal give Zeus who delights in the lightning
366 so many fat-covered thighs or such excellent hecatombs as you,
367 all that you offered to him as you prayed you might safely attain to
368 rich old age and nurture your glorious son into manhood;
369 now he has wholly deprived you alone of the day of returning.
370 Surely the women of strangers made mock of him also in this way,
371 whenever he came to the glorious house of a faraway people,
372 just as these shameless dogs, every one, make mockery of you.
373 Now to escape from their outrage and manifold acts of dishonor,
374 you do not let them wash you; but I am not here unwilling;
375 Icarius’ daughter, prudent Penelope, gave the command here.
376 Therefore I wash your feet for Penelope’s sake and your own too,
377 since in the depths of my heart affliction is newly awakened.
378 Come now, however, attend to the word I shall utter before you:
379 many the sorrow-worn strangers who came to this place in the old days,
380 yet I declare I have never seen anyone more in resemblance;
381 you in your body and voice and your feet are alike to Odysseus.”
382 Then Odysseus of manifold counsel addressed her in answer:
383 “Old nurse, thus do all people say who have looked with their own eyes
384 on the two of us: that we are exceedingly like one another,
385 just as you yourself have thoughtfully said in the words you have spoken.”
386 Thus did he speak; and the old nurse took up the glittering basin
387 where she would wash his feet, and poured in abundant cold water,
388 adding the hot water after. But meanwhile noble Odysseus
389 sat by the hearth and turned himself rapidly into the darkness;
390 straightway he thought in his heart that the nurse, when she grasped him there,
391 might recognize the scar, and his deeds might become openly known there.
392 Closer she went and washed her own master; and straightway she knew the
393 scar where long ago a wild boar struck him with white-pointed tusk when
394 he had gone into Parnassus with lord Autolycus and his children,
395 visiting his mother’s excellent father, a man who surpassed all
396 people in theft and in swearing of oaths, for a god gave this gift to him,
397 Hermes; because he burned thighbones that pleased the immortal,
398 taken from lambs and kids, and the god in his kindness went with him.
399 Once Autolycus came to the fertile country of Ithaca,
400 there he discovered the newly born son of his own daughter.
401 Taking the boy, Eurycleia set him upon the dear knees of
402 lord Autolycus after his supper had ended, and told him:
403 “Lord Autolycus, you yourself must find what name you shall give this
404 child of your daughter, a child who has long been an answer to prayer.”
405 Then in his turn Autolycus answered and raised up his voice there:
406 “Son-in-law, daughter, now give him the name I shall tell you.
407 Since I have come here hated by many throughout the broad-pastured
408 country, by women as well as by men who are living upon it,
409 therefore let Odysseus now be his name from the hatred upon me.
410 When he has grown into youth and arrives at his mother’s great household,
411 there on Parnassus, where all of my manifold property lies now,
412 some I shall give to the youth and shall send him away in rejoicing.”
413 Therefore Odysseus came, that the man might present him with splendid gifts.
414 Both Autolycus there and the sons of Autolycus greeted
415 him with embraces of hands and with gentle and welcoming speeches.
416 His mother’s mother Amphithea enfolded Odysseus closely,
417 kissing his head and the beautiful light of his two eyes together.
418 Then Autolycus called to his glorious sons and commanded
419 them to prepare the dinner; the sons gave heed to his urging.
420 Straightway into the house they led one five-year-old bull there;
421 first they skinned it and dressed it, and cut the whole carcass in pieces,
422 skillfully slicing the flesh and piercing the pieces with spit-rods;
423 carefully then they roasted and shared out equal portions.
424 So through the whole of the day till the light of the sun had descended,
425 feasting they sat, and no heart lacked any equitable portion.
426 When the light of the sun went down and the darkness descended,
427 then they lay down to sleep and took hold of the gift of their slumber.
428 Soon as the early-born Dawn showed forth with her rose-colored fingers,
429 both the dogs and the sons of Autolycus went to the hunting;
430 sons of Autolycus; with them noble Odysseus followed.
431 They climbed the steep, forest-clad mountain of great Parnassus
432 folds where the winds travel over the heights of the mountain.
433 Helios then was newly striking the fields with his sunlight,
434 rising from Ocean’s quietly flowing and deep-running waters.
435 Into a valley the hunters came; and in front of them always
436 ran all the dogs as they searched for a track, while following after
437 came Autolycus’ sons; and beside them noble Odysseus
438 journeyed close to the dogs with his long-shadowed spear in his hand there.
439 There in a densely grown thicket an enormous wild boar was lying;
440 neither the damp-blowing strength of the winds could ever pass through it,
441 nor could the radiant sun strike into it ever with sunbeams,
442 nor could the rain pierce thoroughly through, for so very dense was
443 all of the thicket, and countless leaves had been poured underneath it.
444 Round him came din of men’s feet and the feet of the hunting dogs,
445 dogs as they pressed on his lair. From the woodland he came out against them,
446 bristling all of the hair on his back, with fire in his two eyes,
447 standing close by the men; but the very first was Odysseus,
448 rushing at him, with the long spear lifted aloft in his strong hand,
449 eager to strike him; the boar came first and drove at Odysseus,
450 striking above the knee, with his tusk tearing much of the flesh there,
451 leaping aside, though it never attained to the bone of the hero.
452 Then Odysseus struck in return on the boar’s right shoulder,
453 straight through it went the point of the glittering spear he had hurled there;
454 down in the dust it fell with a grunt, and its life-spirit left it.
455 All of the sons of Autolycus tended the young Odysseus;
456 skillfully binding the wound of the blameless and godlike hero,
457 they with a spoken charm held back the dark blood from flowing,
458 then they returned at once to the house of their own loving father.
459 Both Autolycus there and the sons of Autolycus healed him,
460 healing him well and bestowing magnificent gifts for his journey,
461 quickly they sent him rejoicing away to the land of his fathers,
462 Ithaca. Both his father and mother, the noble-born lady,
463 joyed at the sight of him home and inquired about each thing that happened:
464 how he had suffered the scar; and he carefully told the whole story,
465 how while he hunted a boar struck him with its white-pointed tusk when
466 he had gone into Parnassus beside Autolycus’ children.
467 Taking the scar in the palms of her hands, the elderly nurse
468 knew it by touch; she let go the foot, which fell all at once there.
469 Into the basin the shin struck; bronze gave a clang as it sounded,
470 tilting away to the side, and the water spilled onto the flooring.
471 Joy and grief took hold of her mind at the same time; both of her eyes were
472 filled with the tears, and the full strength of her voice was arrested.
473 Taking Odysseus’ chin in her hand, she addressed him and told him:
474 “Surely you are Odysseus, beloved child! Yet I never
475 knew you until I had felt every part of the body of my lord.”
476 Thus she spoke and looked with her eyes toward prudent Penelope,
477 meaning to show her beloved husband was there in the household.
478 Yet Penelope neither could look straight at them nor notice,
479 since Athena distracted her mind; but noble Odysseus
480 reached out, seized the old nurse by the throat with his right hand,
481 drew her closer to him with the other, and spoke in her hearing:
482 “Nurse, why do you desire my death? You yourself were the woman
483 nursing me here at your breast; now after my manifold sufferings
484 I in the twentieth year have come to the land of my fathers.
485 But since you have recognized me and a god put the thought in your spirit,
486 silence, so nobody else in the palace may learn of the matter.
487 Thus I declare to you, and the word will surely be finished:
488 if beneath me a god should subdue the illustrious suitors,
489 I will not spare you although you have served as my nurse, when the other
490 serving women here in my palace I put to death.”
491 Then in her turn the prudent Eurycleia gave him an answer:
492 “Child, what a word has escaped from the fence of your teeth in your speaking!
493 You understand what spirit is mine, ever firm and unyielding;
494 I shall hold fast as a stone that is solid or a hard piece of iron.
495 Something else I shall tell you, and you must lay it inside you:
496 if beneath you a god should subdue the illustrious suitors,
497 then I shall name all the women who serve in the halls of the palace,
498 those who dishonor you and those who remain without guilt there.”
499 Then Odysseus of manifold counsel addressed her in answer:
500 “Nurse, why should you tell me of them? You have no need to do it.
501 I by myself shall observe and discover each woman exactly;
502 only keep silent the matter and leave the result to the godheads.”
503 Thus did he speak; and the old nurse went through the hall of the palace,
504 going to bring a new footbath, for all the first water was spilled there.
505 Soon as she washed him and rubbed his body richly with olive oil,
506 once again nearer the fire Odysseus drew up his chair there,
507 meaning to warm himself, while hiding the scar underneath all his tatters.
508 First of the two to begin was prudent Penelope, saying:
509 “Stranger, I myself shall ask you a little while longer;
510 soon it will surely be time for the sweetness and comfort of sleeping,
511 should sweet sleep take hold of a man, even one who is troubled.
512 Yet some spirit has given me grief without measure or limit.
513 All through the day I take pleasure in mourning and lamentation,
514 watching my own work and that of the serving women in the household;
515 but when night arrives and the bed takes hold of all people,
516 there in my bed I lie down, while close round my turbulent heart are
517 sharpened anxieties stirring my sorrow and goading my mourning.
518 Just as Pandareus’ daughter, the pale-green nightingale,
519 sings a beautiful song when the spring has newly begun in its season,
520 sitting concealed in the thick-growing leaves of the trees there,
521 she who continually turns as she pours forth her many-toned music,
522 mourning Itylus, her own dear son, whom long ago with the
523 bronze she killed in her folly, the son of Zethus the ruler,
524 so is the heart in my breast drawn now in one way and now in the other:
525 whether I stay by my son and guard every thing in its place here,
526 all my possessions and servants and high-roofed house in the palace,
527 honoring still my husband’s bed and the voice of the people,
528 or if already I follow the best of Achaeans among those
529 courting me here in the palace, the one who gives numberless bride-gifts.
530 While my son was young and still foolish and feeble in judgment,
531 he would not let me remarry and leave the house of his father;
532 now, however, since he is full-grown and attains to the measure
533 of early manhood, he prays I shall leave and go back from the palace,
534 angry about the possessions which here the Achaeans devour.
535 Come now, interpret this dream and hear all the things I shall tell you:
536 twenty geese in the house are eating the grains of the wheat that
537 lie in the water beside them, and I look on with warm satisfaction.
538 Down from the mountain there came an enormous eagle with crook-beak,
539 broke every neck and killed them; together they lay in a heap there,
540 all in the halls, but the eagle was lifted to shining heaven.
541 I was lamenting and crying aloud although still in the dream-state;
542 fair-haired women of all the Achaeans gathered around me,
543 while I lamented in pity because the great eagle killed my geese.
544 Then it came back to the house and settled aloft on a roof-beam;
545 speaking aloud in a human voice, it restrained me and told me:
546 ‘Take heart, daughter of far-renowned Icarius; this is
547 not a dream but a favorable vision that shall be fulfilled here.
548 All of the geese are suitors; while I was an eagle beforehand,
549 now I have come as your husband, returned to you once more,
550 I who shall bring an unseemly doom upon all of the suitors.’
551 Thus it spoke, and the honey-sweet sleep then released me from dreaming;
552 looking around, I saw all the geese in the hall of the palace,
553 pecking the wheat at the trough in the place where they always had eaten.”
554 Then Odysseus of manifold counsel addressed her in answer:
555 “Lady, there is no way to interpret the dream by diverting
556 it to another fulfillment, since your own lord Odysseus
557 clearly declared how he brings it to pass: destruction is evident
558 for every suitor, and none will escape from his death and destruction.”
559 Then in her turn the prudent Penelope gave him an answer:
560 “Stranger, dreams are bewildering things and their words are uncertain;
561 not all that people behold in their dreams finds human fulfillment.
562 Two are the gates through which the powerless dreams issue outward:
563 one of them fashioned of horn and the other one fashioned of ivory.
564 Those among dreams which come through the ivory sawn into pieces
565 practice deception, and carry the words that attain no fulfillment;
566 those which come into daylight through polished gates made of horn there
567 bring what is true to fulfillment whenever a mortal beholds them.
568 Yet I do not believe my terrible dream came out from that gateway;
569 welcome indeed would it be to myself and my son if it did so.
570 Something else I shall tell you, and you must lay it inside you:
571 this ill-named dawn is coming, the dawn that shall sever me ever
572 from Odysseus’ house, for now I shall set up a contest,
573 using the axes which he in his palace would set in a long row,
574 all of the twelve, like timbers that prop up the keel of a vessel;
575 standing at great distance away, he would shoot an arrow through all of them.
576 Now I shall set up this contest among all the men who are suitors;
577 he who most easily strings the great bow in his hands there
578 and shoots an arrow through all twelve axes, that man I shall follow,
579 I shall follow him, leaving the house where I came as a bride then,
580 beautiful, filled to the brim with possessions and livelihood;
581 even in dreaming I think I shall always remember the household.”
582 Then Odysseus of manifold counsel addressed her in answer:
583 “Revered wife of Laertes’ son Odysseus, lady,
584 do not delay any longer to set up the contest inside here;
585 for Odysseus of manifold counsel will come here before those
586 men can have taken the polished bow in their hands and have handled it,
587 strung the bowstring and sent an arrow through all of the iron.”
588 Then in her turn the prudent Penelope gave him an answer:
589 “Stranger, if you were willing to sit in the palace beside me,
590 giving me joy, sleep never would pour on the lids of my two eyes.
591 Yet it is not in any way possible for humans to always be sleepless;
592 for the immortals appoint their proper portion to every one
593 of the mortal-born people who live on the grain-giving earth here.
594 Now, however, I shall go up to the upper rooms and lie down
595 there in my bed, which has grown for me a place full of sorrow,
596 always stained with my tears from the day that Odysseus departed,
597 going to look on accursed Ilium, never to be named.
598 There I shall lie; you lie yourself down in this house for the nighttime,
599 either spreading your bed on the floor or having these women make bedding.”
600 Thus having spoken, she went to the glorious upper apartments,
601 not alone, for her serving women went together beside her.
602 Into the upper rooms she ascended with all of her women,
603 then she lamented Odysseus, the husband she loved, till Athena,
604 gray-eyed goddess, poured sweet sleep on her eyelids and stilled her.
