# The Odyssey — Book XIII

*Odysseus comes home to Ithaca; Athena reveals the land and disguises him.*

1 Thus he spoke, and all of them there fell silent together,
2 held by the spell of his words in the shadowy halls of the palace.
3 Then in his turn Alcinous answered him, raising his voice there:
4 “Odysseus, now you have come to my bronze-founded house with its lofty
5 roof, and I think you will not go wandering backward hereafter,
6 homeward bound, though the measure of all you have suffered is heavy.
7 Now I address this charge to each man here among you,
8 all who continually drink the bright wine of the elders
9 here in my halls, while you listen as well to the song of the minstrel.
10 Garments already are laid for our guest in the well-polished strongbox,
11 gold of elaborate workmanship also, together with all the
12 gifts that Phaeacia’s counselors brought and presented among us.
13 Come then, let each man give him a sizeable tripod and cauldron;
14 afterward we, collecting the cost from the people among us,
15 shall repay it: one man finds lavish giving a hardship.”
16 Thus Alcinous spoke, and his counsel delighted the others.
17 Each of the men then departed for home and the comfort of sleeping.
18 Soon as the early-born Dawn showed forth with her rose-colored fingers,
19 down to the ship they hastened, all carrying man-strengthening bronze-work.
20 Revered Alcinous carefully stowed all the gifts they had carried,
21 going himself through the ship and beneath the rows of the benches,
22 lest they hamper a comrade at work when the oarsmen drove onward.
23 Then to Alcinous’ house they returned and prepared for the banquet.
24 Revered Alcinous slaughtered an ox in their midst for the honor
25 of Zeus, Cronus’ son of the darkening clouds, who is lord over all things.
26 Burning the thighs, they feasted upon the magnificent banquet,
27 taking their pleasure; among them the godlike singer made music,
28 Demodocus, honored among them; meanwhile Odysseus
29 upward his head toward the radiant brilliance and face of the sunlight,
30 yearning to see it go down, for his heart was afire to be homeward.
31 Just as a man longs eagerly after his supper, when all day
32 two wine-dark oxen have dragged the compacted plow through the fallow;
33 welcome to him is the moment the brilliance of sunlight has fallen,
34 calling him home to his supper, and weak are his knees as he journeys;
35 so to Odysseus welcome came the descent of the sunlight.
36 Straightway he spoke to Phaeacians, lovers and masters of oarcraft,
37 speaking his words above all to Alcinous, telling him plainly:
38 “Lord Alcinous, marked out for honor among all the people,
39 pour the libation and send me unharmed; may you prosper hereafter.
40 All is accomplished already that ever my own heart desired:
41 passage and welcoming gifts—may the gods who inhabit Olympus
42 prosper them for me. And may I find upon reaching my household
43 my blameless wife and the friends whom I love all safe and uninjured.
44 You who remain here, gladden your wedded wives in the household,
45 gladden your children; may gods grant every excellence to you,
46 every kind, and may no evil arise in your people.”
47 Thus he spoke, and all of the men gave praise and commanded
48 sending the stranger away, since his words had been rightly delivered.
49 Then the revered Alcinous turned to his herald and told him:
50 “Pontonoüs, mix us the wine in the bowl and distribute it freely
51 all through the hall, so that, having prayed to Zeus the Father,
52 we may send our guest to the land of his fathers in safety.”
53 Thus he spoke, and Pontonoüs blended the honey-sweet wine there,
54 passing a portion to everyone present in turn; from their places
55 all poured wine to the blessed gods who possess the broad heaven.
56 Then from his seat the noble Odysseus rose in their presence,
57 into Arete’s hands he placed the double-handled cup, and
58 lifted his voice and spoke these swift-winged words in her hearing:
59 “Queen, may you prosper forever, until there arrive in their season
60 age and death, which fall to the lot of all human beings.
61 Now I am going away; may you prosper and joy in this household,
62 joy in your children and people and lord Alcinous also.”
63 Thus having spoken, noble Odysseus stepped over the threshold.
64 With him revered Alcinous sent his herald to guide him,
65 leading the way to the swift ship waiting beside the seashore.
66 Queen Arete also sent serving women beside him,
67 one with a mantle thoroughly washed and a tunic to wear there;
68 next she appointed another to carry the strong wooden chest there;
69 while yet another carried the bread and the red wine beside them.
70 When they had come to the ship and arrived at the edge of the sea there,
71 readily into the hollow ship the renowned escorters
72 took and deposited all of the food and the drink they had brought there.
73 Then for Odysseus they spread a rug and a linen sheet under him,
74 high on the deck of the hollow-built ship, that his sleep be unbroken,
75 back at the stern; and he himself boarded and lay down to rest there;
76 silently, each at his oar, the others sat in their order.
77 then they released the cable that hung from the perforated stone there.
78 Soon as they leaned themselves backward and flung up the sea with their oar-blades,
79 sweet sleep fell on Odysseus’ eyelids and covered him wholly,
80 sound and most pleasant, the nearest of all things to death in its likeness.
81 Then, as a team of four stallions running together across the
82 plain, when they all surge forward beneath the blows of the lash-stroke,
83 lifting themselves high up, and swiftly accomplish their journey,
84 so the stern of the ship was lifted, while backward behind her
85 thundered a towering wave, dark-purple, of loud-roaring sea-swell.
86 Steadily onward she ran, unshaken; not even a falcon,
87 swiftest of all things winged, could have followed and matched her for fleetness.
88 Thus she ran on swiftly and cut through the waves of the sea there,
89 bearing the man whose designs were comparable even to gods’ plans,
90 one who before had suffered a multitude of pains in his spirit,
91 passing through wars among men and the wearisome breakers of ocean.
92 Now without stirring he slept, forgetting the griefs he had suffered.
93 When that star stood high which is brightest and more than another
94 rises as herald announcing the light of the early-born Dawn-goddess,
95 then the sea-faring vessel drew near to the shores of the island.
96 There is a harbor of Phorcys, the ancient one born of the sea-depths,
97 there in the land of Ithaca; two projecting and broken
98 headlands bend toward each other, embracing the mouth of the harbor.
99 These hold back the enormous waves that the harsh winds awaken
100 outside; within, well-benched ships can remain without mooring
101 once they have reached to the anchorage’s proper limit and measure.
102 There at the harbor’s head stands flourishing, long-leafing olive;
103 close by the tree is a lovely and shadowy cavern, a sacred
104 place of the nymphs whom the people call Naiads, daughters of waters.
105 Inside stand great bowls for blending and two-handled jars made of stone;
106 there in the hollow the bees build stores of their honey.
107 There are enormous looms made of stone, where the nymphs in the cavern
108 weave their garments of purple that gleams like the sea—a wonder.
109 There, too, are ever-flowing waters, and two are the doorways:
110 one opening downward toward the north wind gives mortals an entrance;
111 that toward the south belongs to the gods, and no mortal may enter
112 in by that way, for it is a road set apart for immortals.
113 Into this harbor they rowed, for they knew it well from aforetime;
114 half of the vessel’s length ran rapidly up on the dry land,
115 driven with speed, such force was behind it from hands of the oarsmen.
116 Then they descended out of the well-benched ship to the dry shore;
117 first they lifted Odysseus out of the hollow-built vessel,
118 carrying man and linen sheet and the splendid rug beneath him.
119 Down on the sand they laid him, subdued by the power of sleeping.
120 Next they brought out the property which the renowned Phaeacians
121 gave him for home, through great-hearted Athena’s beneficent counsel.
122 All of the goods they gathered beneath the trunk of the olive,
123 well off the roadway, lest one of the travelers passing that way should,
124 ere Odysseus woke, come upon them and cause any damage.
125 Then they journeyed homeward again. Yet the Earth-shaker did not
126 lose his remembrance of threats he had made to the godlike Odysseus
127 earlier still; and he questioned Zeus to discover his purpose:
128 “Father Zeus, I shall no longer be held in honor among the
129 deathless gods, when mortals show me no honor whatever—
130 even Phaeacians, although by descent they belong to my family.
131 Now, for I said Odysseus would come to his home after suffering
132 many afflictions; I never deprived him of homecoming wholly,
133 since at the first you promised his passage and nodded agreement.
134 These men carried him sleeping across the sea in a swift ship,
135 set him down in Ithaca, giving him limitless presents,
136 bronze and gold in abundance and finely woven apparel,
137 more than Odysseus ever could carry away out of Troia,
138 even if he had returned unharmed with his portion of plunder.”
139 Zeus who gathers the cloud banks spoke in reply to Poseidon:
140 “Ah, Earth-shaker of measureless strength, what word have you spoken?
141 Gods do not slight you at all; it would surely be difficult ever
142 heaping dishonor upon the eldest and best among us.
143 If some mortal man, though yielding before your strength and your power,
144 pays you no honor, the power of vengeance is always upon you.
145 Act as you wish, in whatever way your spirit finds pleasing.”
146 Then in his turn Poseidon, the shaker of earth, gave an answer:
147 “Straightway I would do as you say, O god of the dark clouds,
148 but I continually honor and dread your spirit’s displeasure.
149 Now I intend to shatter the beautiful ship of Phaeacians,
150 coming back home from escort, out on the mist-laden sea-way,
151 so that at last they will cease and abandon the escort of mortals;
152 also I mean to enclose their city beneath a great mountain.”
153 Zeus who gathers the cloud banks spoke in reply to Poseidon:
154 “Friend, to my mind the very best way of accomplishing this is:
155 once all the people look from the city and see her approaching,
156 set her as stone near the shore when she comes, and then shape her
157 like a swift ship, so that all who behold her may marvel,
158 all mortal humans; then cover their city with bulk of a mountain.”
159 Soon as Poseidon, the shaker of earth, had heard this decision,
160 straight to Scheria he went, where the Phaeacian people were living.
161 There he awaited them; very close drew the sea-faring vessel,
162 onward and rapidly driven; and close came the shaker of earth then.
163 There he transformed her to stone and rooted her deep in the seabed,
164 striking her once with the flat of his hand; then away he departed.
165 Winged words meanwhile they spoke to one another in wonder,
166 long-oared Phaeacians, seafaring men of renowned skill.
167 Looking at one who was near him, each man would utter his question:
168 “Ah, who has fastened the swift ship fast in the sea as she hurried
169 homeward? Already the whole of the vessel was coming in plain view.”
170 Thus each would question; they did not know how the thing had been fashioned.
171 Then Alcinous spoke in their midst and addressed the assembly:
172 “Gods! The oracles spoken of old by my father have reached me.
173 He would say that Poseidon begrudged us the harmless conveyance
174 which we provided to everyone journeying over the sea-way.
175 Once, he declared, a beautiful ship of Phaeacian sailors,
176 coming back home from escort over the mist-laden sea-way,
177 he would destroy, and enclose our city beneath a great mountain.
178 Thus spoke the old man; and now all his words are achieving fulfillment.
179 Come then, all of you follow whatever command I shall give you:
180 cease from escorting mortals whenever a mortal comes hither
181 into our city; and let us offer twelve chosen bulls now
182 unto Poseidon, in hope he will pity us, lest he should cover
183 all of our city around with the towering bulk of a mountain.”
184 Thus he spoke; they feared and prepared all the bulls for the altar.
185 So to Poseidon the king those men now lifted their prayers there,
186 leaders and governing lords of the land of Phaeacian people,
187 standing around his altar. But noble Odysseus awakened,
188 sleeping upon his ancestral soil, but he did not perceive it,
189 long having been away; for a goddess had poured mist around him—
190 Pallas Athena, the daughter of Zeus—that she might make him unknown there
191 while she explained each thing, and conceal him from everyone’s notice,
192 lest his wife recognize him, or townsmen or friends, before he
193 punished the suitors for every last act of their arrogant outrage.
194 Therefore all things showed themselves strange to the eyes of the ruler:
195 long-reaching footpaths and harbors affording a safe anchorage,
196 sheer overhanging rocks and the trees that were flourishing tall there.
197 Quickly he sprang to his feet and surveyed the beloved land around him;
198 then he groaned aloud and struck both of his thighs with the flat of
199 either hand, and lamenting he spoke this word of bewilderment:
200 “Ah, to the land of what mortal people have I come again now?
201 Are they violent, savage, and lacking in justice and fairness,
202 or are they friendly to strangers, their minds holding fear of the godhead?
203 Where shall I carry this mass of possessions? Where am I straying?
204 Would I had stayed where I was, in the land of Phaeacian people!
205 Then I could go to another of all the high-hearted rulers,
206 one who would cherish and send me away on my homeward journey.
207 Now I do not know where I can store them, but neither will I leave
208 these things here, lest another should seize them and take them as plunder.
209 Gods! The leaders and governing lords of Phaeacian people
210 were not entirely prudent or just, it would seem, after all things:
211 they have conveyed me to some other land, although they had promised
212 Ithaca bright in the sunlight; their promise they have not accomplished.
213 Zeus of the suppliants punish them, he who watches all other
214 mortal beings, and visits with vengeance the one who transgresses.
215 Come then, I shall count and examine the whole of my property,
216 lest they have stolen away with some in the hold of their vessel.”
217 Thus having spoken, the beautiful tripods together with cauldrons
218 he counted, the gold and the beautiful woven garments beside them.
219 None was missing; nevertheless he mourned for the land of his fathers,
220 crawling along the shore of the sea that resounds with a great roar,
221 making his manifold moans. Then Athena approached him from near by,
222 taking the bodily form of a young man, a shepherd of sheep flocks,
223 tender and delicate, such as the children of rulers are often.
224 Round both shoulders he wore the well-fashioned fold of a mantle;
225 under his glistening feet were sandals, his hands held a spear-shaft.
226 Seeing him, brave Odysseus rejoiced and came forward to meet him;
227 lifting his voice, he spoke these swift-winged words in his hearing:
228 “Friend, since you are the first I encounter here in this country,
229 greetings, and do not encounter me here with an evil intention.
230 Save my possessions here and save me; for I make prayer to
231 you as a god and come as a suppliant clasping your dear knees.
232 Tell me this thing also in truth, so I clearly may know it:
233 what is this land, what people, and what kind of men have been born here?
234 Is it an island clear to the eye, or some projecting
235 coast of a deep-soiled mainland, sloping away into sea-swell?”
236 Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena addressed him in answer:
237 “Stranger, you must be foolish or else have arrived from a distant
238 country, if you ask after this land; it is surely not so
239 wholly without a name, but exceedingly many have known it,
240 all who inhabit the country toward both the Dawn and the sunlight,
241 likewise all those dwelling behind toward shadowy darkness.
242 Rugged indeed is the island and not a good country for horses,
243 yet it is not too poor, though it has not broad land in abundance.
244 Grain beyond telling grows in the soil, and the island gives wine-grapes;
245 always the showers possess it, and flourishing dews lie upon it.
246 Excellent pasture for goats and cattle; there also is woodland
247 of every kind, and watering places that never diminish.
248 Therefore the name of Ithaca, stranger, has reached even Troia,
249 though they say it is far from the fertile Achaean country.”
250 Thus spoke the goddess; much-enduring noble Odysseus was gladdened,
251 taking delight in his fatherland, once she had named it before him—
252 Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus who carries the aegis.
253 Lifting his voice, he spoke these swift-winged words in her hearing;
254 yet he withheld what was true and fashioned another account there,
255 always revolving the mind of manifold gain in his bosom:
256 “I have heard tell of Ithaca even in broad-open Crete-land,
257 far over sea; and now I have come to the island in person,
258 bringing these goods, while leaving as much for my children behind me.
259 Now I am fleeing because I have killed a beloved son of Idomeneus,
260 swift-footed Orsilochus, who in the broad-open country of Crete-land
261 bested all bread-eating men in the speed of his feet when he raced them.
262 He had desired to strip me of all of the plunder from Troia,
263 that for whose sake I suffered such manifold grief in my spirit,
264 passing through wars among men and the wearisome breakers of ocean,
265 since I had never served at his father’s side to win favor
266 there in the Trojan country, but led companions of my own.
267 Coming back home from the fields, I struck him with bronze-headed spear-point,
268 ambushed close by the road with a comrade in arms to assist me.
269 Utterly dark was the night that possessed the broad heaven; no person
270 noticed us there, and I secretly stole the life from his body.
271 Soon as I killed that man with the edge of my bronze in the darkness,
272 straight to a ship I went and entreated illustrious Phoenicians,
273 giving the men an acceptable share of my plunder as payment.
274 I told them to carry me onward and set me ashore at Pylos,
275 or in the sacred land of Elis, where Epeians are ruling.
276 Yet the force of the wind drove them away from that place and its shoreline,
277 much against all their desire—they did not intend to deceive me.
278 Driven astray from there, we came to this place in the nighttime.
279 Hard did we row to the harbor, and nobody thought of his supper,
280 though we were badly in need and desired to take our refreshment;
281 just as we were, all of us left the ship and lay down there.
282 Sweet sleep came over me there, for my body was weary from labor;
283 meanwhile, taking the goods that were mine from the hollow-built vessel,
284 they set them here where I lay on the sands of the sea when they found me.
285 Boarding again, they departed for Sidon, the well-peopled city,
286 while I was left here alone with a sorrowful heart in my bosom.”
287 Thus he spoke; and smiling, the gray-eyed goddess Athena
288 stroked him gently by hand; then her body resembled a woman,
289 beautiful, tall, and accomplished in glorious works of the handcraft.
290 Lifting her voice, she spoke these swift-winged words in his hearing:
291 “Cunning and thievish indeed must the person be who could surpass you
292 in all manner of guile, even if a god should encounter you.
293 Stubborn, devious schemer, insatiate master of trickery,
294 even at home in your land you were not to abandon deception
295 or those thievish tales that are dear to your heart from its bedrock.
296 Come, let us speak no longer of this, since both of us know well
297 cunning: you are the best among all mortal men in the counsel
298 and in the framing of speeches, while I among all of the godheads
299 have won fame for my craft and my cunning. Yet you did not know me,
300 Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus, who always beside you
301 stands in every affliction and keeps you under her guarding.
302 I made all Phaeacian people love you and show you their kindness.
303 Now I have come here to weave a plan and counsel beside you,
304 also to hide the wealth that the excellent men of Phaeacia
305 gave you for home, by my design and thoughtful intention,
306 and to tell all the sorrows your fate in the well-built halls
307 bids you endure; you must bear them, constrained by necessity.
308 Speak no word to anyone, neither a man nor a woman,
309 telling that you have come from your wandering, but you must silently
310 suffer your manifold griefs and submit to the violence of menfolk.”
311 Then Odysseus of manifold counsel addressed her in answer:
312 “Hard is it, goddess, for any mortal meeting to know you,
313 even a knowledgeable man, for you liken yourself to all people.
314 This I know full well: you were gracious and friendly beside me
315 all the while that we sons of Achaeans were fighting at Troia.
316 Yet when at last we had sacked the precipitous city of Priam,
317 boarded the ships, and a god scattered all the Achaean people,
318 then I did not see you, O daughter of Zeus, or perceive you
319 coming aboard my vessel to ward any grief from beside me.
320 Always I wandered about with a heart that was torn in my bosom,
321 till from the grip of affliction the gods at last had released me—
322 until, among the Phaeacian men in the heart of their rich land,
323 you gave me courage with words and yourself led me into the city.
324 Now by your father I clasp your knees—for I cannot believe I
325 really have come to Ithaca, clear to be seen, but am roaming
326 round some other land. I think you are speaking in mockery,
327 saying these words with intention to cheat and beguile my perception.
328 Tell me whether in truth I have come to my own beloved country.”
329 Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena addressed him in answer:
330 “Always a purpose like this is deep in the heart of your bosom;
331 therefore I cannot abandon you even in wretched misfortune,
332 since you are gentle in manner and shrewd and possessed of good judgment.
333 Any other man, when he came from wandering, gladly would hurry
334 into his halls with desire to behold both his children and wife there.
335 Yet it is not your wish to be told or to learn any tidings,
336 not till you first put your wife to the proof: she is still as she was then,
337 sitting within your palace, and miserable always her nighttime
338 and her days waste away as she sheds her tears in her sorrow.
339 As for myself, I never was doubtful, but knew in my spirit
340 you would return after losing the whole of your band of companions.
341 Yet I was not willing to fight with the lord Poseidon,
342 brother of my own father, who planted the wrath in his spirit,
343 angered because you blinded the son that he loved and begot there.
344 Come then, I shall show you the landmarks of Ithaca; trust me.
345 Here is the harbor of Phorcys, the ancient one born of the sea-depths;
346 here at the harbor’s head stands flourishing, long-leafing olive;
347 close by the tree is a lovely and shadowy cavern, a sacred
348 place of the nymphs whom the people call Naiads, daughters of waters.
349 This is the wide-vaulted cavern where many a sacrifice, perfect
350 hecatombs, you once offered in honor to each of the nymphs there;
351 this is the mountain of Neriton, clothed with the forest above it.”
352 Thus spoke the goddess, scattering mist, and the country appeared there.
353 Much-enduring noble Odysseus was gladdened to see it,
354 joying in his own land, and he kissed the grain-giving earth there.
355 Straightway he prayed to the nymphs with his hands uplifted above him:
356 “Naiad nymphs, O daughters of Zeus, I had thought that I never
357 would behold you again; with my loving prayers now I greet you.
358 Gifts we will give you hereafter as once we had done in the old days,
359 if Zeus’ daughter, the giver of spoils, in her kindness permits me
360 both to remain alive and my own dear son to be strengthened.”
361 Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena addressed him in answer:
362 “Take heart; do not let any of this weigh down on your thinking.
363 Let us immediately put your possessions away in the deepest
364 part of the marvelous cave, so that all may remain in their safety;
365 then we ourselves shall consider the very best way to proceed here.”
366 Thus spoke the goddess, and entered the shadowy cave at its doorway,
367 searching the cavern’s recesses; meanwhile, noble Odysseus
368 carried all nearer—the gold and the unyielding bronze-work;
369 also the well-fashioned garments which men of Phaeacia gave him.
370 Carefully there he stored them; a stone at the doors of the cavern
371 Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus who carries the aegis,
372 placed. Then both sat down by the trunk of the sacred-grown olive,
373 plotting destruction together against the insolent suitors.
374 First of the two to begin was the gray-eyed goddess Athena:
375 “Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus, master of many
376 stratagems, consider how you will lay hands on the shameless
377 suitors, who now for three years have lorded it over your household,
378 courting your godlike wife and presenting their gifts for the wedding.
379 Always she mourns for your homecoming deep in the heart of her bosom;
380 every man she encourages, making a promise to each one,
381 sending him messages, while her purpose is bent upon other things.”
382 Then Odysseus of manifold counsel addressed her in answer:
383 “Gods! I was surely about to share Agamemnon son of Atreus’
384 pitiful fate and perish within the halls of my palace,
385 had you not told me, goddess, the proper account of each matter.
386 Come now, weave me a scheme whereby I may punish the suitors.
387 Stand by me yourself and inspire my heart with courageous
388 force like that which I had when we loosened the bright crown of Troia.
389 If with such eagerness you, gray-eyed one, stood there beside me,
390 I would fight even three hundred men in the heat of the battle,
391 with you, sovereign goddess, whenever you eagerly helped me.”
392 Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena addressed him in answer:
393 “Truly I shall be beside you, nor shall you find me absent,
394 when we begin this work; and I think one man will bespatter
395 all the immense floor with blood from his body and brains from his skull—
396 one of the suitor-men who consume your life and possessions.
397 Come, I shall make you unrecognizable now to all mortals:
398 I shall shrivel the beautiful skin on your flexible members,
399 strip the blond hair from your head, and clothe you in rags that another
400 person will hate in his heart at the sight of a wearer approaching.
401 Both of your eyes, which were beautiful once, I shall dim and disfigure,
402 so to all of the suitors you seem an unsightly vagabond,
403 even your wife and the son you left in the halls of your palace.
404 You yourself must first go straight to the man who tends your swine herd,
405 guardian over your pigs, who remains well disposed and devoted to you,
406 loving the son that you love and prudent Penelope also.
407 You will discover him sitting beside the swine; they are grazing
408 near to the Raven’s Rock by the fountain known as Arethusa,
409 eating the heartening acorns and drinking the dark-running water,
410 all that develops and nourishes flourishing fat upon swine flesh.
411 Stay in that place, and sit by his side while you question him fully,
412 till I have gone to the lovely-womaned country of Sparta,
413 calling Telemachus home, your beloved son, Odysseus.
414 He to the broad dancing places of Lacedaemon and Menelaus
415 went to inquire for news and report of you, whether you still live.”
416 Then Odysseus of manifold counsel addressed her in answer:
417 “Why did you not tell him, when all things were known to your wisdom?
418 Was it that he, too, should wander and suffer affliction and sorrow,
419 over the barren sea, while others consume his possessions?”
420 Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena addressed him in answer:
421 “Do not allow concern about him to possess you too deeply.
422 I was the guide of his journey, so he might win a good glory,
423 going to Sparta. No labor afflicts him, but sitting at leisure,
424 safe in the palace of Atreus’ son, he has plenty around him.
425 True, young men in a black-hulled vessel are lying in ambush,
426 eager to kill him before he can come to the land of his fathers;
427 yet I do not think this will be, before the earth has closed over
428 some of the men among suitors who now are consuming your living.”
429 Thus having spoken, Athena touched him once with her wand-staff.
430 She made the beautiful skin on his flexible members all shrivel,
431 stripped the blond hair from his head, and over the whole of his body
432 laid the skin of a man made old by the length of his lifetime.
433 Both of his eyes, which were beautiful once, she dimmed and disfigured;
434 round him she cast another vile rag and a tunic upon it,
435 tattered and filthy with grime and blackened by foul-smelling smoke-stains.
436 Over them all she covered him up in an enormous deerskin,
437 stripped of its hair; she gave him a staff and an ugly old carry-bag,
438 riddled with holes, and the twisted cord that suspended it from him.
439 Thus having made their plans, they parted as each took a pathway;
440 she to the sacred country of Lacedaemon, to find his son there.
