# The Odyssey — Book XVII

*Telemachus comes home; Odysseus enters his own hall.*

1 When the Dawn born early appeared, her fingers like roses,
2 then Telemachus bound his beautiful sandals beneath him,
3 he who was dear son born to Odysseus, godlike in counsel,
4 grasped the powerful spear that fitted the grip of his hand well,
5 meaning to go to the city, and spoke to his faithful swineherd:
6 “Father, now I shall go to the city, so that my mother
7 sees me; for I do not think she will ever stop her lamenting,
8 terrible weeping and tearful grief that she suffers,
9 until she sees me herself. But this is the order I give you:
10 lead this unfortunate stranger to town, so there he can beg for
11 dinner; whoever desires will give him a portion,
12 biscuit and cup; but I cannot shoulder every mortal’s afflictions,
13 since I myself have a heart already burdened with sorrow.
14 If the stranger is terribly angry, it will be worse for
15 him; for I always love to speak what is truthful and honest.”
16 Then the resourceful lord Odysseus answered Telemachus:
17 “Friend, I myself have no wish to linger here or be hindered.
18 Better it is for a beggar in town than out in the farmland
19 there to beg for his food; whoever desires will give me a portion.
20 I am no longer the age to remain at the stead of the farmyard,
21 able to do everything that a master commands and directs me.
22 Go on; the man whom you order will lead me there to the city,
23 soon as I warm by the fire and the air becomes warmer around us.
24 Dreadfully poor are these garments I wear; may nothing defeat me,
25 no morning frost, since you say the city is far in the distance.”
26 So he spoke. Telemachus went out through the farmstead,
27 striding along on swift feet, and planted disaster for suitors.
28 When he arrived at last at the beautifully settled dwelling,
29 first he stood with his spear and leaned it against a tall pillar,
30 then went into the hall, crossing over the threshold of stone there.
31 Eurycleia his nurse was by far the first to behold him,
32 spreading the fleeces of sheep on elaborately fashioned armchairs.
33 Weeping, she came straight toward him; the other women who served in
34 much-enduring Odysseus’ household gathered around him,
35 kissed him with love as they welcomed him, kissing his head and his shoulders.
36 Circumspect Penelope came from the room of her sleeping,
37 looking like Artemis there or Aphrodite the golden.
38 Weeping, she threw both her arms around the son she so cherished,
39 kissed him upon his head and the beautiful light of his two eyes;
40 then in lament she addressed him aloud with the following winged words:
41 “You have come, Telemachus, sweet light! I no longer imagined
42 I would behold you again when you went in a ship to Pylos,
43 secretly, despite me, seeking a report of your father.
44 Come now and tell me clearly whatever you saw on your journey.”
45 Then wise-minded Telemachus answered his mother directly:
46 “Mother, do not stir mourning in me or trouble the heart in
47 my breast, now I have fled from precipitous death and destruction.
48 Wash yourself clean with water and take clean clothes for your body,
49 go upstairs in the house with all of the women attending,
50 pray to the gods you will offer them perfect hecatombs duly,
51 if Zeus ever brings deeds of requital to their completion.
52 I shall go to the place of assembly, meaning to summon
53 home the guest who followed me here when I came from that country.
54 I sent him on ahead in the care of my godlike companions,
55 telling Piraeus to lead him away to his own house,
56 giving him welcome and honor until I myself should arrive there.”
57 So he spoke, and the word remained there wingless within her.
58 She washed herself with water and took clean clothes for her body,
59 prayed to the gods she would offer them perfect hecatombs duly,
60 if Zeus ever brought deeds of requital to their completion.
61 Then Telemachus went out again through the hall of the household,
62 holding his spear, and two swift-footed dogs went beside him.
63 Marvelous grace was poured on him then by the goddess Athena;
64 all of the people admired him as he came walking among them.
65 Insolent suitors gathered around him closely together,
66 speaking him fair, while deep in their hearts they were brooding on evils.
67 He avoided the great throng of them and went to the places
68 where sat Mentor and Antiphus there and Halitherses with them,
69 men who had been companions of his father from the beginning.
70 There he sat down, and they asked him of every separate detail.
71 Spear-famed Piraeus then came near and approached their gathering,
72 leading the guest through the city to where the people assembled.
73 Nor did Telemachus long remain at a distance from him,
74 but stood beside him. Piraeus first addressed him and told him:
75 “Send your women at once to my home, Telemachus, so I
76 send you the gifts Menelaus gave you to carry.”
77 Then wise-minded Telemachus gave him an answer directly:
78 “How these things will turn out, Piraeus, we cannot yet know it.
79 If the arrogant suitors secretly kill me at home there,
80 then divide all of the wealth of my fathers among them,
81 I would prefer you yourself to possess and enjoy them, not these men.
82 But if I plant destruction and death in the midst of those suitors,
83 then in your joy bring them to my house and delight me, rejoicing.”
84 So having spoken, he led the long-suffering stranger homeward.
85 When they arrived at last at the beautifully settled dwelling,
86 first they laid their mantles down on the couches and armchairs,
87 then stepped into the finely polished bathtubs and washed there.
88 When all the servant women had bathed them and rubbed them with olive oil,
89 also wrapped fleecy mantles and tunics around them,
90 out of the bathtubs they stepped and sat themselves down on the armchairs.
91 Water for washing was carried and poured by a maid from a pitcher,
92 beautiful, golden, above a silver basin beneath it,
93 so they could wash, and she drew a polished table beside them.
94 Bread the venerable housekeeper carried and set there before them,
95 adding a wealth of food, giving freely of all in her keeping.
96 Opposite them by the pillar of the great hall sat his mother,
97 leaning back in a chair as she twisted the delicate yarn there.
98 Both men reached out their hands to the good things lying before them.
99 When they had put aside desire for drinking and eating,
100 circumspect Penelope started to speak and addressed them:
101 “Now, Telemachus, I shall go up again to the upper
102 room and lie in my bed, which has been a place of lamenting,
103 always befouled with the tears from my eyes since the day that Odysseus
104 went with the sons of Atreus to Ilios. Yet you did not dare,
105 before the arrogant suitors came to this house for their banquet,
106 clearly to tell me the news of your father’s return, if you heard it.”
107 Then wise-minded Telemachus gave her an answer directly:
108 “Mother, I therefore shall tell you the truth of the matter.
109 We went on to Pylos and Nestor, the shepherd of peoples.
110 He received me inside the lofty halls of his household,
111 giving me love and care as a father would give his own dear son,
112 newly returned after long years spent in a faraway country.
113 So he cared for me well together with all of his glorious children.
114 Yet of much-enduring Odysseus, Nestor declared he
115 never had heard any earth-dweller say whether living or fallen.
116 But he sent me to Atreus’ son, the spear-famous hero
117 Menelaus, with horses and close-joined chariot to bear me.
118 There I saw Helen of Argos, for whose sake many
119 Argives and Trojans suffered and toiled by the will of immortals.
120 Menelaus, good at the war cry, asked me directly
121 what desire had brought me to sacred Lacedaemon’s country.
122 Then I related to him every truth of the matter,
123 and at that point he replied to me and addressed me with these words:
124 ‘Shame! In the bed of a man whose spirit is mighty these cowards
125 wished to lie down, though they themselves have no courage within them.
126 Just as a doe in the woodland lair of a powerful lion
127 beds her newborn young and her milk-fed fawns there together,
128 then searches the slopes and the grassy valleys that cover the mountains;
129 she goes to graze, but the lion returns to his lair and imposes
130 on both of the young ones together a shameful destruction,
131 so shall Odysseus bring on those men a shameful destruction.
132 Father Zeus and Athena and Lord Apollo, if only
133 he were the man that he was in well-built Lesbos, when once he
134 stood and wrestled the son of Philomeleus in contention,
135 threw him down with his might, and all the Achaeans rejoiced there—
136 would that Odysseus meet the suitors in this kind of power!
137 All would be swift in their deaths and find their marriages bitter.
138 As for the things you entreat me to answer and ask me directly,
139 I shall not turn from the truth into other things and deceive you;
140 rather, whatever the truthful old god of the sea has reported,
141 not one word will I hide from you or conceal in my answer.
142 He said he saw him upon an island in terrible sorrow,
143 inside the halls of Calypso the nymph, who by force and compulsion
144 holds him; and he has no power to come to the land of his fathers,
145 since he possesses no oar-equipped ships and companions
146 able to carry him over the broad-ridged back of the seaway.’
147 So Atreus’ spear-famous son Menelaus had spoken.
148 Finishing all of my mission, I sailed, and the gods gave a fair wind,
149 those who have sent me swiftly again to the land of my fathers.”
150 So he spoke, and the spirit was stirred in her breast as she heard him.
151 Godlike Theoclymenus also addressed them directly:
152 “Revered wife of Laertes’ son Odysseus, listen:
153 he does not clearly know, but attend to the word I shall tell you,
154 for I shall prophesy truly and hide not a word of my knowledge.
155 Zeus be my witness first of the gods, and this table of strangers,
156 also the hearth of blameless Odysseus, to which I have come here:
157 truly Odysseus is already here in his homeland,
158 sitting or moving, learning of all these wicked proceedings;
159 he is here now and is planting disaster for all of the suitors.
160 Such was the bird sign I observed on the well-benched vessel,
161 sitting there, and proclaimed it aloud to Telemachus clearly.”
162 Circumspect Penelope gave him an answer directly:
163 “Would that this word, my guest, might come to its final fulfillment!
164 Soon you would know my friendship and many gifts I would grant you,
165 all from myself, so whoever encountered you afterward called you blessed.”
166 Thus they were speaking together and answering one another.
167 Meanwhile suitors in front of the palace hall of Odysseus
168 took their delight in throwing the discus and casting the javelin,
169 out on the leveled court where they always displayed their arrogance.
170 But when it came to the hour of dinner, and sheep from the pasture
171 came from every field, brought in by their usual herdsmen,
172 then Medon spoke to the suitors, the herald who most of all pleased them,
173 best of the heralds in their regard and always beside them at banquets:
174 “Youths, now all of your hearts have taken delight in the contests;
175 come to the house, so that we may make the banquet in order.
176 Nothing is worse than failing to take your meal at the right time.”
177 So he spoke; they stood up and went in obedience to him.
178 When they arrived at last at the beautifully settled dwelling,
179 first they laid their mantles down on the couches and armchairs;
180 then they slaughtered great sheep and goats exceedingly fat there,
181 slaughtered the fattened pigs and an ox from the herd for the banquet,
182 making the feast. Meanwhile from the countryside toward the city
183 lord Odysseus and the noble swineherd hastened to travel.
184 Then the swineherd, a leader among men, began speaking before him:
185 “Stranger, since you are eager now to go to the city
186 on this day, as my master commanded—though I would prefer you
187 stay as a guardian here and defender of all of the farmstead,
188 yet I respect and fear him, lest he afterward harshly
189 scold me; the words of rebuke from a master are hard to be borne there—
190 come now, let us go. The day has already gone greatly
191 onward, and soon toward evening the weather will grow colder round you.”
192 Then the resourceful lord Odysseus answered Eumaeus:
193 “I know and understand; you command a man who is thinking.
194 Let us go, and continue to lead me over the whole way.
195 Give me a staff as well if somewhere you have one cut for
196 me to support myself, since you said that the road is exceedingly slippery.”
197 So he spoke, and he threw an ugly bag round his shoulders,
198 densely tattered, with twisted cord for the strap that sustained it.
199 Then Eumaeus gave him a staff that delighted his spirit.
200 Both set off, while the dogs and the herding men who remained there
201 guarded the stead at their back; and the swineherd guided his master
202 into the town, though he looked like a wretched and elderly beggar,
203 leaning upon the staff with miserable clothes round his body.
204 But as they went on their way down over the rough, stony roadway,
205 drawing near to the city, they came to the well-fashioned fountain,
206 flowing fair, from which the people who lived in the city drew water.
207 Ithacus fashioned it once with Neritus, also Polyctor.
208 Round it a grove of the water-fed poplar trees extended,
209 circling everywhere; cold water streamed from a high-set
210 rock, and above it an altar had been constructed and founded,
211 sacred to Nymphs, where all who traveled the way made offerings.
212 There the son of Dolius met them, Melanthius goatherd,
213 driving goats that surpassed all others in every herd of the she-goats,
214 dinner for all of the suitors; two herdsmen followed behind him.
215 Seeing the two, he reviled them and shouted abusive names at them,
216 terrible, shameful words, and stirred Odysseus’ spirit within him:
217 “Look at it now: one thoroughly wicked man leads another!
218 Always a god brings like to his like, the one to the other.
219 Where are you taking this greedy glutton, you luckless swineherd,
220 troublesome beggar and nuisance appointed to spoil every banquet?
221 Standing at many doorposts, he will bruise both his shoulders,
222 begging for scraps, not asking for cauldrons and swords to be given.
223 If you would give him to me as a guardian here at the farmstead,
224 sweeping the pens and carrying fresh green fodder to goat-kids,
225 then, while he drank the whey, he could put good flesh on his thighbones.
226 But he has learned only wicked work and will never consent to
227 labor at anything; skulking about the country forever,
228 begging, he wants to feed the insatiable gut in his body.
229 Yet I shall tell you a thing that will surely come to fulfillment:
230 if he should enter the halls of Odysseus, godlike in counsel,
231 many a footstool hurled from the hands of men round his head there
232 will scrape his ribs as he runs through the house amid all of the missiles.”
233 So he spoke, and as he passed he kicked out in his folly,
234 striking the hip, but he did not thrust him out of the pathway,
235 for he remained there firm. But Odysseus pondered within him
236 whether to leap on the man and beat out his life with the staff there,
237 or lift him up and dash his head on the ground with both hands.
238 Yet he endured, restraining the heart in his breast. But Eumaeus
239 looked on the man face-to-face, denounced him and prayed with hands lifted:
240 “Nymphs of the fountain, daughters of Zeus, if ever Odysseus
241 burned for you thighbones and covered them over with rich fat,
242 taken from lambs and kids, bring this one wish to fulfillment:
243 let that man return; let a spirit conduct him among us!
244 Then he would scatter away all the splendor you carry around you,
245 all of the splendor you insolently carry, forever
246 roaming the city, while worthless herdsmen ruin the flocks you abandon.”
247 Then Melanthius, goat-herder, spoke to the swineherd and answered:
248 “Look at the murderous words this dog who knows evil is barking!
249 One day I shall put him aboard a black well-benched vessel,
250 carry him far from Ithaca, where he will earn me a fortune.
251 Would that Apollo the silver-bowed might strike Telemachus
252 down in his halls this day, or suitors might bring him destruction,
253 since the day of return for Odysseus perished far from us.”
254 So having spoken, he left the two as they slowly came onward;
255 he went ahead and soon reached the house of the king there.
256 Straightway he went inside and sat himself down with the suitors,
257 facing Eurymachus, whom he loved more than all of the others.
258 Those who attended the meal set a portion of meat there beside him;
259 bread the venerable housekeeper carried and set there
260 ready to eat. Close by Odysseus and the noble swineherd
261 stopped on arriving, while round them floated the clear sound
262 of a hollow lyre, for their singer rose and began singing;
263 Phemius; then Odysseus took the swineherd’s hand and addressed him:
264 “Surely these beautiful halls are Odysseus’ own house, Eumaeus.
265 Easy it is to distinguish and see them among many others:
266 building follows on building; the courtyard is fashioned and finished
267 both with a wall and coping, and well-barred double doors guard it.
268 No mere man could easily storm it and overpower its defenses.
269 I know a multitude here must now be holding a banquet,
270 since the odor of meat rises up and the lyre resounds here,
271 lyre that the gods have fashioned to partner the feast and the banquet.”
272 Then in reply, swineherd Eumaeus, you spoke and addressed him:
273 “Easily you recognized it, since you are not witless in other things.
274 Come now, let us consider the way this business shall happen.
275 Either you enter first in the beautifully settled dwelling,
276 mixing among the suitors, while I remain here behind you;
277 or, if you want, wait here and I shall go on before you.
278 Do not delay here long, lest someone outside should see you,
279 strike you with something or drive you off; I command you to heed this.”
280 Then much-enduring, godlike Odysseus answered Eumaeus:
281 “I know and understand; you command a man who is thinking.
282 Go on ahead while I remain in this place here behind you.
283 I am no stranger to beatings or missiles hurled against me;
284 this heart is enduring, since I have suffered many afflictions
285 on waves and in war; let this too be added among them.
286 Yet there is no way to hide a belly that ravenously clamors,
287 cursed thing that gives to mortal men so many afflictions.
288 For its sake even well-joined ships are fitted and readied,
289 crossing the barren sea and bearing distress to their foemen.”
290 Thus with words such as these they spoke to each other.
291 Lying nearby, a dog raised up his head and his two ears,
292 Argos, the dog of much-enduring Odysseus, whom once his
293 master himself had reared but never enjoyed before he departed
294 onward to sacred Ilios. In former days all of the young men
295 used to conduct him after wild goats and deer and the hares there.
296 Now he lay cast aside, since his master had gone from the country,
297 deep in the dung that lay in abundance before the gateways,
298 poured there from mules and cattle, until Odysseus’ servants
299 carried it out to enrich the great estate of Odysseus.
300 Argos the dog lay there, completely covered with dog ticks.
301 But in that moment, perceiving Odysseus standing beside him,
302 softly he wagged with his tail and lowered both ears on his head there;
303 yet he no longer had strength to come any closer and could not
304 reach his master; Odysseus turned aside and wiped off a teardrop,
305 easily hiding it from Eumaeus, then quickly he questioned:
306 “This is a marvel, Eumaeus, the dog that lies here in the dunghill.
307 Beautiful is his form, but this is a thing I cannot determine:
308 whether he also was swift on his feet with a body so handsome,
309 or was the kind of dog that men keep at the table,
310 one with no other purpose, whom masters tend for appearance.”
311 Then in reply, swineherd Eumaeus, you spoke and addressed him:
312 “Yes, this dog belonged to a man who died far away now.
313 If he were still such a creature in form and in deeds as he was then,
314 when Odysseus left him and went to the country of Troia,
315 soon you would marvel to see his speed and the power he once had.
316 Never a quarry he hunted escaped in the depths of the woodland,
317 any beast he pursued; and he surpassed all dogs at tracking.
318 Now he is held in misery, while his master, far from his homeland,
319 perished; and careless women give him no tending.
320 Servants, whenever the masters no longer command them and govern,
321 cease to desire any longer to do their rightful labors;
322 Zeus far-thundering takes one half of the virtue and value
323 out of a man on the day when slavery takes him and binds him.”
324 So he spoke and went into the beautifully settled dwelling,
325 straight to the hall and the glorious suitors gathered within it.
326 Argos, however, black death now took in accordance with fate,
327 instantly after he saw Odysseus in the twentieth year there.
328 Godlike Telemachus first of them all saw the swineherd
329 coming along through the house—the swineherd—and swiftly thereafter
330 nodded and called him; he looked around him and lifted a footstool,
331 where the divider of meat would sit at his work in the banquet,
332 cutting the many pieces for suitors who feasted throughout the household.
333 Carrying this, he set it beside Telemachus’ table,
334 facing him there, and then he sat himself down; and the herald
335 took him a portion and set it, then lifted bread from the basket.
336 Close behind him Odysseus entered the halls of the household,
337 looking like some unfortunate beggar, aged and wretched,
338 leaning upon the staff with miserable clothes round his body.
339 Down on the ashwood threshold inside of the doors he seated,
340 leaning against the doorpost of cypress wood, which a craftsman
341 once had expertly planed and trued to a line for its making.
342 Then Telemachus called the swineherd to him and told him,
343 taking a whole loaf of bread from the beautiful basket and also
344 meat, as much as his hands when spread out wide could enclose there:
345 “Carry and give these things to the stranger himself, and command him
346 go and beg from every suitor in turn in the household.
347 Shame in the presence of need does a destitute man no good there.”
348 So he spoke, and the swineherd went when he heard the commandment,
349 then, drawing close to the stranger, addressed him with the following winged words:
350 “These things Telemachus gives you, stranger, and he commands you
351 go and beg from every suitor in turn in the household;
352 he says shame is no good companion to anyone begging.”
353 Then the resourceful lord Odysseus answered Eumaeus:
354 “Lord Zeus, let Telemachus live among men as a blessed one,
355 and may everything come to him that the heart in him longs for.”
356 So he spoke, then accepted it all in both hands and set it
357 there at his feet on top of the bag that was ugly and tattered.
358 Then he ate while the bard sang on in the halls of the household.
359 Just as he finished his dinner, the godlike singer concluded.
360 Suitors filled all the hall with uproar. Meanwhile Athena
361 stood close by Laertes’ son, Odysseus, godlike in counsel,
362 urging him onward to gather the bread from the suitors, so he might
363 know which men respected the laws and which were lawless.
364 Even so, she intended to spare not one from disaster.
365 Off he went to beg of every man from the right side,
366 holding his hand out everywhere like a long-practiced beggar.
367 Pitying him, they gave and were filled with wonder before him,
368 asking each other about who he was and from where he had come there.
369 Then Melanthius, goat-herder, also addressed them and told them:
370 “Listen to me, you suitors of our illustrious queen here,
371 as to the matter of this guest; I have seen him already.
372 Truly the swineherd was guiding this man here to the household,
373 but I do not know the man or the land he declares as his birthplace.”
374 So he spoke. Then Antinous chided the swineherd with harsh words:
375 “Notorious swineherd, why have you brought to the city
376 such a man? Do we not have vagabonds enough already,
377 troublesome beggars, the kind that destroy the delight of the banquet?
378 Are you angered because they consume all your master’s possessions,
379 gathering here, so you even invited this man to join them?”
380 Then in reply, swineherd Eumaeus, you spoke and addressed him:
381 “Antinous, though you are noble, the words you are saying are shameful.
382 Who in the world goes out and summons a stranger from elsewhere,
383 unless he is one of the men whose work serves all of the people,
384 whether a prophet, a healer of sickness, a builder of timber,
385 or an inspired bard who delights all men with his singing?
386 Those are the mortals invited throughout the limitless country;
387 no man calls in a beggar who only will wear him away there.
388 Always you are the cruelest among all the men who are suitors,
389 toward Odysseus’ servants, and most of all you are cruel to me. Yet
390 I do not care, while circumspect Penelope still lives
391 here in her halls together with godlike Telemachus living.”
392 Then wise-minded Telemachus gave him an answer directly:
393 “Silence; do not answer him now with a long chain of speeches.
394 Antinous always is accustomed to goad men badly,
395 using his bitter words, and he urges the others to join him.”
396 So he spoke, then addressed Antinous with the following winged words:
397 “Beautifully you care for me like a father, Antinous,
398 ordering me to drive this guest away from the household
399 using coercive speech! May no god bring that to fulfillment.
400 Take something, give it to him; I do not resent it but urge you.
401 Do not refrain from fear of my mother, nor any other
402 servant who works in the house of Odysseus, godlike in counsel.
403 Still, there is no thought of this kind held in your bosom;
404 much you prefer to eat things yourself rather than give them to others.”
405 Then Antinous gave Telemachus an answer directly:
406 “Lofty in speech, Telemachus, wild in your strength—what words you have spoken!
407 If all the suitors were each to extend him as much as I shall,
408 then this house would hold him away for the space of three whole months.”
409 So he spoke, and pulled out the footstool lying under his table,
410 one where he rested his glistening feet as he banqueted.
411 All of the others gave and filled up the bag with provisions,
412 bread and meat; and soon Odysseus once again would be going
413 back to the threshold to taste without payment Achaean gifts there.
414 But he stood beside Antinous and spoke to him, saying:
415 “Give, friend; you do not seem the worst of all the Achaeans,
416 rather the best, for you have the look of a king among people.
417 Therefore you ought to give me a better portion of bread than
418 others; and I shall spread your fame through the limitless country.
419 I too once had a house of my own among mortal people,
420 blessed and rich, and often I gave to a wanderer freely,
421 all that he needed, whoever he was and however he came there.
422 Numberless slaves I had and many other possessions,
423 all of the things that make men live well and be called the wealthy.
424 But Zeus son of Cronus despoiled me, perhaps since he willed it:
425 he sent me with seaborne raiders who wandered over the waters,
426 bound for Egypt, a long voyage, meaning that I should perish.
427 There in the Egyptian river I moored all my balanced vessels.
428 There I commanded my trusted companions to heed me and follow:
429 stay in that place by the ships and keep watch on the vessels;
430 then I commanded the lookouts to take up their posts on the hilltops.
431 Yielding instead to arrogance, trusting only their war strength,
432 swiftly they ravaged the beautiful fields of Egyptian people,
433 carrying off all the wives and the innocent children in bondage,
434 killing the men. Soon cries of alarm reached the city.
435 Hearing the shouting, they came with the first appearance of morning;
436 all of the plain filled up with footmen and horses in numbers,
437 bronze flashing brightly; and Zeus, who delights in the thunder,
438 cast a disastrous panic among my men, and not one man
439 dared stand facing the foe, for destruction stood everywhere round them.
440 Many of us they killed on the spot with the pitiless bronze there;
441 others they carried alive to labor for them under compulsion.
442 But they gave me to a guest from Cyprus who came and encountered them,
443 Dmetor the son of Iasus, who strongly ruled over Cyprus.
444 From there now I have come to this place, enduring these sorrows.”
445 Then Antinous spoke and answered Odysseus loudly:
446 “What spirit led this plague here, this affliction upon our banquet?
447 Stand there, just as you are, in the midst and away from my table,
448 lest you discover a bitter Egypt and Cyprus again soon!
449 What a bold and shameless beggar you are in your begging,
450 standing beside every man in succession; they give to you freely,
451 rashly, since no restraint or pity can keep anyone from
452 giving another man’s goods, for each man has plenty beside him.”
453 Drawing away, the resourceful lord Odysseus answered:
454 “Shame! You have not, it appears, any mind to match your appearance.
455 Out of your own house you would not give even salt to your suppliant,
456 since, now seated at someone else’s abundance, you could not
457 bear to give me a morsel of bread, though much lies beside you.”
458 So he spoke. Then Antinous felt in his heart greater anger,
459 looked at the man from beneath his brows, and spoke to him winged words:
460 “Now I no longer imagine that you will make your departure
461 safely back through the hall, since you also speak these insults.”
462 So he spoke, seized the footstool and struck the man’s right shoulder,
463 down at the base of the back; but he stood like a rock there,
464 firmly, and Antinous’ missile did not dislodge him.
465 Silently he shook his head and was brooding on evils.
466 Going back to the threshold, he sat there again and lowered his satchel,
467 filled to the brim, onto ground, then spoke to the suitors before him:
468 “Listen to me, you suitors of our illustrious queen here,
469 so I may tell you what the heart in my breast now bids me.
470 Surely there is no pain in the mind and no cause for lamenting
471 when a man fighting hard for his own possessions is struck there,
472 whether in war for his cattle or for his white-fleeced sheep there.
473 Antinous struck me, however, because of my miserable belly,
474 cursed thing that gives to mortal men so many afflictions.
475 But if there are gods and avenging Furies for beggars,
476 let the completion of death find Antinous prior to marriage.”
477 Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke to him, saying:
478 “Eat in peace, old stranger, remaining seated, or go elsewhere,
479 lest young men drag you all through the halls for your speaking,
480 taking a hand or foot and completely stripping your body.”
481 So he spoke; but all of the suitors were fiercely indignant.
482 Thus one spoke from among the young men with arrogant spirits:
483 “Antinous, you did not do well to strike the unfortunate wanderer,
484 doomed man, if indeed he is some god from the heavens.
485 Often gods who resemble strangers from faraway countries,
486 taking on every form, make rounds of cities and nations,
487 watching the violent arrogance and lawful ways of the people.”
488 So the suitors spoke, but Antinous paid no heed to their speeches.
489 Deep in his heart Telemachus nourished a terrible sorrow
490 over the blow, yet he let no tear fall down from his eyelids.
491 Silently he shook his head and was brooding on evils.
492 When circumspect Penelope heard what had happened to him,
493 that he was struck in the hall, she spoke among all of her servant women:
494 “Would that Apollo, renowned for his bow, strike you in the same way!”
495 Then the housekeeper Eurynome gave her an answer directly:
496 “Would that fulfillment were granted the prayers we have spoken!
497 Not one man among these would arrive at Dawn on her fair throne.”
498 Circumspect Penelope gave her an answer directly:
499 “Nurse, they all are hateful because they are planning their evils,
500 but Antinous most resembles the black doom of dying.
501 Some unfortunate stranger is wandering through the household,
502 begging from all of the men, for poverty drives and commands him.
503 There all the other men filled his bag and gave him provisions,
504 but Antinous struck his right shoulder-blade with a footstool.”
505 Thus among all of the servant women she spoke these words there,
506 seated within her chamber; and godlike Odysseus ate dinner.
507 Then she summoned the noble swineherd to come and addressed him:
508 “Go, noble Eumaeus; command that the stranger should come here,
509 so I may welcome him kindly and ask him directly whether
510 he has heard any tidings of much-enduring Odysseus,
511 or has beheld him himself, for he looks like a widely traveled man.”
512 Then in reply, swineherd Eumaeus, you spoke and addressed her:
513 “Would, my queen, that the Achaeans would only fall silent!
514 Such are the tales he tells, and they would charm the heart in your bosom.
515 Three whole nights I kept him with me, and three days restrained him,
516 there in my hut, for he first came to me when he fled from a vessel;
517 yet he had not made an end of the evils he told of his lifetime.
518 Just as a man looks toward a singer who, from the gods’ teaching,
519 knows how to sing words full of desire and delight for all mortals,
520 and they desire to hear without end whenever he sings there,
521 so did he charm me, sitting beside me inside of the farmstead.
522 He says he is an ancestral guest-friend of lord Odysseus,
523 living in Crete, in the country where Minos’ descendants have houses.
524 From there now he has come to this place, enduring these sorrows,
525 rolling from trouble to trouble. He claims he has heard of Odysseus
526 close by, among the rich-soiled land of Thesprotian people,
527 living and bearing a wealth of treasure to bring to his own home and household.”
528 Circumspect Penelope gave him an answer directly:
529 “Go now, summon him here, so he may tell me directly.
530 Let these men be seated outside by the doors and make merry,
531 or here within the halls, since the hearts in their bodies are cheerful.
532 Their own possessions lie at home completely uninjured,
533 stores of bread and sweet wine, and their servants consume them;
534 yet they frequent our halls on every day as they come here,
535 slaughtering cattle and sheep and exceedingly fat goats,
536 holding their banquets and drinking our bright wine without limit,
537 rashly; much is consumed, for there is no man here among us
538 such as Odysseus was, able to ward this curse from the household.
539 If Odysseus came and returned to the land of his fathers,
540 soon with the help of his son he would punish the violence of these men.”
541 So she spoke. Telemachus gave a tremendous sneeze, and the household
542 rang terribly all around; Penelope laughed then,
543 quickly addressing Eumaeus aloud with the following winged words:
544 “Go now, summon the stranger to come here and stand in my presence.
545 Do you not see that my son has sneezed at every word I have spoken?
546 Therefore death will not fail to be brought to complete fulfillment
547 for all the suitors, and none will escape death’s doom and destruction.
548 I shall tell you another thing; keep it deep in your thinking:
549 if I discover he tells me the whole truth, honest in all things,
550 I shall dress him in tunic and mantle, beautiful garments.”
551 So she spoke, and the swineherd went when he heard the commandment.
552 Then he drew close to the stranger and spoke the following winged words:
553 “Stranger and father, discerning Penelope calls for you now here,
554 mother of lord Telemachus; something urges her spirit
555 into inquiring about her husband, although she has suffered.
556 If she discovers you tell her the whole truth, honest in all things,
557 then she will dress you in tunic and mantle, which are the garments
558 most in your need; as for bread, by begging throughout the country
559 you can supply your stomach, and he who desires will give you.”
560 Then much-enduring, godlike Odysseus answered Eumaeus:
561 “Quickly, Eumaeus, I would report every truth of the matter
562 all to Icarius’ daughter, discerning Penelope herself.
563 Well do I know of the man, and we have borne common affliction.
564 But I am fearful of this harsh crowd of the suitors,
565 whose insolence and violence rise to the iron heavens.
566 Even now, when this man encountered me here in the household,
567 struck me though I had done him no harm and gave me pain with his striking,
568 neither Telemachus nor anyone else came to help me.
569 Therefore tell Penelope now in the halls she must wait there,
570 though she is eager, until the sun has descended.
571 Then let her ask me concerning the homecoming day of her husband,
572 seated close to the fire; for the clothes I possess are—remember—
573 wretched; you know for yourself, since you were the first I entreated.”
574 So he spoke, and the swineherd went when he heard the commandment.
575 As he crossed the threshold Penelope spoke and addressed him:
576 “You do not bring him, Eumaeus. What has the wanderer thought there?
577 Is it perhaps that he fears some outrage beyond all measure,
578 or is ashamed in the house? It is bad for a wanderer to be shamefaced.”
579 Then in reply, swineherd Eumaeus, you spoke and addressed her:
580 “Rightly he speaks, as anyone else who was thoughtful would reckon,
581 trying to flee the insolence of these overbearing people.
582 He has commanded you wait until the sun is descended.
583 That way, queen, it will be much better for you yourself here,
584 speaking your word to the stranger alone and hearing his answer.”
585 Circumspect Penelope gave him an answer directly:
586 “Not without sense is the stranger; he thinks of the way these men may act here,
587 since perhaps nowhere else among mortal human people
588 do such insolent men devise such reckless offenses.”
589 Thus she spoke; but the noble swineherd departed and went on,
590 into the gathering of suitors, when he had clearly reported the matter.
591 Swiftly he spoke to Telemachus then with the following winged words,
592 holding his head close by, so the other men would not hear him:
593 “Friend, I am leaving to guard the swine and all of the farmstead,
594 your livelihood and mine; let everything here be your concern.
595 Save your own self first, and carefully think in your spirit,
596 lest you should suffer; many Achaeans are plotting your ruin.
597 May Zeus utterly destroy them before they become our affliction.”
598 Then wise-minded Telemachus gave him an answer directly:
599 “So shall it be, father. Go when you finish your afternoon dinner;
600 then in the morning come here and bring beautiful victims for offering.
601 All these matters shall be my concern and the deathless gods’ with me.”
602 So he spoke. The swineherd sat once again on the finely wrought footstool,
603 and when he filled his heart with the food and the drink of the banquet,
604 off he departed to tend the swine, leaving court and the great hall,
605 full of the banquet guests; and they took delight in the dancing
606 and in the singing, for now the afternoon came on among them.
