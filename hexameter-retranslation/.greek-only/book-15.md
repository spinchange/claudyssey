# The Odyssey — Book XV

*Telemachus returns to Ithaca; the stranger at the swineherd’s farm.*

1 Then to broad-fielded Lacedaemon journeyed Pallas Athena,
2 on her way toward the glorious son of great-hearted Odysseus,
3 think of his homeward return and to urge him onward to voyage.
4 There she discovered Telemachus, there with the splendid son of
5 Nestor asleep in the forecourt of glorious lord Menelaus.
6 Nestor’s son lay wholly subdued by the softness of slumber;
7 sweet sleep, though, had not taken Telemachus; deep in his spirit,
8 cares for his father kept him awake through the deathless nighttime.
9 Standing beside him, the bright-eyed goddess Athena addressed him:
10 “Telemachus, it is no longer right to be roaming far from
11 home, while leaving your wealth behind and such men in your household,
12 swollen with arrogant pride; beware they devour your possessions,
13 sharing them all, while the journey you make has yielded you nothing.
14 Come then, exhort Menelaus at once, the lord of the war cry,
15 swiftly to send you, that you may still find your blameless mother.
16 Even now both her father and brothers are pressing her onward,
17 urging a marriage to Eurymachus; he outstrips all the suitors,
18 lavishing presents and steadily raising his promised bride-payment.
19 Watch that she carry no treasure away from the house against you.
20 Well you know what manner of spirit a woman carries:
21 she wants to swell the estate of the man who marries and takes her;
22 then for the children she bore before, and the husband who loved her,
23 once he has died, she has no remembrance and makes no inquiry.
24 Go there yourself, then, and place all your goods in the keeping
25 of whichever woman among your maids appears to be best there,
26 till such a time as the gods reveal your illustrious consort.
27 One more warning I give you now; take heed in your spirit.
28 Leaders and best of the suitors are lying in ambush to catch you,
29 there in the strait between Ithaca’s shore and the crags of Samos,
30 eager to kill you before you arrive at the land of your fathers.
31 Yet I think it will not be so: the earth will enfold one
32 sooner, at least, of the suitors who now are devouring your substance.
33 Keep your well-fashioned ship at a distance away from the islands;
34 sail through the night no less; a fair wind behind you will be sent
35 by whichever immortal is guarding and keeping you safely.
36 Then, when you reach the first projecting coastland of Ithaca,
37 urge the vessel and all your companions to go to the city;
38 you yourself must first make your way to the keeper of swine there,
39 guardian over your pigs and a man whose heart remains kindly.
40 Spend the night in his hut, then urge him to go to the city,
41 carrying word to thoughtful Penelope, telling the woman
42 you are safe and have come out of Pylos to Ithaca once more.”
43 Having spoken so, she departed for towering Olympus.
44 Telemachus roused Nestor’s son from the sweetness of slumber,
45 prodding his foot with a kick, and then these words he addressed him:
46 “Rise up, Peisistratus, Nestor’s son; lead out the single-hoofed horses,
47 yoke them under the chariot, so we may finish our journey.”
48 Then Peisistratus, Nestor’s son, in answer addressed him:
49 “Telemachus, although we are eager to travel, no one can drive through
50 night when the darkness is heavy; and very soon Dawn will be with us.
51 Wait till the gifts have been brought and set on the chariot for us
52 by Atreus’ heroic son, spear-renowned Menelaus;
53 let him address you with gentle words and dispatch you in honor.
54 All of his days a guest will remember the host who received him,
55 when that host has offered the wanderer friendship and kindness.”
56 So he spoke; and at once gold-throned Dawn came over the country.
57 Menelaus, lord of the war cry, drew near them shortly,
58 risen from bed and the side of Helen with beautiful tresses.
59 Soon as the well-loved son of Odysseus noticed him coming,
60 urgently then he slipped his glittering tunic around him,
61 and cast a broad cloak over the strength of his shoulders;
62 then the hero strode through the door, stood close, and addressed him,
63 Telemachus did, the dear son of Odysseus, godlike in counsel:
64 “Zeus-born son of Atreus, Menelaus, marshal of peoples,
65 send me away even now to the dear native land of my fathers;
66 for in my spirit already I long to return to my household.”
67 Menelaus, lord of the war cry, answered him, saying:
68 “Telemachus, I will not hold you here for a long time,
69 eager for home as you are; I resent any host who goes too far,
70 one who loves a guest far too much or hates him excessively;
71 measure is better in all things, always observing what is fitting.
72 Equal wrong is committed by one who urges departure
73 on an unwilling guest and by one who restrains him when eager.
74 Welcome a guest while he stays, but send him away when he wishes.
75 Wait until I have brought out beautiful gifts for the chariot;
76 see them yourself with your eyes, while I bid the women prepare you
77 breakfast here in the hall from the ample supplies that we keep here.
78 Fame and splendor together, and profit as well, will be ours here
79 when you have eaten before you go over vast tracts of country.
80 If you desire to range all over Hellas and central Argos,
81 I will accompany you myself and yoke up the horses beneath you,
82 leading you into the cities of men; there is nobody in them
83 who will dismiss us with nothing, but each will give us one treasure,
84 whether a tripod of finely worked bronze or perhaps some cauldron,
85 whether two mules for the road or a goblet fashioned of goldwork.”
86 Telemachus, full of his good sense, answered him, saying:
87 “Zeus-born son of Atreus, Menelaus, marshal of peoples,
88 I want now to return to my home; for when I departed,
89 I left no guardian behind to keep watch over all my possessions.
90 May I not perish myself in the search for my godlike father,
91 or may some excellent heirloom vanish away from my palace.”
92 When Menelaus, lord of the war cry, heard him say this,
93 straightaway he ordered his wife and the serving women beside her
94 there in the house to prepare them a meal from their plentiful stores there.
95 Eteoneus, Boethous’ son, had come near him shortly,
96 risen from bed, for his home stood there, not far from the palace.
97 Menelaus, lord of the war cry, ordered him kindle a fire there
98 and roast meat for the meal; on hearing, the man obeyed him.
99 Down to the chamber fragrant with cedar the king then descended,
100 not alone: Megapenthes accompanied him there, and Helen.
101 Then, when they reached the room where the treasures were laid up in storage,
102 Atreus’ son took up a cup there, double-handled;
103 then he commanded his son Megapenthes to carry the mixing bowl,
104 made all of silver; Helen stood near to the storage chests holding
105 richly embroidered robes which her own hands once had fashioned.
106 Helen, the brilliant among women, lifted and carried
107 one of these robes, the broadest of all and the finest in needlework;
108 bright as a star it shone, and beneath all the others was lying.
109 Then they went forward again through the house until they had reached
110 Telemachus; and the golden-haired Menelaus addressed him:
111 “Telemachus, as your heart’s own purpose desires your homecoming,
112 so may loud-thundering Zeus, lord of Hera, bring it to fullness.
113 Out of the heirlooms laid away as treasures here in my household,
114 I will give you the finest and most esteemed in its value.
115 I will present you a mixing bowl, exquisitely fashioned,
116 silver from bottom to top, though the lips are finished with goldwork;
117 Hephaestus made it, and Phaedimus, hero and ruler
118 over the Sidonians, gave it when I was lodged in his palace,
119 there on my homeward road; and this I would grant you as keepsake.”
120 So he spoke, and placed the double-handled cup in his hands there.
121 So did heroic Atreides; then the glittering mixing bowl
122 powerful Megapenthes carried and set there before him,
123 all silver; Helen with beautiful cheeks stood close, holding the robe in
124 both hands; then she spoke to him, calling the young man by name there:
125 “I too give you this gift, dear child, to keep as a treasure,
126 made by the hands of Helen, a token for your delightful
127 marriage day, for your bride to wear; till then let your dear mother
128 guard it there in her hall. Go happily now, and may you
129 reach your well-founded house and the dear native land of your fathers.”
130 So she spoke and placed it in both of his hands, and he took it gladly.
131 Hero Peisistratus took all the gifts and arranged them
132 there in the wagon box, admiring each in his spirit.
133 Golden-haired Menelaus conducted the young men inside then;
134 there they sat themselves down upon chairs and high-backed seats there.
135 Water was poured for their hands by a maid who carried the pitcher,
136 beautiful, golden, and held it over a basin of silver;
137 near them she drew up a polished table, ready for dining.
138 Bread was brought by the dignified housekeeper, setting it near them,
139 adding a wealth of provisions, generous out of her stores there.
140 Near her, Boethous’ son was carving and dealing out portions;
141 wine was poured by the son of renowned Menelaus among them.
142 They reached out their hands to the good things ready before them.
143 Then, when they had put away their desire for eating and drinking,
144 Telemachus and the splendid son of Nestor together
145 yoked up the horses and mounted the richly decorated wagon;
146 out from the echoing porch and the outer gateway they drove them.
147 Golden-haired Menelaus, the son of Atreus, followed,
148 holding some honey-sweet wine in the palm of his right hand,
149 there in a golden cup, so the youths could pour and go onward.
150 Standing before the horses, he toasted them and addressed them:
151 “Farewell, youths, and remember me to Nestor, shepherd of peoples,
152 since he was gentle to me like a father throughout all the time when
153 we, the sons of Achaeans, were waging our warfare in Troy-land.”
154 Telemachus, full of his good sense, answered him, saying:
155 “Certainly, Zeus-born king, just as you tell us to do here,
156 we will relate everything when we reach him. If only I likewise,
157 coming again to Ithaca, found Odysseus at home there,
158 so I could tell him how I received every kindness beside you
159 and have come home, bringing treasures abundant and good in their value.”
160 Even as he was speaking, a bird on the right wing flew over:
161 it was an eagle carrying off in its talons a massive
162 tame white goose from a courtyard; with shouting they followed behind it,
163 women and men; but the eagle came close to them all, and then,
164 rightward, darted before the horses. They saw it together,
165 rejoiced, and every spirit within them was warmed with delight there.
166 Nestor’s son Peisistratus opened his speech to the others:
167 “Think now, Zeus-born lord Menelaus, marshal of peoples:
168 did the god show this portent to us, or to you as the host here?”
169 So he spoke, and Menelaus, beloved of Ares, considered
170 how he should understand it and answer in proper proportion.
171 Long-robed Helen forestalled him, however, and spoke out before him:
172 “Listen to me, and I will prophesy just as the deathless
173 gods put it into my spirit and as I believe it will happen.
174 Just as this eagle has seized the goose that was reared in the household,
175 coming from out of the mountain, the place of his birth and his breeding,
176 so will Odysseus, after much suffering and wandering widely,
177 come to his home and exact his revenge; or perhaps he already
178 waits in his house and is planting disaster for all of the suitors.”
179 Telemachus, full of his good sense, answered her, saying:
180 “So may loud-thundering Zeus ordain it, the consort of Hera;
181 then, even there in my home, I would pray to you as to a goddess.”
182 So he spoke, and he laid his whip on the horses; they quickly
183 darted away through the town, sped on by their zeal for the plainland.
184 All of the day they shook and rattled the yoke they were bearing.
185 Now the sun went down, and the roads grew darkened with shadow;
186 into Pherae they came, to the house belonging to Diocles,
187 son of Ortilochus, whom the river Alpheus fathered.
188 There they spent the night, and he set out a stranger’s provisions.
189 When early Dawn had appeared with her beautiful rose-colored fingers,
190 then they harnessed the horses and mounted the richly decorated wagon;
191 out from the echoing porch and the outer gateway they drove them.
192 Peisistratus whipped them to run, and willingly both horses flew on.
193 Soon they arrived at the steep-built citadel standing in Pylos.
194 Telemachus then addressed his companion, the son of old Nestor:
195 “Nestor’s son, how can you promise to do what I ask you,
196 for we proclaim ourselves guest-friends in unbroken tradition,
197 thanks to our fathers’ affection, and both of us equal in age too?
198 Surely this journey will make us still more united in friendship.
199 Zeus-born prince, do not drive past my ship, but leave me beside her,
200 lest the old king detain me against my desire in his household,
201 eager to give me his welcome; but I must reach home swiftly.”
202 So he spoke, and the son of old Nestor pondered in spirit
203 how he could promise and bring this proper request to fulfillment.
204 Thinking it over, he judged that this was the better decision:
205 turning the horses toward the swift ship and the shore of the salt sea,
206 then he unloaded the beautiful gifts in the stern of the vessel,
207 gold and the clothing that lord Menelaus had given to Telemachus.
208 Urging the prince, he spoke these winged words and addressed him:
209 “Hurry aboard now and order all of your comrades to join you,
210 before I can get to the house and tell the old king what has happened.
211 This I know very well in the heart and the soul within me:
212 such is the overpowering will of the king, he will not release you,
213 but he himself will come here to call you, and surely he will not
214 journey away empty-handed; for even so he will be furious.”
215 So he spoke, and drove his horses with beautiful manes back
216 into the Pylians’ city, and quickly arrived at the palace.
217 Telemachus roused his companions at once and commanded:
218 “Set all the gear in order, companions, aboard the black vessel;
219 let us ourselves go aboard, so we may finish our journey.”
220 So he spoke, and eagerly all of the company heard and obeyed him.
221 Quickly they went aboard and sat in their places for rowing.
222 While he attended to these things, he prayed and sacrificed to Athena;
223 close by the stern of the ship, a stranger approached him from nearby,
224 far from his country, a fugitive out of Argos for killing a man,
225 one who could prophesy, born in the line descending from Melampus.
226 Long before, Melampus had lived in Pylos, the mother of sheep flocks,
227 rich among all Pylians, dwelling in high-roofed halls there.
228 Then he went to the country of others, a fugitive from homeland,
229 fleeing great-hearted Neleus, proudest among all the living,
230 who had retained great stores of Melampus’ wealth for an entire
231 year by force, while he in the house of Phylacus suffered,
232 held in oppressive bonds and enduring terrible anguish,
233 all for the daughter of Neleus and the dreadful delusion
234 put in his mind by the savage goddess of vengeance, Erinys.
235 Yet he escaped his death and drove the deep-bellowing cattle
236 out of Phylace to Pylos, and punished the shameful transgression
237 wrought by godlike Neleus, then brought the bride to his brother’s
238 household. He himself reached the land of another people,
239 Argos, the horse-grazed country; for there it had been ordained that
240 he should live and command the numerous Argive people.
241 There he married a wife and established a high-roofed household,
242 fathering Antiphates and Mantius, two mighty princes.
243 Antiphates, then, was the father of great-hearted Oicles,
244 Oicles in turn of Amphiaraus, marshaller of armies,
245 one whom Zeus of the aegis and lord Apollo entirely
246 loved in their hearts with every affection; he came not to old age,
247 dying in Thebes for the gifts that his wife had accepted.
248 Two were the sons that he fathered, Alcmaeon and Amphilochus.
249 Mantius fathered Cleitus together with Polypheides;
250 Cleitus was carried away by Dawn of the golden throne, for
251 such was his beauty, that he might live with immortal divinities.
252 As for bold Polypheides the seer, it was lord Apollo
253 who made him best of the prophets among men after Amphiaraus died.
254 Angered with his father, he moved far away to Hyperesia;
255 living there, he prophesied for all who came to consult him.
256 This was his son who approached, Theoclymenus being his name there;
257 he now stood beside Telemachus, whom he discovered
258 pouring libations and praying beside his swift black vessel.
259 Calling to him, he spoke these winged words and addressed him:
260 “Friend, since I find you offering sacrifice here in this station,
261 I implore you by your sacrifice and god, and moreover
262 by your own head and the heads of the men who accompany you here,
263 tell me the truth when I question you now, and conceal nothing from me:
264 who are you, out of what people; where are your city and parents?”
265 Thoughtful Telemachus answered in turn and addressed him:
266 “Then I shall tell you, stranger, with perfect exactness and plainness.
267 I am by birth out of Ithaca; my father is lord Odysseus,
268 if he once lived; by this time he has met with a pitiful death, though.
269 Therefore now I have taken companions and this black vessel,
270 coming to seek word of my father, so long gone away from his homeland.”
271 Godlike Theoclymenus answered him then, and addressed him:
272 “So I too have come out of my country, after I killed one
273 born of my clan; and many his brothers and kinsmen are living
274 throughout horse-grazing Argos, with mighty power among the Achaeans.
275 Fleeing from them, from death and the black doom following after,
276 now I am wandering, since such is my lot among humankind always.
277 Take me aboard, for in flight I come to you as a suppliant;
278 lest they should kill me; for I think they are hunting me down now.”
279 Telemachus, full of good judgment, replied to the stranger:
280 “Surely I will not turn you away from the balanced vessel,
281 if you desire to follow; be welcomed with all we possess there.”
282 So he spoke and accepted his bronze-pointed spear from the stranger,
283 laying it lengthwise over the deck of the seagoing vessel;
284 then he himself went aboard the vessel that travels the seaway.
285 There at the stern he seated himself, and sitting beside him
286 placed Theoclymenus; meanwhile the men cast loose the stern cables.
287 Telemachus called his companions to work and commanded
288 all to lay hands upon tackle; eagerly they obeyed him.
289 They raised up the firwood mast, and inside the hollow mast-box
290 stood it upright, then tied it fast with the ropes at the forestay;
291 then with the strongly twisted oxhide lines drew up the white canvas.
292 Bright-eyed Pallas Athena sent them a favoring stern wind,
293 strongly and stormily gusting through upper air, so that swiftly
294 running, the ship might finish her course across water of salt sea.
295 They sailed past the streams of Crouni and Chalcis with beautiful waters.
296 Now the sun went down, and the roads grew darkened with shadow;
297 sped by the wind out of Zeus, the vessel was reaching the Pheae,
298 passing beside divine Elis, where the Epeians are rulers.
299 On from that place toward the sharp-pointed islands he steered her,
300 pondering whether he would escape death or be captured.
301 Meanwhile, there in the hut, Odysseus and the noble swineherd
302 ate their supper, and near them the other men also were eating.
303 Then, when they had put away their desire for eating and drinking,
304 Odysseus spoke among them, testing the heart of the swineherd,
305 whether he still would welcome him warmly and ask him to linger
306 there at the farm, or now would urge him away to the city:
307 “Listen to me, Eumaeus, and all you other companions:
308 early tomorrow I long to return and go into the city,
309 begging, so I may not wear down you and your comrades by staying.
310 Give me some good advice and an able guide to attend me,
311 one who can lead me there; but through the city necessity
312 will make me wander, in case someone gives me a cupful and bread there.
313 or a crust. I could go to the halls of godlike Odysseus,
314 giving the wise Penelope news of the man’s situation,
315 even could mix with the insolent suitors who gather about her,
316 if they would give me a meal from the countless supplies they possess there.
317 Quickly and ably I would perform any task they required.
318 For I will tell you now; take heed to my words and attend me:
319 through the favor of messenger Hermes, the god who on all men
320 bestows grace and glory in every work that their hands undertake here,
321 nobody else among mortals could rival my skill as a servant:
322 stacking a fire well, splitting the lengths of the dry wood,
323 carving and roasting the meat and pouring the wine for the banquets,
324 such are the tasks that inferiors render to nobler companions.”
325 Deeply distressed, Eumaeus the swineherd, you answered Odysseus:
326 “Ah me, stranger, why did this purpose arise in your spirit?
327 Surely you utterly long to be killed right there in the palace,
328 if you intend to go in among those suitors assembled!
329 Their violent force and arrogant pride reach up to the iron heavens.
330 Servants of theirs are no wandering people such as you are,
331 but they are young and dressed very well in their mantles and tunics,
332 always sleek are their heads, and their beautiful faces are shining;
333 such are the people who serve them, while finely polished tables
334 are burdened with bread and meat and wine in abundance.
335 Stay here: nobody grieves that you are among us as guest here,
336 neither myself nor any companion who stays at the farmstead.
337 Then, when the well-loved son of Odysseus comes to the household,
338 he will clothe you himself in a mantle and tunic as raiment,
339 sending you where your heart and the spirit within you command you.”
340 Long-enduring, godlike Odysseus answered him, saying:
341 “Would you were dear to Zeus our father, Eumaeus, as you are
342 dear now to me, because you have ended my grief and my grim wandering.
343 Nothing that falls upon mortals is worse than the life of a vagrant;
344 yet for the sake of the ruinous belly men suffer the evil
345 anguish, when wandering, disaster, and pain have come to assail them.
346 Since you now restrain me, commanding me here to await him,
347 come then, tell me the fate of the mother of godlike Odysseus,
348 and of his father, whom, when he departed, he left on the threshold
349 of old age. Are both of them still alive underneath the sunlight,
350 sunlight, or have they died and entered the household of Hades?”
351 Then the swineherd, the leader of men, in answer addressed him:
352 “Stranger, I will relate it exactly and hide nothing from you.
353 Still Laertes is living, but always he prays to lord Zeus that
354 life may waste from the limbs of his body there in his household,
355 since he grieves beyond measure for his son who has gone from the country,
356 and for the wife of his youth, that wise woman who above all
357 grieved him by dying and brought him into the harshness of old age.
358 She, in her sorrow for her renowned son, wasted and perished,
359 meeting a wretched death—may no one dear to me ever
360 die so, any friend among people who live here and treat me with kindness.
361 While that woman was living, although she was always in sorrow,
362 it was a pleasure to ask her questions and hear her responses,
363 since she herself had reared me beside long-robed Ctimene,
364 her own stalwart daughter, the youngest child she had borne there.
365 I was brought up together with her; she honored me only a little less.
366 But after the two of us both reached the lovely prime of our young age,
367 then they gave her in marriage in Same and took countless presents.
368 Then that woman clothed me herself in a mantle and tunic,
369 splendid in every respect, and gave sandals under my feet there,
370 sending me out to the farm; but she loved me more in her heart then.
371 Now I am lacking these things; but still in the work I continue,
372 blessed gods make me prosper and cause what I labor at to flourish.
373 Out of its fruits I have eaten and drunk and given to honored people.
374 Now from my mistress there is nothing gentle for one to hear there,
375 neither a word nor a deed, since evil fell on the household—
376 arrogant men; and the servants greatly yearn to be near her,
377 speaking and asking about every thing that has happened around her,
378 eating and drinking with her, and afterward carrying something
379 out to the field, such gifts as always warm up the heart of a servant.”
380 Resourceful lord Odysseus answered Eumaeus, saying:
381 “Ah, how little you were at the time, Eumaeus the swineherd,
382 when you were forced to wander so far from your country and parents.
383 Come then, tell me of this, and relate it with perfect precision:
384 was the broad-streeted city of men ever stormed and demolished,
385 where both your father and lady mother had lived in their household?
386 Or were you all alone beside sheep or cattle when hostile
387 men took you into their ship and sold you away from your homeland,
388 here at this man’s house, and he gave them a suitable purchase price?”
389 Then the swineherd, the leader of men, in answer addressed him:
390 “Stranger, now that you question me so and ask for the story,
391 listen in silence and take your delight while sitting and drinking.
392 These are nights beyond measure in length: there is time enough both to
393 sleep and to listen with pleasure; you have no need to lie down
394 earlier than the hour requires, since too much slumber is wearisome.
395 As for the others, whoever is urged by his heart and his spirit,
396 let him go out and sleep, then when early Dawn is appearing,
397 let him eat breakfast and go with the swine that belong to the master.
398 We two here in the hut, as we drink and take our meal together,
399 may take pleasure in each other’s grievous sorrows, recalling
400 all; for a man will delight even afterward in his afflictions,
401 one who has suffered abundantly and wandered over the wide world.
402 Now I will tell you the thing you are asking and question me over.
403 There is an island called Syria, if you have heard of it ever,
404 lying above Ortygia, there where the sun makes his turning;
405 not very populous, certainly, but it is fertile,
406 good land for cattle and sheep, rich in wine and abounding in wheat crops.
407 Famine never enters the country, nor does another
408 hateful disease ever fall on the wretched mortals who live there.
409 Rather, whenever the generations of men in the city grow older,
410 then the god with the silver bow, Apollo, together with Artemis,
411 goes among them and strikes them down with his gentle arrows.
412 There are two cities, and everything there is divided between them;
413 over them both together my father governed as sovereign,
414 Ctesius, son of Ormenus, a man like the deathless immortals.
415 Phoenician men who were famed for their ships came there to the island,
416 greedy deceivers, carrying countless trinkets aboard their black vessel.
417 There in the house of my father a Phoenician woman was living,
418 tall and attractive and skilled in the glorious work of her hands.
419 Her the Phoenicians, practiced in cunning, managed to beguile.
420 One of them first had intercourse with her beside their hollow
421 ship while she washed clothes; love and the bed can beguile the
422 mind of a woman, however industrious she in her work is.
423 Then he proceeded to ask who she was and where she had come from;
424 straightaway she told him about the high-roofed house of her father:
425 ‘Sidon abounding in bronze is the place where I boast I was born from;
426 I am a daughter of Arybas, rich beyond measure in treasure.
427 But the Taphian pirates seized me one day from the country,
428 coming in from the fields, and brought me and sold me in this land,
429 here at the house of this man, and he gave them a suitable purchase price.’
430 Then the man who had lain with her secretly answered the woman:
431 ‘Would you now come back with us to the home you remember,
432 so you could see the high-roofed house of your mother and father,
433 and see them themselves? They are living and said to be wealthy.’
434 Then in turn the woman responded and gave him an answer:
435 ‘Even this might be, if you sailors are willing to pledge me
436 under an oath that you will conduct me unharmed to the land of my fathers.’
437 So she spoke, and every man swore the oath she demanded.
438 Then, when they had sworn and brought the oath to fulfillment,
439 once again the woman spoke out to them and gave them an answer:
440 ‘Silence now: do not let any one of your menfolk
441 speak with me when they meet me by chance in the street of the city
442 or at the spring, lest somebody go to the house of my aged
443 master and tell him, and he grow suspicious and bind me
444 fast in oppressive bonds, devising destruction against you.
445 Hold this word in your minds, and hasten to purchase your road-stores.
446 Then, whenever the ship has been filled with the goods for your living,
447 let your message arrive very swiftly for me at the palace;
448 I will bring gold as well, whatever comes under my hand there;
449 gladly would also I give something else as my fare for the passage.
450 I am nursing the son of my master here in the palace,
451 clever, the sort who already runs round outside of the doorway.
452 I could bring him aboard your ship; he would fetch you a countless
453 price wherever you sell him among foreign-speaking peoples.’
454 So she spoke and went on her way to the beautiful palace.
455 They remained there among us, however, an entire year through,
456 buying their many supplies in the hold of the hollow vessel.
457 Then, when the hollow ship had been laden for them to depart there,
458 only at that time they sent a messenger back to the woman.
459 Came to the house of my father a man well practiced in cunning,
460 holding a necklace of gold that was strung with pieces of amber.
461 There in the hall my mother and all of her servants together
462 handled it in their hands and gazed on it closely with their eyes,
463 offering payment; in silence the man gave the woman a nod as his signal.
464 Once he had nodded to her, he went on his way to the hollow vessel;
465 holding my hand, the woman conducted me out from the palace.
466 There in the forecourt she found both cups and tables remaining
467 after the banqueting men who had been attending my father.
468 They had gone on to the meeting place and the people’s assembly.
469 Quickly she hid three deep goblets underneath her clothing,
470 carrying them; and I followed her out in the folly of childhood.
471 Now the sun sank downward, and every roadway grew shadowed;
472 we two hurried and came very swiftly to the illustrious harbor,
473 where the swift-sailing ship of the Phoenician men lay anchored.
474 Then they themselves went aboard and sailed on the watery pathways,
475 taking us also aboard; and Zeus sent a favoring wind there.
476 Six days equally onward we sailed through the daytime and nighttime;
477 then, when Cronus’ son brought the seventh day over the heavens,
478 Artemis, showerer of arrows, struck the Phoenician woman;
479 down in the bilge she fell with a crash like a bird of the sea there.
480 Then, as a meal for seals and fishes together, they threw her
481 over the side; but I remained, and my heart was afflicted.
482 Wind and the water conveyed the ship till they closed upon Ithaca,
483 where Laertes purchased me there with some of his possessions.
484 Such is the way that my two eyes first looked on this country.”
485 Zeus-born lord Odysseus answered Eumaeus, saying:
486 “Surely, Eumaeus, you stirred the spirit within me profoundly,
487 telling me all the sorrows of heart you suffered and carried.
488 Still, beside the evil, Zeus also placed something good there
489 for you: after much toil you arrived at the house of a gentle
490 man, who provided you kindly with plentiful food and with drink there,
491 so you are leading a good life now; as for me, I have wandered
492 over many cities of mortals and reached this country at last here.”
493 Thus did the two men talk over these things with one another.
494 Then they lay down to sleep, though not for long, only briefly:
495 very soon fair-throned Dawn came over the world. On the shoreline
496 Telemachus’ comrades quickly loosened the sails, then lowered
497 down the mast, and rowed the vessel ahead to her mooring.
498 Over the side they cast out anchors and fastened the stern ropes;
499 they themselves disembarked on the line where the sea washed the shingle,
500 making their breakfast ready and blending the fiery wine there.
501 After they put their desire for eating and drinking behind them,
502 Telemachus, full of his good sense, opened his speech to the others:
503 “You men now sail the black ship onward again to the city;
504 I will visit the fields in the country and all of the herdsmen;
505 then at evening come to the city when I have seen my possessions.
506 Then, in the morning, I will set out a reward for your journey,
507 one good banquet of meat together with sweet-tasting wine there.”
508 Godlike Theoclymenus answered him then, and addressed him:
509 “Dear child, where shall I go? Whose house should I now be approaching,
510 out of the men who hold command in the rugged country of Ithaca?
511 Shall I go straight to the house where you and your mother are living?”
512 Telemachus answered in turn, with his mind well balanced:
513 “Under other conditions I would bid you to come to our household;
514 there would be no lack of guest-gifts; but for you it is worse now,
515 since I will be absent, and even my mother will not see you.
516 For she appears in the house but seldom among the gathered suitors,
517 sitting apart from them all and weaving a web in her upper room.
518 Yet there is one other man I can show you, whom you may visit:
519 Eurymachus, the glorious son of thoughtful Polybus;
520 people in Ithaca look on that man now as they look on a god there.
521 He is much the best of the suitors and most of all is determined
522 both to marry my mother and hold the office of lord Odysseus.
523 Only Olympian Zeus, whose home is the radiant heaven,
524 knows whether an evil day ends them before they accomplish the marriage.”
525 Even as he was speaking, a bird on the right wing flew over,
526 one swift hawk, the messenger sent by Apollo; the bird in its talons
527 held and was plucking a dove, and scattered the feathers down earthward,
528 midway between the ship and the place where Telemachus stood there.
529 Then Theoclymenus called him apart from all of his comrades,
530 clasping him by the hand, and spoke these words as he named him:
531 “Telemachus, not without some god did this bird fly past on the right;
532 watching its swoop, I knew it at once for a sign and a portent.
533 There is no other family here with a greater right to the kingship
534 anywhere through the country of Ithaca; yours remains mighty forever.”
535 Telemachus, full of his good sense, spoke in response then:
536 “Stranger, would that this word might be brought to fulfillment.
537 Then you would quickly know of my friendship and many a present
538 given by me, so that everyone meeting you called you a blest man.”
539 So he spoke and addressed Peiraeus, a trusted companion:
540 “Son of Clytius, Peiraeus, you above all of the comrades
541 who accompanied me to Pylos pay heed to whatever I ask you;
542 take this stranger away to your house now, and welcome him warmly,
543 honoring him with attentive care until I have come there.”
544 Peiraeus, renowned for the war spear, answered him, saying:
545 “Telemachus, even if you remain for a long time here in the country,
546 I will look after the stranger, and he will lack no provision.”
547 So he spoke and went on the ship, commanding his comrades
548 each to embark and cast off the cables fastened at stern there.
549 Quickly they went aboard and sat in their places for rowing.
550 Telemachus bound his beautiful sandals under his two feet;
551 then he took up the powerful spear, with keen bronze sharpened,
552 from the ship’s deck; meanwhile the men cast loose the stern cables.
553 Pushing away, they sailed toward town, according to what young
554 Telemachus ordered, the well-loved son of godlike Odysseus.
555 Swiftly his feet bore him onward until he came to the courtyard;
556 courtyard where his countless swine were gathered together;
557 there the good swineherd slept among them, kind to his masters.
