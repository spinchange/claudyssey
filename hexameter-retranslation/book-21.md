# The Odyssey — Book 21

*The bow: pedigree, contest, recruitment, and the stringing.*

1 Into her mind the goddess set it, gray-eyed Pallas Athena,
2 in Icarius’ daughter, wise-minded Penelope’s spirit,
3 to set before the suitors the bow and the pale gray iron
4 there in Odysseus’ hall, for the trial and first of the bloodshed.
5 Up she went on the high stairway of her own house climbing,
6 and in her thick strong hand she took the curved key of the chamber,
7 lovely, of bronze, and upon it an ivory handle was fitted.
8 She made her way to the storeroom attended by serving-women,
9 far in the back, where there for her lord the treasures were lying,
10 bronze and gold, and iron wrought through labor in plenty.
11 There lay the bow with its backward bend, and the quiver for arrows,
12 arrow-receiving; and many grief-giving shafts were within it,
13 gifts that a guest-friend gave him in Lacedaemon upon meeting,
14 Iphitus son of Eurytus, like to the deathless immortals.
15 These two had met one another in Messene country,
16 in the house of warlike Ortilochus; there Odysseus
17 came on a matter of debt, which the whole folk owed unto him;
18 for the Messenian men had lifted from Ithaca sheep-flocks,
19 three hundred, with shepherds besides, in their many-benched vessels.
20 For their sake over a very long road came godlike Odysseus,
21 still but a boy; for his father had sent him, and elders beside him.
22 Iphitus meanwhile was searching for horses, the mares that were missing,
23 twelve brood-mares, and beneath them hard-working mule-colts were suckling.
24 These were the mares that later became for him death and destruction,
25 when indeed he came to the mighty-hearted son of great Zeus,
26 to Heracles, man well skilled in the knowledge of splendid achievements,
27 who killed him, though he was a guest within his own dwelling,
28 ruthless man; he respected not gods’ overseeing, nor table,
29 that which he set before him; and afterward murdered him also,
30 and he himself in his halls kept hold of the strong-hoofed horses.
31 Asking of these, he encountered Odysseus and gave him the bow,
32 which in former days great Eurytus bore; but he left it
33 to his son in his high-roofed house as he lay there dying.
34 And Odysseus gave him a sharp sword and a powerful spear-shaft,
35 first beginning a guest-friend bond of affection between them;
36 yet they had never known one another at table, because first
37 Zeus’ son had killed Iphitus son of Eurytus, godlike,
38 he who had given the bow. But noble Odysseus never
39 took it along when he went to the war on the black ships sailing;
40 rather it stayed in his hall as a keepsake of his dear guest-friend,
41 but on his own land always he carried and used it.
42 But when now that bright queen among women came to the chamber,
43 and stepped upon the oaken threshold, which once a craftsman,
44 planing it well with his skill, had straightened it true to the line-rule,
45 and in it fixed the doorposts, and fastened the shining doors on,
46 straightway then she quickly unloosed the strap from the handle,
47 thrust in the key, and she hammered back the bolts of the doorway,
48 aiming straight; and the doors gave bellow, like some stalled bull beast
49 feeding out in a meadow; so bellowed the beautiful panels,
50 struck by the key, and before her they flew wide open quickly.
51 Onto the lofty floorboards she stepped; and there stood the chests where
52 fragrant clothing was lying away in the boxes among them.
53 Thence she reached up and took the bow down from the peg it was hung on,
54 with its bright case itself, which lay all around it enclosing.
55 Sitting down on the spot, and laying it there on her knees dear,
56 she wept very shrilly aloud, and drew forth her lord’s bow from it.
57 But when at last she had had her fill of the tearful lamenting,
58 then she went toward the hall and the haughty suitors within it,
59 holding in hand the bow with its backward bend, and the quiver
60 arrow-receiving; and many grief-giving shafts were within it.
61 With her the serving-women bore also a chestful of contest-gear,
62 where much iron was stored and bronze, the prizes of that lord.
63 But when she came to the suitors, that bright queen among women,
64 there she stood by a pillar that held the close-fitted roofing,
65 holding before her cheeks the shining folds of her head-veil;
66 and a good faithful handmaid stood attending her on either side.
67 And at once among all the suitors she spoke out and addressed them:
68 “Hear me, you overweening suitors, who over this household
69 feed and drink your fill continually, all of your days long,
70 while its master is gone for so long; and you could find no other
71 pretext at all to put forth, no word of excuse whatsoever,
72 only desire to wed me and make me your own wife hereafter.
73 Come then, suitors, since now this contest is plainly before you.
74 For I will set out the great bow of godlike Odysseus.
75 Whoever among you most easily strings the bow in his fingers,
76 and shoots an arrow clean through the holes of all twelve axes,
77 him I will follow then, leaving behind me this household,
78 this my wedded home, so beautiful, rich in the means of existence;
79 this, I think, I shall yet remember even within dreams only.”
80 So she spoke, and commanded Eumaeus, the noble swineherd,
81 to set forth the bow for the suitors, and the gray iron beside it.
82 Weeping, Eumaeus received them and laid them down on the floor-boards;
83 elsewhere the cowherd wept when he looked on the bow of his master.
84 But Antinous scolded them harshly and spoke out and named them:
85 “Foolish field-hand servants, with thoughts for the day and no farther,
86 why do you let tears pour down, poor fools, and trouble the woman's
87 heart in her breast, when even without that her spirit is lying
88 deep in pain, since she lost her beloved husband forever?
89 No, sit down and feast in silence, or take yourselves outdoors
90 both of you, weep out there, but leave the bow where it lies here,
91 an ordeal for the suitors, hard to accomplish; for truly
92 I do not think this polished bow will be strung up so lightly.
93 For there is no man among all these men assembled before us
94 such as Odysseus was; I myself have seen him plainly,
95 yes, and remember him well, though then I was only a young child.”
96 So he spoke; but his heart in his chest was hoping within him
97 he would string the cord on the bow and shoot through the iron.
98 Yet he was destined himself to be first to taste of the arrow
99 from the hands of noble Odysseus, the man whom he slighted
100 there as he sat in his halls, while he urged on all his companions.
101 The sacred strength of Telemachus also spoke out among them:
102 “Oh for shame! Zeus the son of Cronos has made me witless.
103 My own dear mother tells me, though wise in her mind as she always is,
104 that she will go with another man, abandoning this household;
105 and I laugh, and delight in it, with my foolish heart in me.
106 Come then, suitors, since now this contest is set forth before you,
107 for such a woman there is not now in the whole Achaean
108 land, not in holy Pylos, nor in Argos, nor in Mycenae,
109 nor in Ithaca itself, nor the dark land over against it;
110 and you yourselves know this. Why should I praise my mother?
111 Come then, drag this out no more with evasions and pleadings,
112 nor hold back any longer from stringing the bow. Let us see it.
113 Yes, and I too would myself make trial and test of the weapon:
114 if I can string it, and send the shaft clean through the iron,
115 then my honored mother would not leave these halls to my grieving,
116 going away with another, while I was left here behind her,
117 I who at last could take up my father's fair contest-prizes.”
118 So he spoke, and flung from his shoulders his cloak of crimson;
119 springing upright, he put off the sharp sword from his shoulder.
120 First he set up the axes, after digging a trench out,
121 one long trench for them all, and he straightened them true to the line-rule,
122 and stamped the earth firm round about them. Wonder seized all men
123 who looked on, seeing how straight he had set them, though he had never
124 seen them before. Going up to the threshold, he tested the bow-stave.
125 Thrice he shook it, eager in spirit to draw it and bend it;
126 thrice his strength gave way, though still in his heart he was hoping
127 to string the cord on the bow and shoot through the iron.
128 And by the fourth pull now he would have strung it by force as he drew it,
129 but Odysseus signaled him no, and for all his desire restrained him.
130 Once again the sacred strength of Telemachus spoke among them:
131 “Ah me, surely I shall be a weakling and coward hereafter,
132 or else I am too young as yet, and still do not trust in
133 these hands of mine to defend myself from a man who assails me.
134 Come then, you who are mightier men in force than I am,
135 test the bow, and let us bring this contest through to completion.”
136 So having spoken, away from himself he laid the bow earthward,
137 leaning it down by the closely joined panels, polished and handsome;
138 and he set the swift arrow against the fair-curved door-hook beside it,
139 and back once more sat down on the chair from which he had risen.
140 And Antinous spoke up among them, the son of Eupeithes:
141 “Rise in due order, companions, each one from left hand to right hand,
142 starting there from the place where the wine-pourer begins his service.”
143 So he spoke, Antinous, and his word was pleasing to all of them.
144 First then Leodes rose up, the son of Oenops, among them,
145 who was their reader of rites, and he always sat by the mixing-bowl,
146 deepest in the recess; to him alone their reckless behavior
147 was hateful, and he was indignant with all of the suitors.
148 He then first took up in his hands the bow and the swift arrow.
149 Going then to the threshold he stood there and tested the bowstring,
150 but he could not string it; before that, his hands as he pulled it,
151 soft and uncallused, grew weary; and then to the suitors he said this:
152 “Friends, I cannot string it. Let somebody else come and take it.
153 Many great men will this bow bereave of spirit and life-breath;
154 since indeed it is far better to die than continue living
155 after we fail in the thing for whose sake we are gathered together
156 here every day, still waiting and hoping through all of the daylight.
157 Now some man among us may hope in his heart and be eager
158 to marry Penelope, spouse of Odysseus the hero.
159 But when he has made his trial of the bow and has seen it clearly,
160 let him go woo some other Achaean woman in fair robes.
161 let him seek her with bride-gifts and suing; and afterward she may
162 marry the man who offers the most and comes as her fortune.”
163 So he spoke, and he set his bow away from his own hands,
164 leaning it down by the well-planed boards that were fitted together,
165 and there he propped the swift arrow close by the beautiful door-hook,
166 and back once more sat down on the chair from which he had risen.
167 But Antinous scolded him harshly and spoke out and named him:
168 “Leodes, out past the fence of your teeth what a word has escaped you,
169 dreadful and hard to endure; I am angered to hear it spoken.
170 Is this bow truly to rob our best men of courage and life-breath
171 only because you yourself are unable to bend it and string it?
172 Surely your honored mother did not bear you to be such a man
173 as could become a drawer of bows and a shooter of arrows;
174 no, but the rest of the splendid suitors will string it quickly.”
175 So he spoke, and he gave command to Melanthius, goatherd of goats:
176 “Come now, Melantheus, kindle a fire in the hall for us,
177 set a great seat beside it, and spread a fleece on top of it,
178 and from the stores bring out a great round of the lard kept within them,
179 so that the young men may warm it and smear it all over with ointment,
180 and make trial of the bow and bring the contest to ending.”
181 So he spoke, and at once Melanthius kindled the tireless fire.
182 and carrying a great seat he set it down and laid fleece on it,
183 and from the stores he brought a great round of the lard kept within them.
184 With this the young men warmed it and made their trial; yet none could
185 string it at all, but they were far lacking in bodily vigor.
186 Still Antinous held back, and Eurymachus godlike beside him,
187 leaders of all the suitors, the finest of all in their manhood.
188 Out from the house those two went forth together, both of them,
189 cowherd and swineherd, servants of noble Odysseus;
190 and after these from the house came out godlike Odysseus himself.
191 But when they had come outside of the doors and outside the courtyard,
192 then he addressed them aloud and spoke with gentle words to them:
193 “Cowherd, and you as well, swineherd, what word shall I speak to you?
194 Shall I conceal it myself? Yet my heart bids me tell it.
195 What sort of men would you be to defend Odysseus
196 if he should suddenly come here somehow, and a god should conduct him?
197 Would you defend the suitors, or rather stand by Odysseus?
198 Speak, as your own heart within you and spirit command you.”
199 To him in answer spoke the master and herdsman of cattle:
200 “Father Zeus, if only you would fulfill this longing for me,
201 if that man might come, and some god might lead him here to us:
202 then you would know what my strength is, and how my hands follow.”
203 And in the same way Eumaeus was praying to all the immortals
204 that resourceful Odysseus might come back home to this house.
205 But when he had fully discerned the true mind in both of them,
206 then once again he answered and spoke with words to them:
207 “Here in the house I myself am that man; after many afflictions
208 I have returned in the twentieth year to my fatherland country.
209 I know that I come as the longed-for one to you two only
210 out of the slaves; of the rest I have never heard any among them
211 praying that I might return once more and come back to my household.
212 Yet to you two, exactly as all this will be, I will tell it.
213 If under my hand some god should subdue the splendid suitors,
214 I will provide for you both with wives and with goods and possessions,
215 and homes built close by mine; and afterward both of you shall be
216 comrades to me and brothers as well to Telemachus.
217 Come then, and let me show you another most evident token,
218 so that within your hearts you may know me and trust and believe me:
219 this scar, which once a boar struck into me with his white tusk
220 when I had gone to Parnassus with sons of Autolycus.”
221 So having spoken, he drew the rags back from the great scar,
222 And when the two had beheld it and clearly observed every detail,
223 then around wise Odysseus they wept and threw both arms about him,
224 and they kept kissing his head and his shoulders in loving welcome;
225 and in the same way Odysseus kissed their heads and their hands.
226 And now the light of the sun would have set on their lamentation,
227 had not Odysseus himself checked them and spoken and said this:
228 “Stop now from weeping and wailing, lest someone should see you and notice,
229 coming out from the hall, and then go tell those inside of it.
230 But go back in to the suitors one after another, not all together.
231 I will go first, and after me you; and let this be our token:
232 for all of the others, all of these splendid and lordly suitors,
233 will not permit that the bow and the quiver be handed to me.
234 But you, noble Eumaeus, must carry the bow through the dwelling
235 and place it into my hands, and tell the women this also:
236 let them shut fast the doors of the hall with their closely fitted joinings;
237 and if any woman should hear a groaning or racket of menfolk
238 here within our walls, let her not come hurrying out through the doorway,
239 but stay where she is in silence and keep to the work that is by her.
240 And to you, noble Philoetius, I entrust the doors of the courtyard;
241 bolt them fast with the key, and quickly cast on the door-bar.”
242 So having spoken, he entered the well-inhabited household,
243 then he went and sat on the chair from which he had arisen;
244 and into the hall came in as well those two thralls of Odysseus.
245 Now Eurymachus already was turning the bow in his fingers,
246 warming it here and there in the blaze and the glow of the firelight;
247 yet not even so could he string it, and greatly his proud heart was groaning.
248 He in vexation spoke, and addressed them and uttered this saying:
249 “Oh for shame, what grief has come over me, for myself and for all men.
250 It is not so much for the marriage that I am lamenting,
251 though I am grieved; for there are many Achaean women besides her,
252 some in seagirt Ithaca here, and some in the other cities.
253 No, but if we are so far lacking the strength and the prowess
254 of godlike Odysseus that we cannot stretch and string
255 this bow, that will be a disgrace for men yet to come to hear of.”
256 Him in turn Antinous answered, the son of Eupeithes:
257 “Eurymachus, it will not be so; and you know it yourself well.
258 Now through the land there is held the feast of the god, and it is holy.
259 Who then would be bending a bow? No, let it rest there in silence.
260 Sit down; and the axes at least we may leave there, every one standing.
261 For I do not think that anyone ever intends to remove them,
262 coming into the hall of Odysseus, son of Laertes.
263 But come, let the wine-pourer first make beginning among the cups,
264 so that after libation we may lay down the curved bow.
265 At dawn bid Melanthius, the goatherd who tends all the she-goats,
266 drive in the goats that are finest by far of all in his keeping,
267 so that, laying the thigh-pieces on for Apollo the famed archer,
268 we may make trial of the bow and accomplish the contest.”
269 So he spoke, Antinous, and his word was pleasing to all of them.
270 Water the heralds poured over their hands for the washing,
271 and the young men filled up the mixing-bowls, crowning them with the drink,
272 and passed it around to them all, beginning the service with goblets.
273 But when they had poured and had drunk as much as the heart was desiring,
274 then among them resourceful Odysseus spoke with deep cunning:
275 “Hear me, you suitors who court the queen of the great renown,
276 so that I may say what the spirit inside of my breast is commanding.
277 Most of all I entreat Eurymachus now, and godlike Antinous,
278 since indeed he also has spoken this word in due measure:
279 for the present let be the bow, and entrust all the matter to heaven;
280 and at dawn some god will grant the victory to whom he wishes.
281 But come, give me the well-polished bow, that here among all of you
282 I may make trial of my hands and my strength, whether still there abides in me
283 force such as once there was in my supple and bendable members,
284 or whether wandering and lack of all care have ruined it wholly.”
285 So he spoke, and they all were indignant beyond any measure,
286 fearing that he might string it — the bow with its beautiful polish.
287 But Antinous scolded him harshly and spoke out and named him:
288 “You unhappy stranger, there are not in you even the slightest wits.
289 Is it not enough that in quiet among us proud men you are feasting,
290 nor are deprived of the meal, while you listen to all of our speeches
291 and to our discourse? No other stranger at all and no beggar
292 gets to hear the speeches and words that belong among us.
293 Honey-sweet wine is what harms you, wine that also harms other men,
294 any man who takes it greedily down and does not drink in due measure.
295 Wine was what drove to madness the Centaur, the glorious Eurytion,
296 in the high hall of the great-hearted lord Peirithoüs,
297 when he came to the Lapiths; and after the wine had deranged him,
298 in his frenzy he wrought evil deeds through the house of Peirithous.
299 Grief took hold of the heroes, and up from their seats they sprang swiftly,
300 dragged him out through the porch to the open, and pitiless bronze sheared
301 both of his ears and his nostrils away; and he, in his spirit
302 crazed, went onward bearing his doom in his senseless heart with him.
303 From that there arose strife and feud between Centaurs and mortals;
304 but he himself, being burdened with wine, was first to find evil.
305 So also for you I foretell a great ruin, if once you should string
306 this bow; for you will encounter no kindness from anyone
307 here in our land, but at once in a black ship we shall send you
308 to King Echetus, the bane of all mortals living upon earth.
309 And from him you will not escape. So drink in quiet, and do not
310 strive in contention with men who are younger and stronger than you are.”
311 In answer prudent Penelope spoke to him, watchful in spirit:
312 “Antinous, it is neither good nor just to dishonor
313 guests of Telemachus, whoever should come to the doors of this household.
314 Do you suppose that this stranger should string the bow of Odysseus,
315 trusting his hands and the strength that belongs to his body,
316 he will conduct me home to his house and make me his bedmate?
317 Not even he himself, I think, hopes this within his own bosom.
318 Let not one of you then sit grieving in spirit about this hereafter,
319 feasting here in this hall, since this would not be fitting at all.”
320 Eurymachus answered her face to face, the son of Polybus:
321 “Daughter of Icarius, prudent Penelope, hear us:
322 it is not this that we think, that he will carry you off; that is not fitting.
323 No, but we shrink from the talk of men and of women,
324 lest at some time one baser Achaean should say it:
325 “Men far inferior woo the wife of a man without blemish,
326 yet they cannot string his bow, well-polished and shapely;
327 but some other beggared man, a wanderer, came among them,
328 easily bent the bow and shot straight through all the iron.”
329 Thus they will speak, and this would become our reproach and dishonor.”
330 In answer prudent Penelope spoke to him, watchful in spirit:
331 “Eurymachus, there is no way for men to have fair glory
332 through all the land, when they consume and dishonor the household
333 of a nobler man than themselves. Why make these things your reproaches?
334 This stranger here is indeed a tall man and strongly fashioned,
335 and he declares that he is a son of a noble father.
336 Come then, give him the polished bow, so that we may behold him.
337 For thus I will speak, and this thing also shall be accomplished:
338 if he shall string it, and Apollo grant him the glory,
339 I will clothe him in cloak and in tunic, garments of beauty,
340 and I will give him a sharp javelin, warder from dogs and from men too,
341 and a two-edged sword; I will give him sandals beneath him,
342 and send him wherever his heart and spirit command him.”
343 Clear-souled Telemachus answered her face to face, saying:
344 “Mother of mine, of the Achaeans there is not any better
345 than I am to give this bow, if I choose, or refuse it,
346 neither of all who hold sway throughout rugged Ithaca,
347 nor of those in the islands toward horse-pasturing Elis.
348 Not one of these will constrain me against my will, if I wish it,
349 even to give to the stranger this bow, to bear it away with him always.
350 But go into the house and see to your own proper labors,
351 to the loom and the distaff, and bid your handmaids beside you
352 follow their work. The bow shall be matter of care for the menfolk,
353 for all men, but for me above all; for the power is mine in the household.”
354 She in her wonder turned and went back again to her chamber,
355 for she laid up within her heart her son’s prudent saying.
356 She climbed up to the upper room with attendant women,
357 and there she wept for Odysseus, her own dear husband,
358 till on her eyelids gray-eyed Athena cast a sweet slumber.
359 But the noble swineherd took up and carried the curved bow;
360 and all through the halls the suitors together were shouting against him.
361 So would a man among those overbearing young men be speaking:
362 “Where are you taking the curved bow, miserable swineherd,
363 vagabond? Soon out among your swine the swift dogs will devour you,
364 lone and apart from men, the dogs that you yourself reared,
365 if Apollo should grant us his grace, and the other immortals.”
366 So they spoke, and he set it down where he stood while he carried it,
367 frightened, because many men in the halls were shouting against him.
368 But from the other side Telemachus called out and threatened:
369 “Old friend, carry the bow on forward. You will not obey all men gladly.
370 Otherwise, though I am younger than you, I will drive you out fieldward,
371 hurling stones at you, since in strength I am stronger than you are.
372 If only against all these men, as many as hold to our household,
373 all the suitors, I were the stronger in hands and in vigor,
374 then I would quickly send some one of them wretchedly homeward,
375 out of our house, since it is evil they scheme and imagine.”
376 So he spoke, and all the suitors laughed out sweetly upon him,
377 and indeed let go of their bitter anger against Telemachus.
378 Through the hall the swineherd, carrying the bow, came onward
379 and stood beside him and put it into the hands of Odysseus.
380 Calling her forth, he spoke to the nurse Eurycleia:
381 “Telemachus bids you, prudent Eurycleia, hear him:
382 bar the doors of the hall, close-fitted firmly together.
383 If any woman should hear within the house some groaning
384 or the crash of men inside our walls, let her not go outward
385 peering from doors, but stay there quietly beside her labor.”
386 So he spoke, and for her the word he had uttered was wingless;
387 and she barred the doors of the halls of the well-built dwelling.
388 Silently out of the house Philoetius sprang through the doorway,
389 and after that he barred the gates of the well-fenced courtyard.
390 Under the porch there lay a cable of seafaring vessel,
391 twisted of byblus, with which he bound fast the gates; then he entered,
392 and going back sat down on the stool from which he had risen,
393 watching Odysseus. And he already was handling the bow-stave,
394 turning it over and over, and testing it this way and that way,
395 lest worms might have eaten the horn in the absence of its master.
396 So would a man speak, glancing across at another beside him:
397 “Surely a connoisseur and an expert thief of bows, this fellow;
398 likely such things as these are lying somewhere at home for him,
399 or he is eager to make one, so in his hands he revolves it
400 this way and that, this vagrant well-schooled in suffering and evil.”
401 So would another among those young men swollen with arrogance:
402 “May he be met with as much of profit and blessing hereafter
403 as ever this man will succeed in stringing that weapon.”
404 So they spoke, these suitors; but then resourceful Odysseus,
405 when he had taken the great bow up and looked it all over,
406 just as a man well skilled in the lyre and practiced in singing
407 easily stretches a string around on a newly made tuning-peg,
408 when he has fastened at either end the well-twisted sheep-gut,
409 so without strain did Odysseus string the great bow in a moment.
410 With his right hand he grasped it and made his trial of the bowstring;
411 sweetly it sang beneath, like the voice of a twittering swallow.
412 Upon all the suitors there came a great sorrow and terror;
413 all of their color was changed, and Zeus gave a mighty thunder,
414 and thereupon much-enduring noble Odysseus was gladdened,
415 glad that the son of shrewd-counseling Cronos had sent him a portent.
416 Up he took a swift arrow lying bare on the table;
417 all of the others were lying inside of the hollow quiver,
418 those that the Achaeans would soon be made to encounter.
419 Laying it on the bow’s grip, he drew the string and the arrow-nock,
420 there from the stool where he sat, and let the shaft fly from him,
421 aiming straight before him; he missed not one of the axe-heads,
422 starting from the first socket, and clean through the whole of them passing,
423 out went the bronze-heavy arrow, through to the outside. Then he said:
424 “Telemachus, the guest sitting here in your halls does not shame you;
425 I did not miss this mark, nor was I long at the stringing.
426 Still my strength is firm within me and has not yet failed me,
427 not as the suitors declare in their scorn and contempt of me.
428 But now it is time for supper to be made for the Achaeans
429 while there is still some light, and thereafter in other fashion
430 to take our delight with song and the lyre, the feast’s adornment.”
431 So he spoke, and he nodded the signal to him with his eyebrows;
432 so Telemachus, dear son of the godlike Odysseus,
433 slung on his sharp sword and clasped in his dear hand his spear-shaft,
434 and stood beside the throne, full-armed in the glittering war-bronze.
