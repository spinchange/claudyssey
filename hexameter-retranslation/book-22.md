# The Odyssey — Book 22

*The slaughter in the hall.*

1 Then resourceful Odysseus stripped off the rags from his body,
2 sprang to the great high threshold, with bow in hand and the quiver
3 full of arrows, and poured the swift shafts out there in a cluster
4 down at his feet, and then among all the suitors he told them:
5 “Here is a trial accomplished, a feat beyond all contending.
6 Now I shall aim at another mark, that no mortal has hit yet,
7 if I can strike it, if Apollo grants me the glory.”
8 So he spoke, and he sighted the bitter shaft straight at Antinous.
9 He was just then on the point of lifting a beautiful goblet,
10 golden, with handles on both sides, and turning it there in his fingers
11 so he might drink of the wine; but slaughter was not in his spirit.
12 Who among men at a feast would ever have thought that a single
13 man in the midst of so many, even though he were mighty,
14 would bring upon him evil death and the black doom with it?
15 But Odysseus, taking full aim at his throat, shot the arrow,
16 and clean through the tender neck went the point of the weapon.
17 Sideways he toppled, and down from his hand fell the cup when it struck him;
18 straight from his nostrils a thick jet rose up of dark blood,
19 blood of a man, and quickly he kicked the table away from him,
20 thrusting it off with his foot, and all the provisions spilled earthward.
21 Bread and the roasted meats were befouled. Then all the suitors
22 raised a great uproar through the hall when they saw the man fallen.
23 Up from their chairs they sprang in confusion about the chamber,
24 glancing around in all directions toward the well-fashioned walling.
25 Nowhere at hand was a shield, nowhere a stout spear to snatch up.
26 Then they reviled Odysseus in furious words and in anger:
27 “Stranger, you shoot at men to your ruin. No longer will any
28 other contests confront you. Now your steep death is upon you.
29 For indeed now you have killed the man who was far the noblest
30 youth among all in Ithaca. Vultures here will devour you.”
31 So each man imagined, since they supposed he had not meant murder,
32 meaning to kill the man by no design. But the fools did not see this,
33 that for them all alike the cords of destruction were fastened.
34 Then with a lowering gaze spoke out resourceful Odysseus:
35 “Dogs, you never expected that I should come home here again from
36 Troy and the land of her people, and so you laid waste to my household;
37 with my serving women you forced yourselves down into bed there,
38 and while I yet lived you sought to win my wife for your own use,
39 having no fear of the gods who possess the broad heaven above us,
40 nor of any revenge from mankind that would come upon you later.
41 Now for you all alike the cords of destruction are fastened.”
42 So he spoke, and pale fear seized hold of them, every one of them.
43 Each man peered all around for some way to flee steep destruction.
44 Then Eurymachus alone made answer and spoke in reply to him:
45 “If indeed you are truly Odysseus, the Ithacan, home now,
46 then what you have spoken is just, for the Achaeans have done these
47 many reckless things in your halls, and many also in the farmland.
48 But now here lies the man who was cause of the whole thing,
49 Antinous. He it was who urged on these actions among us,
50 not that he needed this marriage so much, nor greatly desired it,
51 but with another design, which the son of Cronos denied him:
52 that he himself might reign in the land of well-builded Ithaca,
53 and in ambush kill your son and so make himself master.
54 Now he lies in his fate. But spare your people, I beg you;
55 we in the land hereafter will make due amends before all men
56 for whatever was drunk and devoured in your halls by the suitors.
57 Each of us bringing a price of twenty oxen in payment,
58 bronze and gold shall repay you, until at last your heart softens.
59 Till then, no man could censure your anger and say it was blameful.”
60 Then with a lowering gaze spoke out resourceful Odysseus:
61 “Eurymachus, not if you gave me all your ancestral possessions,
62 all that is yours at present, and added more from elsewhere,
63 not even so would I hold back these hands of mine from the slaughter
64 till the suitors have paid for the whole of their arrogant outrage.
65 Now there lies before you the choice, either stand up and fight me,
66 or flee, if any man thinks to escape his death and destruction.
67 Yet I do not suppose one man of you will escape steep destruction.”
68 So he spoke, and the knees of the others were loosed, and their heartstrings.
69 Then Eurymachus spoke out among them a second time, calling:
70 “Friends, this man will not hold back those invincible hands now,
71 since he has taken the polished bow and the quiver beside him.
72 From the smooth threshold there he will keep on shooting until he
73 kills us all. So let us remember the work of battle.
74 Draw out your swords, and hold up the tables against the arrows,
75 arrows of sudden death, and let us all rush upon him
76 packed together, if so we may push him away from the threshold,
77 drive him out from the doors, and go through the town, and the alarm cry
78 will rise at once. Then that man would have shot his last arrow.”
79 So he spoke, and he drew his sharp sword out of the sheath there,
80 bronze and keen on both edges, and sprang straight forward against him
81 with a blood-freezing yell; but noble Odysseus did not miss him,
82 loosed the shaft and struck him there in the chest by the nipple,
83 and planted the swift-flying arrow deep fast in his liver;
84 from his hand the broadsword fell to the floor, and over the table
85 down he collapsed in a heap, and spilled to the earth all the viands
86 and the two-handled cup; and he battered the ground with his forehead,
87 writhing in anguish of heart, and with both of his feet he kept kicking,
88 shaking the chair; and darkness was poured all over his eyesight.
89 Then Amphinomus rushed at renowned Odysseus in fury,
90 charging headlong against him, and drew his keen-edged sword there,
91 hoping somehow to force him away from the doors; but before him
92 Telemachus struck from behind with a bronze-shod spear in his onset,
93 square in the midst of the shoulders, and drove it on through his chest-bone;
94 thundering he fell, and he smote on the ground with his full brow.
95 Then Telemachus sprang back, leaving the long-shadowing lance there
96 fixed in Amphinomus; for he feared lest one of the Achaeans,
97 while he was tugging out the long-shadowing spear, might attack him
98 rushing with sword, or might strike him down as he bent forward.
99 Then he ran, and very swiftly came to the side of his father,
100 and standing close beside him spoke out winged words in address there:
101 “Father, now I will bring you a shield and a pair of spears here,
102 and a helmet of solid bronze, fitted close over the temples;
103 I myself too will put armor on as I go, and I will give some
104 also to swineherd and cowherd; for to be armed is far better.”
105 Then resourceful Odysseus in answer addressed him and said this:
106 “Bring them at a run, while arrows are still here for my defense yet,
107 lest they should drive me away from the doors, when I stand here alone.”
108 So he spoke, and Telemachus obeyed his beloved father,
109 and went on his way to the chamber where glorious armor was lying.
110 From there he took four shields, and eight spears together,
111 and four bronze helmets thick-crested with horsehair upon them;
112 then he came back bearing them, and quickly reached his dear father.
113 First of all he clad his own body about with the bronze there;
114 and in the same way the two serving men put on the fair armor,
115 and stood on both sides of Odysseus, master of battle and counsel.
116 Now he, so long as the shafts for defense were still there beside him,
117 kept on striking one suitor after another within his own house,
118 aiming unerringly; and they fell one by one at close quarters.
119 But when the arrows deserted the lord as he shot from the bowstring,
120 then he leaned the bow by a pillar of the well-builded hall there,
121 setting it upright against the bright-faced wall by the entry,
122 and around his shoulders he slung a shield made of four hides,
123 and upon his mighty head he set a well-fashioned helmet,
124 horse-haired, and the crest above it nodded terribly over him;
125 then he grasped in his hands two stout spears tipped over with bronze.
126 There was a side-door set in the wall of the well-builded chamber,
127 and a way by the top of the threshold out of the strong hall
128 led to a passage; and well-fitted planks were fastened upon it.
129 Noble Odysseus commanded the godlike swineherd to guard it,
130 standing close by the place; for that was the one sole outlet.
131 Then Agelaus spoke among them, declaring his thought to them all there:
132 “Friends, could not one of you climb through the side-door upward
133 and give word to the people, so the cry might arise all the quicker?
134 Then at once this man would shoot now for the last time with the bowstring.”
135 Then Melanthius the goatherd addressed him again and answered:
136 “No, Agelaus, cherished by Zeus, there is no way to do this;
137 for too terribly near are the fair doors leading into the courtyard,
138 and the mouth of the passage is hard; one man might hold back all of us,
139 if he were strong. But come now, I will bring you armor to harness on
140 out of the chamber; for there, I am thinking, and nowhere else have
141 arms been stored by Odysseus and by his illustrious son here.”
142 So saying, Melanthius the goatherd climbed up through the hall there,
143 making his way by the smoke-vents into Odysseus' storerooms.
144 From there he took out twelve shields, and as many spears with them,
145 and as many bronze helmets thick-crested with horsehair upon them;
146 then he went on his way, and swiftly brought them and gave them to suitors.
147 Then indeed the knees of Odysseus were loosened beneath him,
148 and his dear heart, when he saw them all putting the armor about them
149 and shaking in hand their long spears; and mighty the task seemed before him.
150 Quickly then he addressed Telemachus, speaking winged words to him:
151 “Telemachus, surely one of the women here in these chambers
152 stirs up this evil war against us, or else it is Melanthius.”
153 Then prudent Telemachus answered him back in reply and said this:
154 “Father, I myself am the one who blundered in this, and no other
155 bears the blame, for I left the close-fitted door of the chamber
156 standing ajar; and their watcher was sharper of eye than I was.
157 But go now, noble Eumaeus, and fasten the door of the chamber,
158 and find out whether it is one of the women who does these things,
159 or Melanthius, son of Dolius; for that is the man whom I suspect.”
160 So they were speaking such words to each other there in the great hall.
161 Back to the storeroom Melanthius went, the goatherd of the homestead,
162 meaning to fetch fine armor; but the noble swineherd observed him,
163 and at once he spoke to Odysseus, standing there very near him:
164 “Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
165 there goes again that secretive man whom we both had suspected;
166 into the storeroom he goes; so now tell me truly and clearly,
167 shall I kill him, if indeed I prove to be stronger than he is,
168 or shall I bring him here to you, so he may repay all transgressions,
169 many and vile, that this man has plotted within your own household?”
170 Then resourceful Odysseus in answer addressed him and told him:
171 “I and Telemachus here will restrain the proud suitors within doors,
172 even though in their fury they long with all might for the fighting.
173 You two, twisting around both his feet and his hands from behind him,
174 cast him into the chamber, and fasten the planks at his backside;
175 then let a braided rope be made fast there firmly upon him,
176 haul him up the tall pillar and draw him close to the roofbeams,
177 so that alive for long he may suffer afflictions and anguish.”
178 So he spoke, and the two of them listened with care and obeyed him;
179 off to the chamber they went, and the man there inside did not see them.
180 There in the innermost part of the chamber he searched among armor,
181 while the two took their stand on each side by the doorposts and waited.
182 But when over the threshold stepped Melanthius, goatherd and traitor,
183 in the one hand bearing a well-made helmet of beauty and brightness,
184 and in the other a broad old shield, all stained by the mildew,
185 once of the hero Laertes, which he in his young days had carried;
186 now it had long lain unused, and the seams of its straps were all loosened.
187 Then with a rush the two men seized him and dragged him back inward
188 by the hair, and cast him down on the floor, though his heart was in anguish,
189 and they bound together his feet and his hands with a painful binding,
190 wrenching them hard behind him and throughly, just as he was ordered
191 by the son of Laertes, enduring Odysseus the noble.
192 Then to him they made fast a braided rope in its plaiting,
193 hauled him up the tall pillar and drew him close to the roofbeams.
194 Then in mockery you spoke to him, Eumaeus the swineherd:
195 “Now beyond all doubt, Melanthius, you will keep watch through the nightlong,
196 lying at ease on a soft bed, as indeed is fit and becoming;
197 nor will Dawn, early-born by the streams of the Ocean, escape you
198 when she comes on her golden throne, at the hour when you drive in
199 goats for the suitors throughout the house, to prepare them a banquet.”
200 So there he was left in that place, stretched tight in the deadly binding;
201 and the two men put on their armor, then shut the bright door of the chamber,
202 and went back to Odysseus, shrewd in war and in woven devices.
203 There with fury breathing they stood; on the threshold were four men,
204 while there were many besides within the hall, and all were good fighters.
205 Then came near to them Athena, the daughter of Zeus in her power,
206 taking the shape of Mentor in stature as well as in speaking.
207 Odysseus rejoiced when he saw her, and spoke out and addressed her:
208 “Mentor, ward off destruction, and think on your dear old companion,
209 who would do good by you always; and you are my agemate in seasons.”
210 So he spoke, for he thought it was Pallas Athena who rouses the battle.
211 But the suitors from their side raised a shout through the hall all together,
212 and the first one to chide her was Agelaus, son of Damastor:
213 “Mentor, do not let Odysseus persuade you with words and cajolings
214 to do battle with suitors and stand in defense of his person.
215 For I think that our purpose in this way surely will be accomplished:
216 when we have killed these two, both the father and son, in the hall here,
217 then you also among them will perish for all that you purpose
218 to do in the palace; and you will repay with your own head the payment.
219 But when by bronze we have stripped the strength from the limbs of you all here,
220 all your possessions, whatever you have, both within and without doors,
221 we will mingle with those of Odysseus; and never your children,
222 not your sons, shall be left alive in the halls of your household,
223 nor your daughters, nor your wise-hearted wife walk Ithaca’s city.”
224 So he spoke, and Athena in the deep heart grew angrier still then,
225 and she rebuked Odysseus and scolded with wrathful expressions:
226 “Odysseus, no longer in you does steadfast fury remain now,
227 nor any valor like that when, for Helen the white-armed and noble,
228 nine long years without cease you fought with the Trojans in battle,
229 and in the grim encounter of war you slew many fighters;
230 and by your counsel the broad-wayed city of Priam was taken.
231 How is it now, when you have come home to your house and possessions,
232 that face to face with the suitors you wail to be thought a brave fighter?
233 Come now here, my dear friend, stand by me and look on this action,
234 so that you may know what sort of a man, among hostile opponents,
235 Mentor the son of Alcimus is in repaying a kindness.”
236 So she spoke, but as yet she did not bestow full victory on either,
237 but went on testing the strength and the fighting spirit and endurance
238 both of Odysseus himself and the glorious son at his side there.
239 Then she herself to the smoke-dark roof of the hall darted upward,
240 and there she perched overhead, in the likeness and look of a swallow.
241 Then Agelaus, the son of Damastor, roused up the suitors,
242 Eurynomus with him, Amphimedon, and Demoptolemus,
243 Peisander son of Polyctor, and battle-wise Polybus also;
244 for in the suitors' strength these men stood out as the bravest,
245 all who were still alive and were fighting around for their life-breath;
246 all the others already the bow and the thick-falling arrows had mastered.
247 Then Agelaus addressed them, declaring his word to them all there:
248 “Friends, now at last this man will have his irresistible hands checked;
249 yes, and Mentor has gone, after speaking his empty assurances,
250 and they are left there alone by the first of the doorway's panels.
251 Therefore do not all of you cast your long spears together;
252 no, come, let six men first let fly, if perhaps Zeus may grant it
253 that Odysseus be struck and that glory be won for ourselves here.
254 For the rest are no care, once this man has fallen before us.”
255 So he spoke, and they all, as he bade them, hurled their spears eager;
256 but Athena made all of them vain and deprived them of issue.
257 One man's spear struck fast in the post of the stout-built great hall there,
258 another man's struck in the door that was closely and strongly fitted;
259 another man's bronze-heavy ash shaft fell in against the wall there.
260 But when they had escaped from the suitors' shower of spears then,
261 to his own men then first spoke out the long-suffering godlike Odysseus:
262 “Friends, now I think it is time that I speak and direct all among us
263 to cast at the throng of the suitors, who yearn in their spirit
264 to kill the two of us, on top of the evils before this.”
265 So he spoke, and they all let fly their sharp spears together,
266 aiming straight before them. Odysseus struck Demoptolemus,
267 Telemachus killed Euryades; Elatus fell to the swineherd,
268 and the master of cattle killed Peisander there also.
269 Then all of them together bit down on the monstrous floor there;
270 and the suitors recoiled to the innermost part of the chamber.
271 But those men rushed upon them and pulled out their spears from the corpses.
272 Then again did the suitors let fly their sharp spears eager,
273 but Athena made many of these as well to fail of their issue.
274 One man's spear struck fast in the post of the stout-built great hall there,
275 another man's struck in the door that was closely and strongly fitted;
276 another man's bronze-heavy ash shaft fell in against the wall there.
277 But Amphimedon struck Telemachus there on the wrist of his hand, just
278 grazing him, and the bronze only nicked the skin at the extremity.
279 And Ctesippus over the shield with his long spear then struck at
280 Eumaeus' shoulder, but it flew past and fell on the earth there.
281 Then those men around wise-hearted Odysseus of many devices
282 cast their sharp spears back in among the crowd of the suitors.
283 There Odysseus the sacker of cities struck down Eurydamas,
284 Amphimedon fell to Telemachus, Polybus to the swineherd;
285 and then the neat-herd, the master and watcher of cattle, struck Ctesippus
286 full in the chest, and boasting over him spoke out and addressed him:
287 “Son of Polytherses, lover of mockery, never hereafter
288 yield to your witless folly and speak some over-proud saying;
289 leave speech to the gods, since surely they are much stronger than mortals.
290 Here is a guest-gift back in return for the hoof that you gave once
291 to godlike Odysseus, while he went like a beggar through his hall.”
292 So spoke the herdsman in charge of the oxen with curving horns there;
293 but Odysseus stabbed Damastor's son from nearby with the long spear.
294 And Telemachus stabbed Leocritus, Evenor's son there,
295 with his spear in the midriff, and drove the bronze clean through him;
296 and he pitched on his face and smote the earth with his forehead.
297 Then Athena lifted on high the man-destroying aegis,
298 up from the roof overhead, and the minds in them reeled into panic.
299 Then through the hall they stampeded, like cattle that run in a herd when
300 the darting gadfly has rushed in among them and set them all shuddering
301 in the spring season, when all the days are lengthening onward.
302 And as vultures with hooked claws and beaks curved inward and deadly,
303 coming down from the mountains, swoop upon flocks of small birds there,
304 and they skim over the plain, cringing low under the cloud-bank,
305 but the vultures leap on them and kill them, and no defense comes,
306 neither escape; and men take joy in watching the hunting;
307 so these men, rushing around through the house at the suitors before them,
308 struck them to this side and that; and terrible groaning arose there
309 from skulls being battered, and all the floor steamed up with bloodshed.
310 Then Leodes rushed up and clasped Odysseus fast by the knees there,
311 and beseeching him earnestly spoke these winged words in entreaty:
312 “By your knees, Odysseus, I implore you; respect me and spare me.
313 For I declare I never spoke to any of the women
314 in your halls, nor did I perform any insolent action;
315 rather I sought to restrain the suitors, if any man acted
316 in such a way; but they would not obey me and keep off their evil.
317 Therefore by their own reckless deeds they have earned a shameful destruction.
318 But I, their seer at the rites, though guilty of nothing among them,
319 shall lie with the rest; for there is no gratitude after good service.”
320 Then with a darkening glance wise Odysseus answered and told him:
321 “If indeed among these men you boast that you are their diviner,
322 many a time, I suppose, in these halls you have prayed in your spirit
323 that far from me the fulfilment of sweet homecoming might be,
324 and that my cherished wife might follow you and bear you children.
325 Therefore you will not escape from a death most bitter for mourning.”
326 So then having spoken, he seized with his massive hand a sword there
327 lying, the one that Agelaus had flung to the ground before him
328 as he was killed; and he drove it straight through the midst of his neck there.
329 While he was still speaking, his head was mingled up with the dust then.
330 Yet still the singer escaped from the black fate hovering near him,
331 Phemius, Terpes’ son, who had sung among those suitors by compulsion.
332 He stood there holding within both his hands the clear-toned lyre there
333 close beside the side-door; and within him his heart was divided,
334 whether, slipping out of the hall, to go to the great altar of Zeus there,
335 Zeus of the Court, and sit by the altar well fashioned in stonework,
336 where Laertes and great Odysseus had often burned there
337 thighbones of oxen, or else rush forward and clasp at Odysseus’ knees there.
338 As he turned it about in his mind, this seemed the better decision:
339 to take hold of the knees of Odysseus, son of Laertes.
340 Then indeed he set down the hollow lyre on the ground there
341 midway between the mixing-bowl and the chair with silver studs on it,
342 and he himself rushed up to Odysseus and clasped at his knees then,
343 and in entreaty to him he addressed him with winged words, saying:
344 “By your knees I implore you, Odysseus; respect me and pity.
345 Grief for this deed will come upon you hereafter, if you kill me,
346 me, a singer who sings for gods immortal and mortal men also.
347 I am self-taught, and a god has breathed into my inward spirit
348 every kind of path for song; and I seem fit to sing in your presence
349 even as if before a god. Therefore do not desire to cut my throat off.
350 Telemachus too, your own beloved son, would surely attest this,
351 that not by my own wish and not from any craving whatever
352 did I come to your house and sing for the suitors at banquet,
353 but they were far more numerous, stronger than I, and compelled me.”
354 So he spoke, and the sacred strength of Telemachus heard him,
355 and at once he addressed his father, who stood there close beside him:
356 “Stop, and do not in any way stab this guiltless man with the bronze now.
357 Let us save Medon the herald as well, who always in our house
358 cared for me when I was a child in my father’s dwelling,
359 if indeed Philoetius has not killed him already, or else
360 the swineherd, or he met your assault as you surged through the hall here.”
361 So he spoke, and Medon heard him, a man of understanding,
362 for he lay huddled beneath a chair, and around him an oxhide,
363 newly flayed, had been wrapped, while he fled from the black doom near him.
364 Quickly he rose from beneath the chair, and swiftly cast off the oxhide,
365 then he rushed up to Telemachus and clasped hard at his knees there,
366 and in entreaty to him he addressed him with winged words, saying:
367 “Dear friend, here I am, yes, myself; hold back, and tell your father
368 not to destroy me in his overpowering might with the sharp bronze,
369 angered against the suitor-men, who kept consuming his substance
370 here in the halls, and in their folly they gave no thought at all to you.”
371 Then with a smile wise Odysseus of many devices addressed him:
372 “Take heart, since this man has held you back and preserved you from ruin,
373 so that you may know in your heart, and hereafter may tell it to others,
374 how much better good action is always than wickedness evil.
375 But now, going outside of the hall, sit down in the courtyard,
376 out of the bloodshed, both you and the singer renowned among many,
377 while I within the house accomplish the work that is needful.”
378 So he spoke, and the two went out from the hall and departed,
379 and indeed both sat down by the altar of Zeus the Almighty,
380 glancing around every way, for their hearts still expected destruction.
381 Then Odysseus looked round through his house, lest any man still there
382 living might yet have escaped him in hiding from black destruction.
383 But he saw them all lying in blood and in dust there before him,
384 many of them, like fishes that fishermen have from the gray sea
385 dragged in the meshes of nets to the hollow curve of the seashore,
386 out from the surf of the salt sea; and all of them lie on the sand there
387 yearning back for the sea-waves, as there on the beaches they sprawl there,
388 and the shining Sun takes away the life-breath out of them wholly.
389 So at that time the suitors lay heaped one upon one another.
390 Then Odysseus of many devices addressed Telemachus, saying:
391 “Telemachus, come now, call me the nurse Eurycleia,
392 so that I may speak out the word that within my heart is intended.”
393 So he spoke, and Telemachus obeyed his beloved father,
394 and shaking the door he called to the nurse Eurycleia:
395 “Come here now, rise up, old woman born in the ancient ages,
396 you who watch over the women servants throughout our great household.
397 Come now; my father is calling for you, that he may tell you something.”
398 So he spoke, and for her the message remained not winged, but heeded.
399 She opened the doors of the well-inhabited halls there before them,
400 and she went on her way, while Telemachus led on before her.
401 Then she found great Odysseus among the bodies of the slaughtered,
402 splashed with blood and with gore, like a lion
403 that after feeding on some farm bull comes back from the pasture;
404 all of his chest and his cheeks on both sides there
405 are blood-smeared, and fearful he looks when a man meets his visage;
406 so was Odysseus befouled on his feet and his hands up above them.
407 Then when she saw the dead men and the boundless blood all around them,
408 straightway she longed to cry out aloud, for she saw a great action;
409 but Odysseus checked and restrained her, eager though she was,
410 and calling to her he spoke with winged words in address there:
411 “In your heart, old woman, rejoice, but hold back and do not cry out;
412 it is not holy to boast above men who have been slaughtered.
413 These men the gods’ destiny crushed, and their own brutal actions;
414 they did not honor any earth-dwelling mortal,
415 neither the bad nor the good, whoever came there among them;
416 therefore through their own reckless sins they drew on shameful destruction.
417 But come now, tell me the women that are here in the chambers,
418 those who dishonor me, and those who are guiltless and faithful.”
419 Then in reply the dear nurse Eurycleia addressed him:
420 “Therefore, my child, I will tell you the truth of the matter.
421 There are fifty women for you in the halls of the household,
422 serving-women, whom we have taught to attend to their labors,
423 both to card out the wool and endure the hardship of bondage.
424 Of these, twelve have all taken to shameless behavior;
425 they respect neither me nor Penelope our lady.
426 Telemachus recently grew to manhood, yet his mother
427 would not allow him to rule with authority over the women.
428 But come, let me go up to the shining room in the upper story
429 and tell your wife, on whom some god has cast a deep slumber.”
430 Then in reply spoke Odysseus of the many devices:
431 “Do not awaken her yet; but tell the women here
432 to come, those very ones who before were devising vile actions.”
433 So he spoke, and the old nurse went through the hall of the dwelling,
434 carrying the word to the women and urging them to come forward.
435 Then to his side he summoned Telemachus, also the cowherd
436 and the swineherd, and spoke to them winged words in address there:
437 “Now begin carrying out the dead, and order the women to help you;
438 after that let them cleanse the beautiful chairs and the tables
439 using water and sponges that are pierced with many holes.
440 Then when you have set in order the whole house completely,
441 lead out the serving-women from the firm-built hall of the household,

454 Then Telemachus and the cowherd and also the swineherd

457 But when they had set all the great hall fully in order,
458 then from the strong-built hall they led out the serving-women,
459 to the place between the round room and the excellent fence of the courtyard,
460 and penned them within a strait place where there was no escaping.
461 Then prudent Telemachus began and spoke out among them:
462 “No, not by any clean death will I take away the life breath
463 of these women who poured disgrace upon my head
464 and on my mother’s, and slept by the side of the suitors.”
465 So he spoke, and fastening a cable of a dark-prowed vessel
466 to a great pillar, he looped it around the round chamber,
467 stretching it taut up high, so none with the feet could touch earth there.
468 As when long-winged thrushes or doves
469 dash into a snare that stands in a thicket,
470 seeking a resting place, and a hateful bed has received them,
471 so in a row the women held up their heads, and around them all
472 nooses were fixed on their necks, so that they might die most piteously.
473 They fluttered with their feet a little while, but not very long there.
474 Then they led Melanthius out through the porch and across the courtyard;
475 and from him with pitiless bronze they cut away nose and both ears,
476 and they pulled out his genitals, to give raw for the dogs to devour,
477 and in the fury of heart they chopped off his hands and his feet there.
478 Then after they had washed off both hands and feet of the slaughter,
479 into the house to Odysseus they went; and the work was accomplished.
480 Then he spoke to the dear nurse Eurycleia: “
481 “Bring sulfur, old mother, a cure against evils, and bring me the fire,
482 so I may sulfur the hall; and bid Penelope also
483 to come here down to me with her attendant women;
484 urge all the serving-women as well through the house to come hither.”
485 Then in reply to him spoke the dear nurse, Eurycleia:
486 “Yes indeed, my child, what you have said is fitting and proper.
487 Come then, let me bring you a mantle and tunic for clothing;
488 do not so, with your broad shoulders wrapped all over in tatters,
489 stand in the halls of your house; it would be shameful and blameworthy.”
490 Then resourceful Odysseus answered and spoke to her:
491 “First of all let there now be fire for me in the hall.”
492 So he spoke, and the dear nurse Eurycleia did not disobey him,
493 but she brought him the fire and the sulfur; and then Odysseus
494 thoroughly fumed the hall and the house and the courtyard around it.
495 Then the old woman went through the beautiful house of Odysseus,
496 carrying word to the women and urgently calling them onward.
497 Out of the hall then they came with torches held in their hands there.
498 Round him they gathered in throngs and embraced Odysseus closely,
499 kissing his head and his shoulders in love, and clasping his hands fast.
500 Then a sweet longing came over his heart for weeping and wailing,
501 for in his heart and his mind he knew every one of the women.
