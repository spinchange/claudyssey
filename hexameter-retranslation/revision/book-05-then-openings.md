# Book 5 — "Then"-openings audit

**Examined: 5. Kept (warranted): 3. Rewritten: 2. Residual "Then"-openings: 3.**

Warrant rule: a verse may open with "Then" only where the Greek carries a real
temporal adverb at that position — τότε, δὴ τότε, καὶ τότε, ἔπειτα, αὐτὰρ
ἔπειτα, temporal ἔνθα, or an ἦμος-clause. A bare connective (δέ, αὐτάρ, ἀτάρ,
ὣς + verb, ἦ ῥα, καί, γάρ, τε) or asyndeton does not warrant it. Where three or
more warranted "Then"-openings crowd inside about ten lines, the least emphatic
is varied anyway.

**Scope note.** The brief anticipated roughly 28 "Then"-openings in this book.
The actual count is 5. See the closing note on where the discrepancy comes from.

---

**05.237** δῶκε δʼ ἔπειτα σκέπαρνον ἐύξοον· ἦρχε δʼ ὁδοῖο
- OLD: Then she presented a smoothly polished adze and conducted
- NEW: Gave him a smoothly polished adze after that, and conducted
- Borderline-warranted, varied for crowding-in-miniature. ἔπειτα is genuine, but
  it is *not* clause-initial: the Greek leads with the verb (δῶκε δʼ ἔπειτα),
  and the old line promoted a mid-clause adverb into a sentence opening. With
  05.233 (καὶ τότʼ, a true clause-initial "Then") only four lines above, the two
  openings were reading as a pattern rather than as two separate acts. The fix
  restores the Greek's own verb-first order — the most Homeric-invisible opening
  available in English — and keeps ἔπειτα as "after that," where the Greek in
  fact has it. δῶκε at 237 also now echoes δῶκέν at 234 ("Into his palms she
  fitted…" / "Gave him…"), as the Greek's repeated δῶκε does.

**05.311** τῷ κʼ ἔλαχον κτερέων, καί μευ κλέος ἦγον Ἀχαιοί·
- OLD: Then I had won full rites for my death and Achaeans had carried my glory;
- NEW: In that case I had won rites, and Achaeans had carried my glory;
- Habitual. There is no temporal adverb here at all: τῷ κε is the apodosis
  marker of the past unreal condition opened by ὡς δὴ ἐγώ γʼ ὄφελον at 308
  ("would that I had died… — *in that case* I would have won…"). The old "Then"
  read as sequence where the Greek means consequence, which flattened the whole
  counterfactual. Secondary gain: the old line ran to seven beats and carried
  "full" and "for my death" as padding (κτερέων is the funeral rites; the death
  is already the subject of 308–310). The new line scans six.

## Kept — warranted

**05.233** (καὶ τότʼ), **05.297** (καὶ τότʼ), **05.406** (καὶ τότʼ).

All three are clause-initial καὶ τότε — the strongest warrant in the list.
297 and 406 are the same formula verbatim in both Greek and English
(καὶ τότʼ Ὀδυσσῆος λύτο γούνατα καὶ φίλον ἦτορ / "Then were Odysseus' knees
unstrung and the heart in his body"), and the repetition is Homer's, not the
translator's; they stay identical. No three warranted openings fall within ten
lines of one another, so the crowding rule fired only in the softened form noted
at 237.

## Scope discrepancy

The brief's estimate of ~28 assumed a book-wide tic. Book 5 does not have one at
verse-opening position. Two things likely inflated the estimate:

1. **Mid-sentence continuation lines.** Five further verses begin with lowercase
   "then" as the continuation of a sentence started on the previous line — 96,
   364, 391, 401, 459. These are not openings; they are the interiors of
   clauses that happen to break across the verse. All five are also warranted
   anyway: 96 καὶ τότε δή, 391 καὶ τότʼ ἔπειτʼ, 459 καὶ τότε δὴ, 364 the
   apodosis of an ἐπὴν-clause, 401 καὶ δή. Left untouched.
2. **Interior "then."** The word appears 23 times in the book overall, mostly
   inside lines ("both then entered," "he felled, then trimmed"). Those are a
   separate question from opening position and outside this pass's remit.

If the ~28 figure came from a different counting method — one that includes the
continuation lines and interior occurrences — say so and I will re-run against
that definition. As the rule is written (verses that OPEN with "Then"), the
book's true exposure was 5, and the two genuinely unearned ones are now fixed.
