Book 20 carried most of the poem's remaining formula drift: seven verses opened
"Thus spoke ..." / "Thus he spoke" against a Greek ὣς ἔφατ(ο)/ὣς φάτο, and the
two participial ὣς εἰπών verses had drifted in opposite directions (one to the
finite "So he spoke," one to a bare "Having spoken"). All are now regularized.

## Job 1 — speech-formula standardization

**20.247** ὣς ἔφατʼ Ἀμφίνομος, τοῖσιν δʼ ἐπιήνδανε μῦθος.
- OLD: Thus spoke Amphinomus, and his counsel was pleasing among them.
- NEW: So he spoke, Amphinomus, and his counsel was pleasing among them.
- The Greek names the speaker in apposition after the formula; the English now
  does the same instead of folding the name into the verb.

**20.275** ὣς ἔφατʼ Ἀντίνοος· ὁ δʼ ἄρʼ οὐκ ἐμπάζετο μύθων.
- OLD: Thus spoke Antinous; but he paid no heed to his speaking.
- NEW: So he spoke, Antinous; but he paid no heed to his sayings.
- Same shape as 247. μύθων is plural, so "sayings" — which also matches 384,
  where the identical half-line οὐκ ἐμπάζετο μύθων recurs.

**20.268** ὣς ἔφαθʼ, οἱ δʼ ἄρα πάντες ὀδὰξ ἐν χείλεσι φύντες
- OLD: Thus he spoke, and they all bit hard with their teeth on their lip-edges,
- NEW: So he spoke, and they all bit hard with their teeth on their lip-edges,

**20.320** ὣς ἔφαθʼ, οἱ δʼ ἄρα πάντες ἀκὴν ἐγένοντο σιωπῇ·
- OLD: Thus he spoke, and they all became wholly silent in stillness.
- NEW: So he spoke, and they all of them then became hushed in a silence.
- Rebalanced, not padded: "wholly" goes, ἄρα πάντες gets its "all of them,"
  and ἀκὴν ... σιωπῇ ("hushed ... in silence") keeps both of its words.

**20.345** ὣς φάτο Τηλέμαχος· μνηστῆρσι δὲ Παλλὰς Ἀθήνη
- OLD: So spoke Telemachus. Then Pallas Athena among them
- NEW: So he spoke, Telemachus. Pallas Athena among them
- Also removes a line-initial-clause "Then" that had no δέ-warrant of its own.

**20.384** ὣς ἔφασαν μνηστῆρες· ὁ δʼ οὐκ ἐμπάζετο μύθων,
- OLD: Thus the suitors were speaking, but he took no heed of their sayings;
- NEW: So they spoke, the suitors; but he took no heed of their sayings;
- ὣς ἔφασαν is plural, so the ruled plural "So they spoke," with the subject in
  apposition as at 247 and 275.

**20.299** ὣς εἰπὼν ἔρριψε βοὸς πόδα χειρὶ παχείῃ.
- OLD: So he spoke and with his heavy hand he hurled an ox-hoof at him,
- NEW: So having spoken, with heavy hand he hurled an ox-hoof at him,
- Participial ὣς εἰπών was reading as the finite formula. Now the poem's
  dominant participial rendering; the freed syllables absorb the change.

**20.371** ὣς εἰπὼν ἐξῆλθε δόμων εὖ ναιεταόντων,
- OLD: Having spoken, he went from the well-inhabited dwelling,
- NEW: So having spoken, he went from the well-inhabited dwelling,
- Same formula as 299, now identical in English as in the Greek.

**20.375** ὧδε δέ τις εἴπεσκε νέων ὑπερηνορεόντων·
- OLD: Thus would a man among those overbearing young suitors be saying:
- NEW: So would a man among those overbearing young suitors be saying:
- The iterative εἴπεσκε keeps its own distinct shape, standardized to the
  poem's dominant "So would (a man) ..." (as at 4.324, 4.331, 17.170, 21.361).

### Residual *Thus* kept

**20.172 / 20.240** ὣς οἱ μὲν τοιαῦτα πρὸς ἀλλήλους ἀγόρευον
- KEPT: Thus they were speaking such things in turn to one another;
- KEPT: Thus they were speaking such things in turn to one another.
- Not the finite ὣς φάτο formula but the scene-transition "in this way they
  were conversing." ὣς genuinely means "in this way," and the Greek repeats the
  verse verbatim at both places — so the English repeats verbatim too.

## Job 2 — here-final sweep

No *here*-final verse in Book 20 was warranted: none of the ten had ἐνθάδε,
αὐτόθι, δεῦρο, τῇδε, or locative ὧδε at that position.

**20.154–155** ἔρχεσθε κρήνηνδε, καὶ οἴσετε θᾶσσον ἰοῦσαι. / ἀλλὰ μάλʼ ἦρι νέονται
- OLD: and bring it back with speed; for not much longer away from the hall here
       / will the suitors remain, but early indeed they will gather,
- NEW: and bring it back with all speed; for not much longer will linger
       / the suitors away from the hall, but early indeed they will gather,
- The enjambment was rebalanced across the pair rather than padded: "the hall"
  moves down into 155, and 154 closes on the verb.

**20.223** ἐξικόμην φεύγων, ἐπεὶ οὐκέτʼ ἀνεκτὰ πέλονται·
- OLD: since these things are no longer at all to be borne or endured here;
- NEW: fleeing, since these things no longer at all are endurable;
- φεύγων ("fleeing") was dropped by the old line and is restored; the recovered
  participle pays for the cut filler, and ἀνεκτά is one word, not two verbs.

**20.227** βουκόλʼ, ἐπεὶ οὔτε κακῷ οὔτʼ ἄφρονι φωτὶ ἔοικας,
- OLD: "Cowherd, since you resemble no wicked man and no fool here,
- NEW: "Cowherd, since you resemble no man that is wicked or witless,
- οὔτε ... οὔτε governs two adjectives of one φωτί; the new line keeps the
  single "man" the Greek has and closes on the alliterating pair.

**20.267** καὶ χειρῶν, ἵνα μή τις ἔρις καὶ νεῖκος ὄρηται.
- OLD: lest some quarrel arise and a fight be stirred in the hall here."
- NEW: lest some quarrel arise between us and strife be awakened."
- "in the hall" has no warrant in this line at all (it was imported filler);
  ὄρηται is "be stirred up / awakened," which supplies the trochaic close.

**20.295** ξείνους Τηλεμάχου, ὅς κεν τάδε δώμαθʼ ἵκηται.
- OLD: any guest of Telemachus who may come to this house here.
- NEW: any guest of Telemachus who may arrive at this dwelling.
- τάδε is already spent on "this"; ἵκηται is "arrive," which lands the close.

**20.312** μήλων σφαζομένων οἴνοιό τε πινομένοιο
- OLD: sheep being slaughtered, and wine being drunk, and bread being consumed here;
- NEW: sheep being slaughtered, and wine being drunk, and the bread being eaten;
- Three parallel passive participles; the third now matches the first two in
  weight instead of reaching for a Latinate verb plus filler.

**20.329** νοστήσειν Ὀδυσῆα πολύφρονα ὅνδε δόμονδε,
- OLD: wise Odysseus would return again to this house here,
- NEW: wise Odysseus would come back again to the house of his fathers,
- ὅνδε δόμονδε is "to his own house" — possessive, not deictic. Kept distinct
  from 20.239, which renders the same idea in the neighboring speech.

**20.362** εἰς ἀγορὴν ἔρχεσθαι, ἐπεὶ τάδε νυκτὶ ἐΐσκει.
- OLD: to the assembly place, since these things seem to him like night here."
- NEW: to the assembly place, since these things seem to him nighttime."
- νυκτὶ ἐΐσκει = "he likens to night." Dropping "like ... here" for the plain
  noun is both shorter and closer.

**20.365** εἰσί μοι ὀφθαλμοί τε καὶ οὔατα καὶ πόδες ἄμφω
- OLD: I have eyes of my own and ears and both of my feet here,
- NEW: I have eyes of my own, and ears, and my two feet under me,
- ἄμφω ("both/two") is kept; "under me" replaces the filler with the sense the
  boast actually carries — Theoclymenus can walk out unescorted.

**20.379** ἔμπαιον οὐδὲ βίης, ἀλλʼ αὔτως ἄχθος ἀρούρης.
- OLD: and nothing of strength, but merely a burden of earth here.
- NEW: and nothing of strength, but merely a burden upon the ploughland.
- ἄρουρα is tilled land, not bare earth; the poem already renders it "ploughland"
  at 19.593. The proper noun supplies the falling close.

### Caught by the same lint

**20.135** οὐκ ἄν μιν νῦν, τέκνον, ἀναίτιον αἰτιόῳο.
- OLD: "Child, do not blame the guiltless woman without cause now.
- NEW: "Child, you would not now blame her, the guiltless, with any blaming.
- Not a *here* line, but it ended on filler *now*. The Greek does have νῦν —
  early in the line, not at the close — so it is repositioned rather than cut.
  The new line also restores the ἀναίτιον / αἰτιόῳο figura etymologica
  ("guiltless ... blaming") that the old line flattened.
