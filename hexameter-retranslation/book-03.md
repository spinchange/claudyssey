# The Odyssey — Book 3

*Pylos: Nestor's tales of the returns; Telemachus rides for Sparta.*

1 Now from the beautiful water rose Helios, suddenly leaping
2 up to the brazen expanse of the sky, there to shine for immortals
3 and for the mortals who live on the grain-giving breadth of the plowland.
4 Into the strong-built city of Neleus, Pylos, they entered.
5 There by the shore of the sea, all the people were making an offering,
6 black-colored bulls, to the dark-haired shaker and lord of the earthways.
7 Nine were the companies seated, five hundred men in every company;
8 nine were the bulls that were standing in front of each gathered division.
9 Just as they tasted the innards and burned the thighbones for godhead,
10 when they came straight into the harbor; they brailed the symmetrical vessel's
11 sails and furled them above; and they moored her, themselves disembarking.
12 Young Telemachus left the ship, with Athena proceeding before him.
13 First the gray-eyed goddess Athena addressed him and gave him instruction:
14 “Young Telemachus, now you have no further need to be bashful,
15 not in the least; for precisely because of this purpose you voyaged,
16 seeking your father, to learn where the earth has concealed him and what doom
17 met him. Go straight to the horse-breaking Nestor and stand there before him.
18 Let us discover the counsel concealed in the depths of his bosom.
19 Ask him yourself, so that he may reveal the infallible true word.
20 No lie shall come from his mouth, for his mind is exceedingly prudent.”
21 In turn replied the thoughtful and steadfast young Telemachus:
22 “Mentor, how shall I go to the man, and with what words shall I greet him?
23 Still I am little experienced in the elaborate art of discussion,
24 and a young man is embarrassed to question a man who is older.”
25 Once more the gray-eyed goddess Athena addressed him, saying:
26 “Young Telemachus, some words your own understanding shall show you,
27 others a god shall suggest; for I do not imagine you entered
28 life or were nurtured apart from the gods’ everlasting affection.”
29 So having spoken, Athena proceeded ahead of him swiftly;
30 close in the steps of the goddess he followed her onward and swiftly.
31 Into the place of assembly they came, to the rows of the Pylians.
32 Nestor was seated there with his sons, and around them companions
33 readied the feast, some roasting the meat, while the others were skewering.
34 Soon as the gathering noticed the strangers, they came in a body,
35 clasping their hands in a welcome and asking the two to be seated.
36 First Peisistratus, Nestor's son, as he came up before them,
37 took them both by the hand and invited them into the banquet,
38 seating them there on the soft woolen fleeces that covered the seashore,
39 close by his brother Thrasymedes and Nestor his father.
40 Portions of innards he gave them and poured out the wine for their drinking
41 into a cup made of gold, and he pledged her and spoke in salutation
42 there to Athena, the daughter of Zeus who possesses the aegis:
43 “Stranger, begin now by praying to lordly Poseidon the sea-god,
44 since his is the banquet upon which you came when you entered.
45 But when you pour him libation and pray in the customary manner,
46 give to this other man also the cup of the honey-sweet vintage,
47 so he may pour, for I think he too makes prayer to immortals.
48 All human beings have need of the gods who are living forever.
49 Yet he is younger, the same age as I in the order of seasons;
50 therefore to you do I first give the beautiful goblet of gold.”
51 So having spoken, he set the cup of sweet wine in her fingers.
52 Joy filled Athena because of the thoughtful and equitable young man,
53 since in her hand before anyone else's he placed the gold vessel.
54 Straightway she prayed at considerable length to the lordly Poseidon:
55 “Hear me, Poseidon, the god who encircles and holds the earth firmly;
56 do not begrudge the fulfillment of these our prayers and intentions.
57 First upon Nestor and all of his sons now bestow recognition;
58 afterward grant to the rest of the Pylians gracious repayment,
59 paying them all in return for this glorious hundredfold offering.
60 Grant that I and Telemachus sail to our home with the purpose
61 fully accomplished for which in our swift black ship we have come here.”
62 So then she prayed, and was herself fulfilling the whole of the prayer.
63 Into the hands of Telemachus placed she the beautiful wine cup;
64 just as the goddess had prayed, so too prayed the dear son of Odysseus.
65 After the outermost meat had been roasted and taken from firelight,
66 they shared portions and feasted together upon the magnificent banquet.
67 But when desire for food and for drinking was put from their bodies,
68 Gerenian Nestor the horseman began to address the assembly:
69 “Now is a better occasion to question and ask of the strangers
70 who they may be, since already the feast has delighted their spirits.
71 Strangers, who are you? From where have you sailed on the waterway pathways?
72 Come you for business, or wander without an appointed direction,
73 even as pirates who rove on the face of the waters and hazard
74 all their own lives while bringing affliction to people of strange lands?”
75 In turn replied the thoughtful and steadfast young Telemachus,
76 now full of courage; Athena herself put the strength in his spirit,
77 so he could ask for a word of his father, so long in his absence,
78 also that noble renown might be his to hold among mortals:
79 “Nestor, the son of great Neleus, glory renowned of Achaeans,
80 you ask from where we have come, and I freely shall give you the answer.
81 We are from Ithaca under the tree-covered mountain of Neion;
82 private, and not of the people, is this our affair I am speaking.
83 I am pursuing the far-ranging fame of my father, to hear some
84 word of the steadfast and godlike Odysseus, whom people have told me
85 fought there beside you and brought down ruin on Troyland’s fortress.
86 All of the other Achaeans, as many as fought with the Trojans,
87 we have discovered by word, and the bitter death each encountered.
88 Only his death has the son of great Cronos made past all discovering.
89 Nobody has the assurance to say where the man may have perished,
90 whether on land he went down underneath the attack of his foemen,
91 or in the deep under waves of the sea-goddess Amphitrite.
92 Therefore I come to your knees in the hope you consent to inform me,
93 telling the bitter death that he met, whether you were an eyewitness,
94 or from the speech of another unfortunate wanderer heard it.
95 More than all others his mother gave birth to the man for affliction.
96 Do not diminish the story from pity or courtesy toward me;
97 tell me in full every sight that your own eyes encountered before you.
98 Hear my entreaty: if ever my excellent father Odysseus
99 promised a word or an action to you and fulfilled what he promised
100 there in the country of Troy, where Achaeans endured their afflictions,
101 call it to mind for me now, and reveal the infallible true word.”
102 Answering him spoke Gerenian Nestor the horseman, saying:
103 “Dear child, since you remind me of grief and the hardships we suffered
104 there in that country, we ungovernable sons of Achaeans—
105 all we endured as we roved in the ships on the mist-covered sea-way,
106 looking for plunder wherever Achilles conducted our war band,
107 all we endured while we fought at the great city ruled by King Priam—
108 there through the years the best of our people were slaughtered in battle.
109 There lies Ajax, beloved of Ares, and there lies Achilles;
110 there Patroclus, whose counsel was equal to counsel of godhead;
111 there my own well-beloved son, altogether courageous and blameless,
112 swift-footed Antilochus, excellent runner and excellent spearman.
113 Many the other afflictions we suffered besides them; but what man,
114 born among mortals, could tell and enumerate all of the story?
115 No, not if you remained for five full years or for six years,
116 asking me all of the evils the excellent Argives encountered;
117 sooner, exhausted, you then would return to the land of your fathers.
118 Nine years long we were stitching destruction around them and plotting
119 every conceivable trickery, and Cronos' son scarcely ended it.
120 Nobody there was ever willing to match him in cunning
121 face to face, since by far the greatest was godlike Odysseus
122 in every form of deception—your father, if you are truly
123 born from his blood. I am seized with astonishment, looking upon you.
124 Certainly all of your speeches resemble the speeches he offered;
125 no one would think that a younger man could resemble him speaking.
126 All of the time we remained there, the noble Odysseus and I would
127 never be found in assembly or council opposing each other.
128 One heart between us, with wisdom of mind and considerate judgment,
129 guided the Argives in counsel, devising the best way before them.
130 Yet when at last we had ravaged the steep-walled city of Priam,
131 entered the ships, and a god had dispersed the Achaeans asunder,
132 Zeus in the depth of his heart designed a calamitous homeward
133 road for the Argives, for not every man had intelligence in him,
134 nor was he just. Many therefore encountered a destiny evil,
135 all through the ruinous wrath of the mighty god's gray-eyed daughter.
136 She set a quarrel between the two sons who were born of great Atreus.
137 Both of them called the Achaeans at once to a general meeting,
138 rashly and out of due order, when Helios sank in the westward,
139 while all the sons of Achaea arrived there made heavy by wine-drink.
140 Both of the kings then explained why the host had been called to assembly.
141 There Menelaus encouraged the whole of the gathered Achaeans
142 all to remember the journey for home on the broad back of ocean.
143 Agamemnon was not at all pleased by the words of his brother;
144 he wished to hold the host and to make the appropriate offerings,
145 holy hecatombs, hoping to soften Athena's harsh anger—
146 fool that he was, for he knew not the goddess would not be persuaded.
147 Minds of the gods everlasting are not turned around in a moment.
148 So the two stood and exchanged bitter words with each other before us;
149 and the Achaeans in strong-plated greaves rose up in a clamor,
150 making a terrible din, while two separate counsels divided.
151 All through the night we reposed on our anger and brooded on harshness
152 aimed at each other, while Zeus was preparing a stroke of destruction.
153 Come the dawning, one half of us hauled down the ships to the bright sea,
154 loading possessions aboard and the women in low-girded garments.
155 Half of the army remained and was held from the journey, continuing
156 there with the son born of Atreus, Agamemnon, shepherd of peoples.
157 Half of us entered the ships and departed; and rapidly ran they,
158 since underneath them a god smoothed over the monster-filled sea-way.
159 Coming to Tenedos, victims we offered the gods everlasting,
160 longing for home; but no thought of returning was yet in Zeus' spirit.
161 Iron of heart, he awakened a second malignant contention.
162 Some wheeled around their curving ships and returned on the sea-way,
163 those with Odysseus, war-seasoned lord of the manifold counsels,
164 once more offering favor to Agamemnon, the son born of Atreus.
165 I with the company close-gathered ships that were following after
166 fled ever onward, because I perceived a divinity scheming.
167 Tydeus' warlike son also fled and aroused his companions.
168 Late in our wake came the fair-haired hero, the lord Menelaus,
169 catching us there upon Lesbos, debating the lengthy sea-crossing:
170 whether to sail on the seaward side of precipitous Chios,
171 passing the island of Psyria, keeping Chios leftward,
172 or on the landward side of Chios, past wind-shaken Mimas.
173 And we entreated the god for a sign, and a sign he provided,
174 bidding us cut through the midst of the sea to the island Euboea,
175 thus to escape from the terrible evil with greatest rapidity.
176 Whistling behind us, a favoring wind rose up, and the vessels
177 ran very swiftly along fish-thronged passages, reaching Geraestus
178 during the night. Many thighs of bulls to Poseidon we offered,
179 laid on the fire, after measuring all of the great sea's distance.
180 It was the fourth day when Diomedes' comrades and faithful companions
181 moored all their well-balanced ships in the horse-grazing country of Argos.
182 I continued to Pylos; the wind never failed from the moment
183 godhead had first sent it forth to arise and to follow behind me.
184 Thus I came home, dear child, knowing nothing and bearing no tidings,
185 which of the Argives had safely returned and which men had been ruined.
186 All I have heard while I sit in my halls and continue as ruler,
187 you shall discover, as fitting; I shall not conceal it before you.
188 Safe home, people report, came the Myrmidons, furious spearmen,
189 led by the splendid son who was born from the great-hearted hero Achilles.
190 Safe also came Philoctetes, the glorious offspring of Poias.
191 All of his comrades Idomeneus safely conducted to Crete-land,
192 all who escaped from the war; not a man did the sea take away from him.
193 Even though distant, yourselves have heard of the son born of Atreus,
194 how he came home and how cruelly Aegisthus devised his destruction.
195 Grim was the price that the man in the end paid fully for murder.
196 How good it is when a son of the man who has perished survives him!
197 That son avenged his own father by killing the treacherous murderer,
198 crooked in counsel Aegisthus, who slaughtered his glorious father.
199 You too, friend—for I see you are handsome and tall in your stature—
200 keep yourself brave, so that people unborn may commend you hereafter.”
201 In turn replied the thoughtful and steadfast young Telemachus:
202 “Nestor, the son of great Neleus, glory renowned of Achaeans,
203 surely that son brought terrible vengeance, and all the Achaeans
204 widely shall carry his fame for the knowledge of people hereafter.
205 Would that the gods might clothe me in similar strength and endow me,
206 so I could pay back the suitors for all their oppressive transgression,
207 insolent men who against me devise every reckless aggression!
208 Yet no such happiness has been spun by the gods for our household,
209 neither for me nor my father; and now I must bear it regardless.”
210 Answering him spoke Gerenian Nestor the horseman, saying:
211 “Dear child, since you yourself have recalled this affair in your speaking,
212 word comes to me that numerous men who are courting your mother
213 fill up the halls in despite of your will and are plotting their evil.
214 Tell me, do you submit of your own free will to oppression,
215 or do the people throughout the land, at a god's prompting, hate you?
216 Who can be certain the man shall not one day return and requite their
217 violence, either alone or together with all the Achaeans?
218 Would that the gray-eyed goddess Athena might choose so to love you,
219 just as she cared in the days long ago for the glorious hero Odysseus
220 there in the country of Troy, where Achaeans endured their afflictions.
221 Never have I seen gods show love to a man so directly,
222 just as Athena the goddess stood plainly beside that Odysseus.
223 If she should choose to adore you like that and attend you in spirit,
224 more than one man would entirely lose every thought of the marriage.”
225 In turn replied the thoughtful and steadfast young Telemachus:
226 “Elder, I think no fulfillment shall ever be made of that language.
227 Far too great is the thing that you say, and amazement has seized me.
228 Never could I hope it happens, not even if gods should desire it.”
229 Once more the gray-eyed goddess Athena addressed him, saying:
230 “Young Telemachus, what kind of word has escaped from your tooth-fence?
231 Easily godhead, if willing, can bring a man safely from far lands.
232 I would prefer to endure an abundance of troubles beforehand,
233 and to return to my home and behold my appointed homecoming,
234 rather than come to my hearth and be killed, as the king Agamemnon
235 died through the treacherous schemes of Aegisthus and even his own wife.
236 Yet from the death that is common to all, not even immortals
237 can shield a man they love when the destined occasion arrives and
238 ruinous death, laying mortals low, overtakes him and holds him.”
239 In turn replied the thoughtful and steadfast young Telemachus:
240 “Mentor, no longer let us speak of him, notwithstanding our sorrow.
241 No true return any longer remains for my father; the deathless
242 gods long ago have devised his destruction and black-colored death-doom.
243 Now there is something else that I wish to inquire and discover
244 here from old Nestor, for he above others knows justice and wisdom.
245 Three generations of men, people say, he has governed as ruler;
246 therefore in looking upon him I seem to behold an immortal.
247 Nestor, the son of great Neleus, tell me the whole of the true thing:
248 how did the wide-ruling son of great Atreus die, Agamemnon?
249 Where was the king Menelaus? What death did the treacherous schemer,
250 crooked in counsel Aegisthus, devise for a man who was better?
251 Was Menelaus not there in Achaean Argos, but roving
252 somewhere among other mortals, that this man took heart and then killed him?”
253 Answering him spoke Gerenian Nestor the horseman, saying:
254 “I shall reveal to you, child, every part of the truth without error.
255 You have already imagined yourself how the story would happen,
256 if Menelaus, the fair-haired son born of Atreus, coming
257 home out of Troyland, had found that Aegisthus alive in the palace.
258 So not even a mound of earth would they have poured for the fallen;
259 carrion birds and the dogs would have torn and devoured him together,
260 lying out there on the plain, far away from the streets of the city.
261 Not one Achaean woman would mourn him; his deed was a horror.
262 We were away in the country of Troy, winning many a war-prize,
263 while in the horse-grazing country of Argos he lingered in comfort,
264 working on Agamemnon's wife all the time with his flattering language.
265 First she rejected the shameful affair and resisted the schemer,
266 radiant Clytemnestra, for sound understanding was still hers.
267 There at her side was a singer, to whom the great son born of Atreus,
268 sailing for Troy, gave strictest command to look after his consort.
269 Yet when the destiny sent by the gods finally bound her to ruin,
270 then to a desolate island Aegisthus conveyed the poor singer,
271 leaving him there to become both the spoil and the prey of the winged ones.
272 Willing, he brought the now-willing woman to live in his household.
273 Many the thighs that he burned for the gods upon sanctified altars,
274 many the gifts that he hung, woven fabric and glittering gold-work,
275 after accomplishing what in his heart he had never expected.
276 Now on our journey from Troy we had sailed in the company of each other,
277 I and the son born of Atreus, joined in affectionate friendship.
278 Yet when we came to the sacred headland of Athens at Sunium,
279 Phoebus Apollo approached with the gentle shafts of his arrows,
280 striking and killing the steersman who guided the ship of Menelaus,
281 still with the steering oar of the fast-running ship in his two hands,
282 Phrontis, the son of Onetor, who far outperformed human peoples
283 steering a ship when the violent storm winds descended upon it.
284 Thus Menelaus was held in that place, though he hastened his journey,
285 burying there his companion and giving the rites that were proper.
286 But when he too went on over wine-colored seas in the hollow
287 ships and in rapidity came to precipitous Malea's summit,
288 Zeus of the wide voice planned for him there an abhorrent road homeward,
289 pouring the breath of the screaming and turbulent winds on his vessels,
290 mountainous waves that enormously swelled on the face of the sea-way.
291 There he divided the fleet, driving part to the island of Crete-land,
292 where the Cydonians lived by Iardanus' river and waters.
293 There a sheer cliff with a smooth face descends to the salt sea below it,
294 there at the edge of the Gortynian land on the mist-covered sea-way,
295 where the South Wind drives a great wave toward the headland on leftward,
296 facing toward Phaestus, while one little rock holds the great wave back.
297 There some ships landed, and barely the men could escape from destruction,
298 though on the reef all the vessels were smashed by the waves of the sea-way.
299 Five other ships with their dark-colored prows were together transported,
300 carried by wind and the water and grounded upon the Egyptian shorelands.
301 Thus Menelaus, collecting abundant possessions and gold-work,
302 wandered by ship through the countries of people whose languages differed.
303 Meanwhile Aegisthus devised those abominable deeds in the homeland.
305 Seven whole years he reigned over Mycenae abundant in gold-work,
304 having killed Atreus' son, while the people submitted beneath him.
306 But in the eighth year noble Orestes returned as his evil,
307 home out of Athens, and killed the assassin who slaughtered his father,
308 crooked in counsel Aegisthus, who murdered his glorious father.
309 After the killing, Orestes provided the Argives a grave-feast,
310 one for his hateful mother and one for the coward Aegisthus.
311 That same day there came to him Menelaus, renowned for the war cry,
312 bringing possessions, as many as all of his vessels could carry.
313 Do not, my friend, be a long time wandering far from your household,
314 leaving possessions behind and abandoning men in your palace
315 swollen with violent arrogance, lest they devour all your substance,
316 sharing your property out and reducing your journey to nothing.
317 Go to the king Menelaus, I urge and advise you directly:
318 he has but lately returned from the foreign and faraway peoples,
319 nations from which no man in the depth of his heart could be hopeful
320 ever to come back home, once the storm winds had driven him wandering
321 into a sea so enormous not even the birds in their wing-flight
322 cross it within a whole year, so vast and so terrible is it.
323 Go with the ship you possess and the company of your companions,
324 or, if you rather would travel by land, both a chariot and horses
325 here you may take, and my sons shall accompany you on the journey
326 into the shining Lacedaemon, home of the fair-haired Menelaus.
327 Ask him yourself, so that he may reveal the infallible true word.
328 No lie shall come from his mouth, for his mind is exceedingly prudent.”
329 So he spoke, while the sun sank down and the twilight descended.
330 Gray-eyed Athena the goddess addressed the assembly among them:
331 “Elder, in all you have said you have spoken with fitting proportion.
332 Come now, cut out the tongues and prepare the wine in the mixing bowls,
333 so we may pour to Poseidon and all of the other immortals,
334 and turn our thought to our sleep, for the hour appointed is on us.
335 Light has already descended beneath the gloom of the westward;
336 sitting too long at a feast of the gods is not fitting. Let us go.”
337 She had spoken, the daughter of Zeus, and the people gave heed to her.
338 Heralds poured water to cleanse all the hands of the people assembled;
339 young men filled up the wine bowls, crowning the mixture with drinking,
340 serving to all, first pouring a portion in each cup for godhead.
341 Into the fire they cast all the tongues; then they stood and made offering.
342 And when they poured and had drunk what the heart of each person desired,
343 young Telemachus rose with the gray-eyed goddess Athena,
344 both then intending to go to the hollow-built ship for the nighttime.
345 Nestor restrained them, however, and caught them with words of insistence:
346 “Zeus and the rest of the gods who are living forever forbid it,
347 you should depart from my dwelling and go to your swift-running vessel,
348 leaving as though from a man altogether without cloak and impoverished,
349 one who possesses no cloaks or abundance of blankets within his
350 house, nor has soft bedding for either himself or the guest-friend!
351 I have an ample abundance of beautiful cloaks and of blankets.
352 Never while I am alive shall the dear son of this man Odysseus
353 lie for the night on the deck of a ship; and not afterward either,
354 while any sons I have fathered remain in the halls of my household,
355 able to welcome whatever guest-friend may come to my doorway.”
356 Once more the gray-eyed goddess Athena addressed him, saying:
357 “Well have you spoken, beloved old friend, and it now is appropriate
358 young Telemachus follows your word, for that course is far better.
359 Let him accompany you and then sleep in the halls of your household.
360 I shall return to the black-colored ship and attend to the comrades,
361 strengthening all of their spirits and giving them each their instructions.
362 I may claim to be there the only man who is elder;
363 all of the others are younger companions who follow from friendship,
364 each of them equal in age to the great-hearted young Telemachus.
365 There let me lie for the night by the hollow and black-colored vessel.
366 Yet in the morning I go to the great-hearted Cauconian people,
367 where an old debt is still owed to me, neither small nor recent.
368 Since this young man has now come to your house, you must send him hereafter,
369 riding a chariot, joined by a son of your own; and provide him
370 horses that run with the lightest of feet and are strongest in labor.”
371 So having spoken, the gray-eyed goddess Athena departed,
372 taking the form of a sea eagle; all who beheld it were startled.
373 Nestor the old man marveled at what his own eyes had discovered.
374 Taking Telemachus' hand, he spoke, and by name he addressed him:
375 “Dear friend, no fear is in me you shall ever prove coward or worthless,
376 if as a man still so young you have gods to attend and escort you.
377 This was no other of all the immortals who dwell on Olympus,
378 no, but the daughter of Zeus, most glorious Tritogeneia,
379 she who among all the Argives gave honor to noble Odysseus.
380 Queen, be propitious, and grant excellent fame and glory,
381 both upon me and my children and also my reverent bedmate.
382 I in return shall provide you a broad-browed heifer, a yearling,
383 never subdued, one no man has led underneath the constraining
384 yoke; I shall offer her, pouring the gold all around on her horn-tips.”
385 So he spoke, praying, and Pallas Athena gave ear to his prayer.
386 Leading the way for them all went Gerenian Nestor the horseman,
387 sons and the husbands of daughters, away to his beautiful household.
388 But when they came to the famous and beautiful house of the ruler,
389 down in their order they sat on the chairs and the benches surrounding.
390 For their coming the elder prepared in a bowl an abundance
391 of wine sweet to be drunk, eleven years aged in the storeroom,
392 opened for them by the stewardess, loosening seals on the vessel.
393 Some of this wine in the bowl the old man mixed and then to Athena
394 prayed for a long time, pouring to Zeus' aegis-bearing daughter.
395 And when they poured and had drunk what the heart of each person desired,
396 all of the others went homeward to sleep, each man to his own house.
397 There Gerenian Nestor the horseman provided a bedstead
398 for Telemachus, cherished son of the godlike hero Odysseus,
399 cord-strung beneath the reverberant porch at the front of the household.
400 Next to him rested Peisistratus, excellent ash-speared commander,
401 only unmarried son who remained in the halls of the household.
402 Nestor himself lay down in the inward room of the high house,
403 where the respected lady his wife made his bed and his resting.
404 When Dawn in her earliness rose with her rose-colored fingers,
405 then from his bed rose up Gerenian Nestor the horseman.
406 Out of the house he proceeded and sat on the smooth-polished stone seats
407 standing in front of the lofty and strongly constructed house door,
408 white-colored stones, as though they were polished with oil, all shining.
409 There in the days long ago Neleus sat, as a counselor godlike;
410 now he was gone to the house of great Hades, subdued by his death-doom.
411 There in his turn sat Gerenian Nestor, the guard of Achaeans,
412 holding the scepter. His sons came around him and gathered together,
413 leaving their rooms: Echephron, Stratius standing beside him,
414 Perseus, Aretus, and godlike Thrasymedes the spearman;
415 sixth of them came Peisistratus, hero, and joined the assembly.
416 Bringing the godlike young Telemachus, they seated him with them.
417 Gerenian Nestor the horseman began to address the assembly:
418 “Quickly, beloved sons, you must now bring my wish to fulfillment,
419 so I may render Athena propitious before all immortals,
420 she who appeared to me openly there at the god’s splendid banquet.
421 Come, let one go to the plain for a heifer as fast as he can go,
422 letting the herdsman who watches the cattle conduct her behind him;
423 one must descend to the black-colored ship of the great-hearted young man,
424 bringing the whole of his company here and abandoning two men;
425 one tell Laerces the goldsmith to come to the place of our feasting,
426 so he may pour out the gold all around on the horns of the heifer.
427 All of the others remain here together, and tell the slave women
428 there in the house to prepare through the glorious halls for the banquet,
429 bringing us seats and the firewood around them and radiant water.”
430 So he spoke, and at once every person was busily working.
431 Up from the plain came the heifer; and out of the swift-running vessel
432 came the companions who followed the great-hearted young Telemachus.
433 In came the smith with the brazen utensils, the means of his craftwork,
434 anvil and hammer and well-fashioned tongs that he used in the firelight,
435 all of the tools that he worked with the gold; and Athena approached them,
436 there to receive her due offering. Nestor, the old man and horseman,
437 gave him the gold, and the goldsmith hammered and poured it around the
438 horns of the heifer, so that the goddess might see and be gladdened.
439 Stratius and noble Echephron led by the horns the young heifer.
440 Out of the storeroom Aretus came with the cleansing water,
441 carried in one hand inside of a basin adorned with flower-work,
442 while in the other he carried the barley; there stood Thrasymedes,
443 steadfast in battle, with keen-edged ax in his hand for the slaughter.
444 Perseus held up the blood bowl. Then Nestor, the old man and horseman,
445 started with water for hands and the barley, and prayed to Athena
446 long, making first dedication and casting head-hairs in the firelight.

453 Then from the earth with its wide roads lifting the heifer, they held her;
454 and Peisistratus, leader of men, cut the throat of the heifer.
455 When from her body the black blood ran, and the bones lost their spirit,
456 rapidly breaking the body apart, they cut out the thighbones,
457 all in the customary way, and they wrapped them abundantly in fat,
458 doubling layers and laying the raw meat upon them in pieces.
459 Nestor burned these on split wood, the fire-colored wine on them pouring;
460 young men beside him were holding the forks that were five-pointed upward.
461 After the thighs had been thoroughly burned and the innards were tasted,
462 slicing the rest into pieces, they fastened the pieces on skewers,
463 roasted it all while they held the sharp-pointed spits in their own hands.
464 Meanwhile the beautiful Polycaste had bathed young Telemachus—
465 she was the youngest-born daughter of Nestor, the offspring of Neleus.
466 When she had bathed him and richly anointed his body with olive oil,
467 she put a beautiful mantle around him together with tunic.
468 Out of the bath he emerged in his body resembling immortals,
469 went and sat down by Nestor, the shepherd and ruler of peoples.
470 After the outermost meat had been roasted and taken from firelight,
471 down they sat and partook of the feast, while excellent men came to serve them,
472 pouring the wine for their drinking in beautiful vessels of gold-work.
473 But when desire for food and for drinking was put from their bodies,
474 Gerenian Nestor the horseman began to address the assembly:
475 “Come, my sons, and the beautiful-maned horses yoke for Telemachus,
476 leading them under the chariot, so he may make his departure.”
477 So he spoke, and the sons gave ear and at once they obeyed him.
478 Rapidly then they yoked the swift-footed horses under the wagon.
479 Bread and wine were placed in the car by the woman who managed the household,
480 also the cooked meats eaten by kings who are cherished by great Zeus.
481 Young Telemachus mounted the beautifully fashioned two-wheeled chariot;
482 close by his side came Peisistratus, Nestor's son and commander,
483 mounted the car and immediately took in his hands all the rein-straps.
484 Whipping the horses to running, they willingly flew on the roadway,
485 into the plain, leaving Pylos' precipitous city behind them.
486 All through the day they shook the yoke that was fitted between them.
487 Down went the sun at the last, and the roadways were shadowed in darkness.
488 Into Pherae they came, to the house of the lord Diocles,
489 son of Ortilochus, he whom the stream-god Alpheus fathered.
490 There they remained for the night, and the lord gave them gifts of the guest-law.
491 When Dawn in her earliness rose with her rose-colored fingers,
492 then they yoked up the horses and mounted the decorated wagon,
493 drove from the gateway and out from beneath the reverberant forecourt.
494 Whipping the horses to running, they willingly flew on the roadway.
495 Into the wheat-bearing plain they arrived, and from there they pressed toward
496 journey’s end, for the swift-footed horses conveyed them securely.
497 Down went the sun at the last, and the roadways were shadowed in darkness.
