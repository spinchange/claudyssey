# The Odyssey — Book 6

*Nausicaa at the river; the naked stranger; the road to the city.*

1 So there the steadfast and noble Odysseus rested in slumber,
2 conquered by sleep and exhaustion. Meanwhile the goddess Athena
3 went to the land and the city that held the Phaeacian people,
4 those who in earlier days made their dwelling in spacious Hypereia,
5 close to the Cyclopes, arrogant men who continually raided,
6 since the Cyclopes surpassed the Phaeacians greatly in power.
7 Godlike Nausithous uprooted and led them away from that country,
8 settling them in Scheria, far from the bread-eating peoples.
9 Round the city he drove a wall and erected the houses,
10 built the temples of gods, and divided the plowlands among them.
11 Long since, broken by destiny, he had gone to the household of Hades;
12 now Alcinous governed with counsels inspired by godhead.
13 Into his house came the gray-eyed goddess Athena,
14 there to devise the return of the great-hearted lord Odysseus.
15 Into a richly constructed chamber she entered, where one young
16 maiden was sleeping, resembling immortals in body and beauty,
17 Nausicaa, the daughter of great-hearted lord Alcinous.
18 Two slave women were sleeping beside her, with beauty from Graces,
19 each at a post of the doorway, and shining doors closed before them.
20 Like a breath of the wind she swept to the bed of the maiden,
21 stood at the head of her bed and addressed her and gave her instruction,
22 taking the bodily form of the daughter of Dymas, the famous
23 shipman, a girl of her age and exceedingly dear to her spirit.
24 Taking her likeness, the gray-eyed goddess Athena addressed her:
25 “Nausicaa, how did your mother give birth to a child so unmindful?
26 All your resplendent clothing is lying uncared for around you,
27 though your own marriage is near, when you need beautiful garments,
28 both for yourself and to give to the people who lead the procession.
29 From such possessions an excellent reputation arises
30 there among people, and both your father and honored mother are gladdened.
31 Come, let us go to the washing pools at the showing of daylight;
32 I shall accompany you and shall labor beside you, so sooner
33 you are prepared, for truly you shall not be long without marriage.
34 Already all of the best men throughout Phaeacian country
35 court you, and all of them spring from the same proud lineage as you.
36 Come then, urge your renowned father before early dawn to provide you
37 mules and a wagon, so all of the sashes and robes may be carried,
38 also the shining blankets and coverlets needing a washing.
39 This is more fitting for you as well than to travel there walking,
40 since from the city the washing pools lie at a very great distance.”
41 So having spoken, the gray-eyed goddess Athena departed
42 up to Olympus, where people declare that the seat of immortals
43 stands unshaken forever by winds and is never made wet by
44 rain; neither snow draws near, but an utterly clear upper heaven
45 spreads out unclouded around, and a radiant whiteness keeps shining.
46 There all the blessed gods take their joy every day of existence.
47 There went the gray-eyed goddess when she had advised the young maiden.
48 Straightway Dawn on her beautiful throne came upward and wakened
49 Nausicaa of the beautiful robe, who marveled at dreamland,
50 passing throughout the house to inform both her father and mother.
51 both her beloved father and mother she found in the household.
52 Mother was sitting beside the fire among serving women,
53 spinning the yarn of a violet sea hue; her father she encountered
54 there at the door, on his way to the famous rulers assembled
55 there in the council where all of the lordly Phaeacians called him.
56 Standing beside her beloved father, she spoke and addressed him:
57 “Dearest Papa, will you now make ready a wagon to carry,
58 one raised high upon excellent wheels, so the beautiful garments
59 lying around me in dirt I may take to the river for washing?
60 It is appropriate also for you, when among the first rulers
61 sitting in council, to have clean clothing around your body.
62 Five well-beloved sons have been born to you here in the palace,
63 two of them married, while three are flourishing men without marriage;
64 always they wish to have clothes newly washed and clean on their bodies
65 when they go out to the dance, and all these concerns fill my thinking.”
66 So she spoke, for she shrank from naming her marriage in fullness
67 there to her well-loved father; but he understood it and answered:
68 “Child, I do not begrudge you the mules or whatever you ask for.
69 Go, and the slaves shall make a fine wagon prepared for the journey,
70 one with a body raised high, good wheels, and a rack fitted over.”
71 So having spoken, he called to the slaves, who at once gave obedience.
72 Out in the courtyard they made the smoothly running mule wagon,
73 bringing the mules underneath and securing them under the harness.
74 Out of her chamber the maiden carried the beautiful garments,
75 placing them all in the wagon’s elaborately polished body.
76 Mother packed in a chest the food that would gladden the spirit,
77 every variety, laying delicacies and pouring wine into
78 goatskin container; and then the young maiden climbed into the wagon.
79 Mother provided the soft olive oil in a vessel of gold-work,
80 so Nausicaa and all of her serving women might use it after bathing.
81 Grasping the whip, she took up the reins that were glossy and shining,
82 striking the mules into running; and clattering loudly their hoofbeats
83 sounded, and tirelessly onward they strained, bearing clothes and the maiden,
84 not by herself, for beside her the serving women were walking.
85 And when they came to the beautiful stream of the flowing river,
86 there where the washing pools never failed and abundance of clear flow
87 welled from beneath and ran over to cleanse even garments most dirty,
88 there from the wagon they loosened the mules and removed all the harness,
89 driving them onward beside the eddying course of the river,
90 there to graze on the honey-sweet water grass; then from the wagon
91 armful by armful the garments they carried and laid in dark water,
92 stamping them briskly in washing trenches and racing each other.
93 But when they thoroughly washed every garment and cleansed it of all dirt,
94 all they spread out in order along the beach pebbles of sea-way,
95 there where the waves coming onto the shore washed cleanest the shingle.
96 After they bathed and had richly anointed their bodies with olive oil,
97 there by the side of the river they ate their meal and were waiting
98 while in the radiance sent by the sun all the garments were drying.
99 Yet when desire for food and for eating was put from their bodies,
100 casting the veils away from their heads, with a ball they were playing,
101 while white-armed Nausicaa led all the women in singing.
102 Just as the arrow-showering Artemis goes through the mountains,
103 either along high Taygetus or on Erymanthus,
104 taking delight in the boars and the deer that are swiftly running;
105 nymphs of the wilds, all daughters of Zeus who possesses the aegis,
106 play there beside her, and Leto is gladdened at heart as she watches.
107 High over all of them Artemis carries her head and her forehead,
108 easily known, although every one of the nymphs is a beauty;
109 so did the unmarried maiden surpass all the women who served her.
110 Yet when at last she intended to fold all the beautiful garments,
111 harness the mules once again, and return to the city and household,
112 then the gray-eyed goddess Athena devised a new counsel,
113 how great Odysseus might wake and behold the beautiful maiden,
114 and she might lead him at last to the city that held the Phaeacians.
115 Then the princess flung the round ball at a serving woman;
116 missing the woman, she cast it instead in a deep-whirling eddy,
117 making them all cry aloud; and illustrious lordly Odysseus
118 wakened, and sitting upright he pondered within mind and spirit:
119 “Ah me, now to the country of what mortal people have I come?
120 Are they insolent people and savage and strangers to justice,
121 or are they friendly to strangers, with god-fearing hearts in their bodies?
122 Surely the feminine cry of young maidens has sounded around me,
123 nymphs who inhabit the steep mountain peaks and the springs of the rivers,
124 also the grass-covered meadows that lie at the side of the waters.
125 Or may it be I am close to articulate human beings?
126 Come then, I shall make trial myself and discover the answer.”
127 So having spoken, from under the thicket illustrious Odysseus
128 crept, and he broke with a powerful hand from the densely grown woodland
129 one leafy branch, so he might cover his nakedness like a man should.
130 Forth he advanced like a mountain-bred lion assured of his power,
131 moving beneath the assault of the rain and the wind, while his two eyes
132 burn in his head, and he comes against cattle or sheep in the pasture,
133 even pursuing the wild deer; hunger within him commands him
134 even to enter a closely built fold and attack all the livestock.
135 So did Odysseus intend to approach the beautiful-haired girls,
136 naked although he was, since a terrible need had befallen him.
137 Dreadful he seemed to the girls, disfigured by brine of the salt sea,
138 scattering each to a different place on the jutting shore beaches.
139 Only the daughter of great Alcinous stayed, for Athena
140 planted boldness within her heart and took fear from her body.
141 Standing, she faced him and held her ground; but lordly Odysseus
142 pondered whether to clasp at the knees of the beautiful maiden,
143 or, while standing apart, to address her with honey-sweet language,
144 begging that she show the city and also provide him with clothing.
145 So, as he pondered, this seemed in his heart to be better and wiser:
146 standing apart to address her with honey-sweet words of entreaty,
147 lest, if he clasped at her knees, the maiden be angered in spirit.
148 Straightway then he addressed her with words both honeyed and cunning:
149 “Queen, I am here at your knees: are you goddess, or are you a mortal?
150 If you are one of the gods who possess the expanses of heaven,
151 Artemis, daughter of Zeus, is the goddess to whom I would match you,
152 nearest in stature and loveliness, nearest in bodily bearing.
153 But if you are one of the mortals who make the earth their dwelling,
154 three times blessed are both your father and reverend mother,
155 three times blessed are your brothers; surely the hearts in their bodies
156 always are warmed with delight and with pleasure because of your beauty,
157 watching a sapling like you step gracefully into the dancing.
158 Yet above all in his heart is that man most greatly exalted
159 who with his bridal gifts outweighs rivals and leads you homeward.
160 Never before have my eyes seen anyone equal in beauty,
161 neither a woman nor man; astonishment takes me in gazing.
162 Once on the island of Delos, beside the altar of Phoebus,
163 I saw a young palm tree rising to heaven with beautiful branches;
164 I had come there as well, and a numerous army attended,
165 following me on a journey from which many sorrows would reach me.
166 Just as in seeing the palm I marveled a long time in spirit,
167 since no such sapling had ever before from the soil arisen,
168 so, young woman, I marvel at you and am filled with amazement,
169 greatly afraid to embrace your knees, though sorrow is on me.
170 Only yesterday morning, the twentieth day, I escaped from
171 out of the wine-dark sea; all the time until then I was carried
172 onward by waves and by violent storms from the island Ogygia.
173 Now some divinity cast me ashore, perhaps to be troubled
174 even in this place; I think that my evil shall not yet be ended,
175 since before that the gods shall fulfill many more of my troubles.
176 Queen, take pity upon me; to you I have first come directly,
177 after my terrible labors, and none of the others I know of,
178 none of the people whose city and country surround me. Now show me
179 where is the town, and provide me a rag to be thrown round my body,
180 if in arriving you brought any cloth that had wrapped up the linen.
181 May all the gods give you everything dear to your heart and your longing,
182 husband and household, and may they bestow good harmony with them;
183 nothing is stronger and finer than when, being like-minded together,
184 husband and wife in the household live; many pains to their haters,
185 joys to their friends they provide, but they know their happiness best.”
186 And in her turn the white-armed Nausicaa answered Odysseus:
187 “Stranger, since you do not seem to be base or devoid of good judgment,
188 Zeus who is lord of Olympus himself gives happiness freely,
189 both to the noble and base, to each as his purpose determines.
190 He must have given you this; you must bear it, however it happens.
191 Now, since you come to our country and city, you shall not be lacking
192 either in clothing or anything else that is due to the hard-worn
193 suppliant stranger who happens to meet us. I shall show the city,
194 also shall tell you the name by which all of the people are called.
195 These are Phaeacians who hold this city and country around it;
196 I am the daughter of great-hearted lord Alcinous,
197 upon whom all Phaeacian power and strength are depending.”
198 She spoke, and called out to the beautiful-haired serving women:
199 “Stop where you are, my attendants! Why do you flee from a mortal?
200 Surely you do not imagine that this is some enemy warrior?
201 No mortal man who is living exists, nor ever shall be born,
202 who could arrive in the land of Phaeacian men bringing warfare;
203 since to the deathless gods this people is greatly beloved.
204 Far from all others we dwell in the greatly resounding salt water,
205 out at the edge of the world, and no mortal people come near us.
206 This man instead is a miserable wanderer, straying among us,
207 whom we must now tend well, since strangers and beggars are all from
208 Zeus, and even a small gift given in welcome is precious.
209 Come, my attendants, provide this stranger with eating and drinking;
210 bathe him as well in the river wherever the wind cannot reach him.”
211 So she spoke, and they halted and called to each other in answer,
212 leading Odysseus away to a place with a shelter, according
213 to the command of Nausicaa, daughter of great Alcinous.
214 There they laid down a tunic and cloak to provide him with clothing;
215 also they gave him the smooth olive oil in the golden container,
216 urging the stranger to bathe in the flowing streams of the river.
217 Then great Odysseus spoke to the beautiful serving women:
218 “Stand at a distance, attendants, while I by myself wash the sea-brine
219 there from my shoulders, and rub olive oil all around me; for truly
220 oil has been long a stranger to all of the skin of my body.
221 Yet in your presence I shall not bathe, since shame would possess me,
222 standing there naked among young women with beautiful tresses.”
223 So he spoke, and they moved off and told the young maiden his answer.
224 But in the river illustrious lordly Odysseus bathed him,
225 washing the brine from his back and his broad strong shoulders, the sea-crust
226 rubbed from his head that had come from the barren expanses of ocean.
227 Once he had washed himself thoroughly, rubbing with oil all his body,
228 also had dressed in the clothes the unmarried maiden had given,
229 him did Athena, the daughter of Zeus, make taller in stature,
230 broader to look at as well; and she caused from the head of Odysseus
231 locks to flow downward in curls like flowers of purple hyacinth.
232 Just as a skillful man pours gold around silver, a craftsman
233 taught by Hephaestus and Pallas Athena in every fine artwork,
234 artworks with manifold grace; so she poured her grace round Odysseus,
235 over his head and his shoulders. He moved off apart from the women,
236 sitting upon the sea-shingle, radiant with beauty and favor.
237 There the young maiden gazed at him long, and she spoke to her fair-haired
238 serving attendants, addressing the women who waited around her:
239 “Listen to me, white-armed serving women, to what I shall tell you.
240 Not without all of the gods who inhabit the heights of Olympus
241 willing it has this man come here among the Phaeacian people.
242 Previously, he appeared to my eyes an unworthy companion;
243 now he resembles the gods who inhabit the expanses of heaven.
244 If it were only that such a man might be called my husband,
245 and if it pleased him as well to remain in our country forever!
246 Come, my attendants, provide this stranger with eating and drinking.”
247 So she spoke, and they listened to her and readily answered,
248 setting the food and the drink in front of the lord Odysseus.
249 Truly great-hearted and much-enduring lordly Odysseus
250 ravenously drank and ate, since long he had gone without eating.
251 Meanwhile white-armed Nausicaa devised something different:
252 folding the clothes, in the beautiful wagon she placed them in order,
253 yoked the strong-hoofed mules underneath it, and mounted the wagon.
254 Urging Odysseus onward, she spoke, addressing him plainly:
255 “Rise now, stranger, and go to the city, so I may direct you
256 there to the house of my prudent father, where I can assure you
257 you shall become acquainted with all the Phaeacians' finest.
258 Only do this, since I do not believe you are lacking in judgment.
259 While we continue among the fields and the works of the people,
260 follow the mules and the wagon on foot with the serving women,
261 walking there briskly; I shall be traveling onward before you.
262 Yet when we come to the city, encircled by high walls around it,
263 there on each side of the city a beautiful harbor is lying;
264 narrow the entrance is found, while the curved ships are drawn from the water
265 up by the road, for every man has a place for his vessel.
266 There is the meeting place at the beautiful shrine of Poseidon,
267 tightly constructed of quarried stones set firmly and deeply.
268 There they attend to the black ships' tackle and all of the rigging,
269 cables and sails, and they plane all the oars until smooth for the rowing.
270 No care at all for the bows and the quivers have these Phaeacians,
271 only for masts and the oars of the ships and the balanced vessels,
272 crossing in these with delight over all of the gray salt water.
273 I must avoid their malicious talk, so that none may revile me
274 later behind me, for insolent men are abundant among them.
275 One of the baser men, meeting us there, might speak in this manner:
276 ‘Who is this stranger who follows Nausicaa, handsome and mighty?
277 Where did she find him? Surely she means him to be her husband.
278 Perhaps she attended a castaway man from a far-sailing vessel,
279 someone who came from an alien land, since no people are near us;
280 or a divinity, long entreated, descended to meet her,
281 coming from heaven in answer, and he shall possess her forever.
282 Better if she has gone elsewhere herself and discovered a husband,
283 since she dishonors the people who live in Phaeacian country,
284 though she has many excellent men among those who would court her.’
285 So they shall speak, and the gossip would make a reproach against me.
286 Even myself I would blame any maiden who acted in this way,
287 who, with her dear father and mother alive and unwilling,
288 mingled with men before she had come to the day of her marriage.
289 Stranger, give close attention to what I am telling you, so you
290 soon may secure from my father an escort and passage for homecoming.
291 Close to the roadway you shall find a beautiful grove of Athena,
292 poplars around it, a spring welling up, and a meadow surrounding.
293 There is my father's estate and his flourishing orchard around it,
294 lying as far from the city as voice of a shouting man carries.
295 Sit there and wait for a while, until we who travel before you
296 enter the city ourselves and arrive at my father's palace.
297 When you believe we have come to the palace and entered it fully,
298 then make your way to the city and ask the Phaeacian people
299 where is the palace belonging to great-hearted lord Alcinous.
300 Easily it may be known; even an infant could lead you
301 there by the hand, since none of the houses Phaeacians inhabit
302 bear any likeness to that noble house of the hero Alcinous.
303 But when the palace and courtyard have covered you there from all eyesight,
304 hurry across the great hall until you arrive at my mother.
305 Close to the fire she sits in the light and the warmth of the hearth-flame,
306 spinning a yarn of the color of sea-purple, wondrous to gaze on,
307 leaning against a great pillar, her serving women behind her.
308 Close to her there is my father's chair, reclining against hers;
309 there he sits drinking his wine every day like an immortal.
310 Pass by my father and cast both your arms round the knees of my mother,
311 if you desire to behold the day of your homecoming quickly,
312 filled with delight, even though your homeland is lying far distant.
313 If in her heart she determines to think with a kindness about you,
314 there is a hope that then you shall see all your friends and your kinfolk,
315 entering into your well-built house in the land of your fathers.”
316 So having spoken, she struck with the shining whip at the mule-team;
317 rapidly onward they left the stream of the river behind them.
318 Smoothly they trotted along, and they carried their legs well beneath them;
319 carefully she kept driving, so all of the walkers might follow,
320 serving attendants and lord Odysseus, and wisely she lashed them.
321 Down went the sun in the west, and they came to the glorious grove-land,
322 sacred to Pallas Athena. There noble Odysseus sat down;
323 straightway then he prayed to the daughter of Zeus in the open:
324 “Hear me now, child of Zeus who possesses the aegis, Unwearied.
325 Hear me now at the last, since you did not hear me before this
326 when I was broken, when the lord of the earth-shaking waters broke me.
327 Grant me a welcome of pity and friendship among the Phaeacians.”
328 So he spoke in his prayer, and Pallas Athena had heard him.
329 Still, she did not yet show herself face to face before him;
330 awe of her father's own brother restrained her, who furiously raged on
331 against Odysseus the godlike, until he arrived in his homeland.
