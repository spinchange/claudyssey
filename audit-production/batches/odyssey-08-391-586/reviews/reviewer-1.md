# Reviewer 1 — Odyssey 8.391–586

## Overall assessment

The passage is consistently strong: actions, agency, sequence, and the major
poetic effects are generally preserved, especially through the gift exchange,
Nausicaa's farewell, the wooden-horse song, and the captive-widow simile. I
found four firm but minor issues. One verse weakens an explicit directive; the
other three concern unsupported claims in the English or explanatory notes.
No fixed-wording-only formula deviations are counted here.

## Especially successful renderings

1. **8.403–405:** The sword's bronze, silver hilt, fresh-cut ivory scabbard,
   and value are all carried cleanly through a syntactically dense gift
   description.
2. **8.461–468:** Nausicaa's `ζωάγρια` is sharply retained as “the price of
   your life,” while Odysseus' answer preserves both the godlike honor he
   promises her and the direct force of `σὺ γάρ μ᾽ ἐβιώσαο`.
3. **8.523–531:** The captive-widow simile preserves its complete sequence:
   the husband's defensive death, the woman's bodily lament, the blows at her
   back and shoulders, enslavement, wasting grief, and the return to
   Odysseus' tears.

## Concerns Considered

| Lines | Confidence | Concern | Why not counted |
|---|---:|---|---|
| 8.443–445 and note | 0.63 | “For fear someone rob you” and the note's assertion that Arete assumes the crew will pilfer narrow `δηλήσεται`, which more generally means that someone may harm, despoil, or damage Odysseus or his goods. | In the immediate context Arete is telling Odysseus to secure a chest for a voyage on which he will sleep, so theft by someone aboard is a natural and probably intended specification. |
| 8.561 | 0.66 | “Swiftest of all things” turns adverbial superlative `τάχιστα` (“most swiftly/with all speed”) into an absolute comparison between the ships and every other thing. | The Phaeacian ships are being described as magical and extraordinarily swift, and the added comparison has little practical effect on the passage; material overstatement remains uncertain. |
| 8.569 | 0.61 | “Pile a great mountain round our city” may soften `ὄρος πόλει ἀμφικαλύψειν`, which can suggest covering or enveloping the city with a mountain. | `ἀμφικαλύπτω` allows surrounding or encompassing force, and “round our city” remains a defensible spatial resolution of the prophecy. |
| 8.584–586 | 0.57 | “Clear-headed and true” adds the ethical quality “true” where `πεπνυμένα εἰδῇ` more directly describes a companion who knows wise or prudent things. | “Clear-headed” carries the principal source characterization, while “true” is contextually apt for the brotherlike companion and produces only uncertain additional force. |
