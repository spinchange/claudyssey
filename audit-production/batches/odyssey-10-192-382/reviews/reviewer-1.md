# Reviewer 1

The verse translation is generally careful and often very strong across Circe's
transformation scene, Hermes' instructions, and the ensuing hospitality
type-scene. I found two localized characterization or role errors in the verse
and notes, together with two more consequential explanatory-note claims that
conflict with the poem's account of divine protection and the Calypso episode.

| R1-001 | 10.225 | `LEXICAL` | `MINOR` | 0.86 | “Closest and dearest” renders both superlatives as degrees of affection and loses Polites' explicit characterization as the most trustworthy or dependable of the companions. | `κήδιστος ... κεδνότατός τε` distinguishes “dearest” from “most trusty/dependable.” | Retain the affection in `κήδιστος` while giving `κεδνότατος` its distinct reliability sense. |
| R1-002 | 10.205 note | `ADDITION` | `MINOR` | 0.87 | Calling Eurylochus “second-in-command” converts leadership of one temporary, lot-assigned search party into a standing rank that the poem does not establish. | Odysseus divides the men into two groups, appoints a leader to each (`ἀρχὸν ... μετ' ἀμφοτέροισιν`), and says only “I led the one, and godlike Eurylochus the other” (`τῶν μὲν ἐγὼν ἦρχον, τῶν δ' Εὐρύλοχος θεοειδής`). | Describe him as leader of the second search party, or mark any broader deputy status as inference rather than fact. |
| R1-003 | 10.297 and 10.343 notes | `MISTRANSLATION` | `MODERATE` | 0.98 | Both notes falsely turn Odysseus' Book 5 oath-demand into a negotiation preceding sex with Calypso: “the poem's second goddess-bed negotiated on the same contract language as the first” and “Twice a goddess invites him to bed; twice he requires the great oath first.” At 5.178–179 the oath safeguards Calypso's proposed raft and voyage, not a bed invitation; the Circe scene is the only one of the pair where sex is the immediate bargain. | Here Hermes explicitly links Circe's bed to the oath (`εὐνηθῆναι` at 296; `ἀλλὰ κέλεσθαί μιν ... ὅρκον ὀμόσσαι` at 299), and Circe's invitation at 333–335 prompts Odysseus' demand at 343–344. The reused oath language does not make the earlier Book 5 context a bed negotiation. | Keep the accurate verbal recurrence, but identify its different function in Book 5: protection against a suspected trap in the departure plan. |
| R1-004 | 10.304 note | `MISTRANSLATION` | `MODERATE` | 0.97 | The note's “Immunity, not counter-magic; the gods' gift is a property of the man by the time it matters” contradicts Hermes' explicit causal explanation. The narrative need not dramatize ingestion for the source to identify the counter-agent. | Hermes gives `τόδε φάρμακον ἐσθλόν` to carry into Circe's house (287–288), then says she will be unable to enchant him because `οὐ γὰρ ἐάσει / φάρμακον ἐσθλόν, ὅ τοι δώσω` (291–292), “the good drug I shall give you will not allow it.” | Preserve the interesting absence of a staged ingestion, but describe moly as the divinely supplied protective counter-drug the poem explicitly makes causal. |

Counts by severity: 0 `CRITICAL`, 0 `MAJOR`, 2 `MODERATE`, 2 `MINOR` — 4 firm findings total.

Especially successful renderings:

1. Lines 236–243 preserve both the purpose of Circe's drug and the cruel split
   between porcine body and unchanged human mind, with the feed catalogue intact.
2. Lines 287–301 render Hermes' detailed sequence of potion, wand, sword-rush,
   bed, oath, and threatened unmanning with unusually clear causal and tactical
   structure.
3. Lines 348–372 keep the four handmaids' differentiated tasks and then move
   cleanly into the recurring bath and table-service type-scene.

## Concerns Considered

| Lines | Confidence | Concern | Why not counted |

| 10.234 note | 0.68 | The note specifies “grated cheese,” while this passage names cheese but does not state that it is grated. | Grating is explicit in the closely compared *Iliad* mixture and may be intended as contextual culinary shorthand; the added preparation detail does not materially alter this scene. |
| 10.277 note | 0.70 | “The only god who assists in all the wanderings” appears overbroad, since divine Circe herself soon assists Odysseus and his men, among other divine helpers across the larger journey. | “The wanderings” may be intended narrowly as the mortal-danger episodes of the *apologoi*, and the sentence's scope is not clear enough for a firm factual finding. |
| 10.296 | 0.67 | “Invite you to her bed” softens `κελήσεται εὐνηθῆναι`, which more literally says she will bid or tell him to lie with her. | Circe is predicted to act from fear and supplication, and “invite” retains the operative proposed action; the local register loss is too uncertain to count. |
