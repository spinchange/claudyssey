# Reviewer 2

## Overall assessment

The verse translation is highly reliable across three very different episodes,
preserving the Aeolian sailing mechanics, the Laestrygonian harbor trap, and the
physical detail of the stag hunt. I found two firm issues, both in explanatory
notes: one converts a simile into unsupported genealogy, and one supplies an
unsupported estimate of the destroyed crews.

| ID | Lines | Category | Severity | Confidence | Finding | Greek evidence | Suggested direction |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---|---|---|
| R2-001 | 10.120 note | `MISTRANSLATION` | `MINOR` | 0.94 | The note calls the Laestrygonians a third appearance of “the Giants' bloodline,” says the Cyclopes and Phaeacians are both kin to the Giants, and makes the Phaeacians a civilized branch of that family. The verse gives only a comparison of appearance or scale, not Laestrygonian descent. The cited Book 7 passage groups Phaeacians, Cyclopes, and Giants by their closeness to the gods; it does not make the Phaeacians and Cyclopes descendants or collateral kin of the Giants. | `οὐκ ἄνδρεσσιν ἐοικότες, ἀλλὰ Γίγασιν` (“not resembling men, but Giants”) is explicitly a resemblance construction. | Describe the recurring comparison or association with the Giants without asserting a single bloodline or genealogical branch. |
| R2-002 | 10.124 note | `ADDITION` | `MINOR` | 0.86 | “Some five hundred men” supplies a crew total that the episode never gives. Eleven destroyed ships are securely inferable, but neither this passage nor the preceding fleet description states roughly fifty men per ship. The estimate adds unsupported numerical scale to an otherwise accurate account of the poem's largest fleet loss. | Lines 10.91-95 and 10.132 distinguish Odysseus' one ship from all the others; 9.159 establishes twelve ships, but no crew complement is stated. | Keep the supported count—eleven ships and their crews—or explicitly label any numerical manpower estimate as an external conjecture. |

Counts by severity: **2 MINOR**; **0 MODERATE**; **0 MAJOR**; **0 CRITICAL**.

Especially successful renderings:

1. **10.19-27** makes the bag's construction, the bound wind-courses, Aeolus'
   delegated power, the silver lashing, and the released West Wind exact and
   easy to follow.
2. **10.87-132** renders the harbor geometry and its tactical consequences with
   exceptional clarity, through the narrow mouth, trapped ships, thrown stones,
   cut cables, and lone escape.
3. **10.156-180** preserves the stag hunt as a continuous physical sequence,
   including the heat-driven descent to water, spine-shot, fathom-long rope,
   neck carry, spear used as a staff, and the companions' uncovering.

## Concerns Considered

| Lines | Confidence | Concern | Why not counted |

| 10.72 note | 0.68 | “He is not wrong” risks endorsing Aeolus' plural claim that the blessed gods hate Odysseus, although the note's own evidence establishes only Poseidon's hostility and other gods favor Odysseus. | The phrase can be read more narrowly: Aeolus correctly detects real divine opposition, even though his collective formulation is excessive. |
| 10.152 | 0.69 | “Whether to go and look” replaces `ἐλθεῖν ἠδὲ πυθέσθαι`, “to go and inquire/learn,” with visual inspection. | Both actions serve the same reconnaissance purpose, and line 155 immediately restores the explicit plan to send men “to learn.” |
| 10.118 | 0.59 | “Raised the war-cry” is more specifically martial than `τεῦχε βοήν`, which can mean that Antiphates raised a shout or alarm through the city. | The call immediately musters the city for a coordinated attack, so “war-cry” is contextually defensible and does not alter the action. |
