# Reviewer 1

## Overall assessment

This is a highly successful and generally faithful rendering of *Odyssey* 12.227–453. The Scylla passage, the sacrificial portents, and the final return to Charybdis preserve difficult syntax and narrative timing with unusual clarity. I find three material issues: one duration/aspect error in the translated verse and two overstatements in the explanatory notes. Fixed-register wording differences have not been counted.

## Especially successful renderings

1. **12.244–250:** “And that was when” sharply preserves `τόφρα`, making the crew's fixation on Charybdis simultaneous with Scylla's attack; the lifted limbs, direct naming of Odysseus, and `τότε γʼ ὕστατον` all remain intact.
2. **12.394–396:** The portents are rendered with exemplary precision: the crawling hides, the lowing flesh, and the distinction between roasted and raw meat all survive without explanatory inflation in the verse.
3. **12.429–444:** The return to Charybdis remains spatially and temporally coherent across the fig-tree suspension, the civic judge simile, the delayed reappearance of the timbers, and Odysseus' hand-rowing escape.

## Concerns Considered

| 227 and 226 note | 0.70 | The verse-and-note phrasing treats Circe's instruction as an explicit order “not to arm” or “don't fight,” whereas `οὔ τί μʼ ἀνώγει θωρήσσεσθαι` strictly says that she did not tell Odysseus to arm. | Circe's substantive advice is that Scylla cannot be fought and that flight is best, so the English may be defensible pragmatic compression; the materiality of converting that advice into an explicit prohibition is uncertain. |
| 261–265 and 264 note | 0.69 | “He hears the cattle before he sees land” is not established by the Greek: the crew has already “come to” the island (`ἐς ... νῆσον / ἱκόμεθʼ`) before Odysseus, still aboard, hears the herds. | The sequence clearly places the sound before disembarkation, and epic “arrival” can describe approach; whether sight necessarily precedes the sound remains unstated. |
| 443 | 0.65 | “Beyond the long timbers” may displace `παρὲξ περιμήκεα δοῦρα`, which more naturally places Odysseus beside or close by the timbers when he falls. | `παρέξ` is spatially flexible, and the immediate next line has him mount and row the timbers, limiting any material narrative consequence. |
