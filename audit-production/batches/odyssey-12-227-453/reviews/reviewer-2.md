# Reviewer 2 — Odyssey 12.227–453

## Overall assessment

The translation is notably controlled through the Scylla loss, Thrinacian
sacrilege, divine reprisal, shipwreck, and second passage by Charybdis. I found
five firm fidelity issues. Two affect the verse: “sleeplessness” replaces the
men's explicit drowsiness or need for sleep, and “Six days after that” changes
a six-day duration into a point six days later. Three explanatory-note claims
overstate the narrative evidence: hearing the cattle before seeing land, a
“third” fatal sleep, and Odysseus' purported eyewitness status for everything
outside the Helios council. No fixed-wording-only formula deviation is counted.

| R2-001 | 12.260–266 note | `ADDITION` | `MINOR` | 0.82 | The note says Odysseus hears the cattle “before he sees land” and builds its “sound first” interpretation on that sequence. | R2-002 | 12.279–283 | `LEXICAL` | `MINOR` | 0.94 | “Worn down with labor and sleeplessness” replaces the men's explicit sleepiness or drowsiness with lack of sleep. The Calypso–Hermes chain remains distinctive, but it does not make the rest of the *Apologoi* uniformly eyewitness testimony. | At 12.338 Odysseus is asleep; 12.339–365 narrates the plan and sacrifice in his absence; and only at 12.366–370 does he wake, approach the ship, and smell the fat. ## Especially successful renderings

1. **12.234–259** preserves the alternating mechanics of Charybdis, the crew's
   diverted gaze, Scylla's six-man seizure, the angler simile, the men's final
   appeal, and Odysseus' superlative grief with unusually clear momentum.
2. **12.343–396** carries Eurylochus' grim calculus through the improvised
   sacrificial substitutions, Odysseus' waking recognition, Helios' demand,
   Zeus' settlement, and the animate portents without confusing ritual
   sequence or divine agency.
3. **12.403–446** renders the ship's mechanical breakup, the steersman's death,
   the crew's destruction, Odysseus' two-timber survival craft, and his timed
   escape from Charybdis with precise physical continuity.

## Concerns Considered

| 12.251–254 note | 0.63 | Calling the ox horn a professional “line-guard” may resolve uncertain ancient fishing tackle too specifically; the horn has also been understood as part of the hook or lure assembly. | The Greek explicitly supplies an ox horn in the fishing rig but does not explain its exact function. The note's technical identification is plausible, and the simile's action and scale remain accurate. |
| 12.295 | 0.61 | “Some power was plotting evils” may weaken `δαίμων` from an unidentified divine agent to a more general force. | In this marked context “power” readily suggests supernatural agency, and the translation preserves both the uncertainty of identification and the active plotting. |
| 12.445 note | 0.68 | “The Father's single direct intervention in all the wanderings” is literally too broad, since this passage itself attributes the storm, cloud, and thunderbolt directly to Zeus. | The surrounding language calls the present act “mercy,” so “intervention” can reasonably be understood as “direct saving intervention.” Under that contextual restriction, the claim is defensible rather than a firm poem-wide exclusivity error. |
