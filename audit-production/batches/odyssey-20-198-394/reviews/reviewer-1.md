# Reviewer 1 — Odyssey 20.198–394

The translation is vivid and generally faithful, particularly in Philoetius’ loyalty, Telemachus’ confrontation, and Theoclymenus’ vision. Eight issues merit revision: one moderate semantic distortion and seven localized explanatory-note errors. Fixed-wording-only formula deviations are excluded.

## Findings

| ID | Lines | Category | Severity | Confidence | Finding | Greek evidence | Suggested direction |
|---|---:|---|---|---:|---|---|---|
| R1-001 | 20.230, 253, 284, 387 notes | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.99 | Four notes incorrectly place the loyalists’ redemption, the battle, Athena’s expenditure of Odysseus’ rage, or the bow contest “tomorrow.” Books 20–22 form one continuous day: the contest and slaughter occur later on the day that dawns at 20.91. | No new dawn intervenes in Books 21–22; the next dawn is mentioned only at 23.241. | Replace “tomorrow” with “later today,” “later that day,” or another non-day-shifting expression. |
| R1-002 | 20.284 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.98 | Calling Athena’s goad “the third day running” again mistakes narrative or book divisions for elapsed days. The cited verbatim goad occurs in Book 18 and here on the following morning, not across three days. | The registered recurrence is 18.346–348 = 20.284–286; Books 18–19 occupy the preceding day and night. | Call this the second occurrence, across two consecutive narrative days. |
| R1-003 | 20.287 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.99 | The note calls Ctesippus “the poem’s last new suitor,” but Agelaus is introduced later in this same passage. | `ὀψὲ δὲ δὴ μετέειπε Δαμαστορίδης Ἀγέλαος` at 20.321 introduces Agelaus by name and patronymic. | Remove “last,” or describe Ctesippus simply as a newly introduced suitor. |
| R1-004 | 20.334 note | ADDITION | MINOR | 0.97 | Saying Agelaus’ premise is false “by about thirty feet” invents a spatial measurement absent from the poem. Odysseus is nearby in the hall, but no distance is supplied. | Odysseus is seated `ἐντὸς ... μεγάρου, παρὰ λάϊνον οὐδόν` (257–259); no measurement separates him from Agelaus. | Use “false with Odysseus nearby” or “false within the same hall.” |
| R1-005 | 20.349 and note | MISTRANSLATION | MODERATE | 0.96 | “The heart in them wanted to wail” changes ominous foreknowledge into a present emotional desire. The Greek says their spirit foreboded or imagined lamentation, sustaining the passage’s prophetic disclosure of their future. The note repeats and builds upon the altered sense. | `γόον δʼ ὠΐετο θυμός`: their spirit thought of, expected, or foreboded lamentation. | Render the predictive force directly: “their hearts foreboded lamentation” or equivalent. |
| R1-006 | 20.351 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.91 | The note claims that the dark-moon festival gives the vanished sun “naturalism” because “the sun has indeed gone out.” An ordinary dark or new moon does not extinguish daylight; the Greek presents supernatural prophetic darkness, not a normal lunar-phase effect. | The vision says `ἠέλιος ... οὐρανοῦ ἐξαπόλωλε, κακὴ δʼ ἐπιδέδρομεν ἀχλύς` (356–357), the sun has vanished and an evil mist overspread everything. | Preserve the dark-moon association as symbolism, without presenting the solar darkness as a natural consequence. |
| R1-007 | 20.371 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.90 | “He exits the poem here (but for the aftermath)” implies a later aftermath appearance by Theoclymenus, but 20.372 is his final occurrence in the poem. | After `ἵκετο δʼ ἐς Πείραιον` at 20.372, Theoclymenus does not reappear in Books 21–24. | Remove “but for the aftermath”; say that he exits the poem here. |
| R1-008 | 20.376 note | ADDITION | MINOR | 0.98 | The note says Odysseus had strung his bow “before any of [the suitors] was born.” The poem supplies neither the suitors’ ages nor a date establishing that absolute priority. | Lines 376–379 attack the beggar’s usefulness and strength; they provide no biographical chronology for the suitors or Odysseus’ earlier use of the bow. | Say that Odysseus mastered the bow long before the present contest, without making an unsupported birth-priority claim. |

## Especially Successful Renderings

- **20.198–239:** Philoetius’ grief, practical dilemma, and conditional declaration of strength remain distinct and dramatically effective.
- **20.257–319:** Telemachus’ tactical seating of Odysseus and escalating defense of household order preserve agency, threat, and the father–son verbal echo.
- **20.350–370:** Apart from line 349’s lead-in, Theoclymenus’ vision and exit speech render the accumulating darkness, blood, ghosts, and inescapable doom with close semantic control.

## Concerns Considered

| Lines | Confidence | Concern | Why not counted |
|---|---:|---|---|
| 20.204 note | 0.66 | The assertion that Philoetius’ body unconsciously recognizes Odysseus supplies an unstated psychological mechanism. | The beggar explicitly prompts his physical grief and remembrance of Odysseus, making unconscious dramatic recognition a plausible interpretation. |
| 20.211 note | 0.65 | “All of it requisitioned” may make the suitors’ demand for cattle exhaustively universal. | The cattle are treated collectively, and generic and exhaustive readings remain viable under the rubric’s quantifier rule. |
| 20.242 note | 0.67 | Calling the Book 15 hawk-and-dove and the present eagle-and-dove “the same sign” compresses differences in species and auspicious side. | The shared raptor-with-dove structure and common relevance to Telemachus support a deliberate thematic recurrence. |
| 20.275 | 0.68 | After singular Antinous speaks, “but he took no heed of his words” can momentarily make Antinous appear to ignore himself rather than Telemachus ignore him. | The contrast and surrounding exchange allow Telemachus to be recovered naturally as the intended subject. |
| 20.358 note | 0.66 | Saying Athena’s possession-laughter has “subsided into ordinary mockery” asserts that her mental disturbance has ended. | The narrator does not mark its cessation, but the return to ordinary verbal ridicule makes the tonal interpretation plausible. |
| 20.364 note | 0.69 | The claim that the suitors have “refused” Zeus’ thunder overstates their participation in an omen directed to Odysseus and interpreted by the mill-woman. | The clear-sky thunder was publicly audible and can be included rhetorically among the day’s ignored divine warnings. |
