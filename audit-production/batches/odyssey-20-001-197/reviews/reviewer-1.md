# Reviewer 1 — Odyssey 20.1–197

The translation is consistently vivid and generally faithful, especially in Odysseus’ self-command, Athena’s reassurance, and the morning household sequence. Five localized semantic issues merit revision. No fixed-wording-only formula deviations are counted.

## Findings

| ID | Lines | Category | Severity | Confidence | Finding | Greek evidence | Suggested direction |
|---|---:|---|---|---:|---|---|---|
| R1-001 | 20.66 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.99 | The note calls the Pandareus material a second tale “in two nights,” but the nightingale passage in Book 19 and this prayer belong to the same continuous night: Penelope speaks before sleeping, wakes here, and Dawn arrives only at 20.91. The book boundary does not mark another night. | Book 19’s nightingale speech precedes Penelope’s sleep; 20.1 resumes Odysseus lying in the fore-hall, and `χρυσόθρονος ἤλυθεν Ἠώς` does not occur until 20.91. | Say that the two Pandareus stories occur during the same night, on opposite sides of Penelope’s sleep. |
| R1-002 | 20.77–78 | LEXICAL | MINOR | 0.91 | “Snatching-winds” collapses the source’s deliberate change from impersonal storm-winds to the personified Harpies. The attached note identifies the Harpies, but the verse still obscures their mythological agency in handing the girls to the Furies. | Line 66 has `θύελλαι`, “storm-winds,” whereas line 77 changes to `ἅρπυιαι`, “Harpies”: `ἅρπυιαι ἀνηρείψαντο / καί ῥʼ ἔδοσαν ... ἐρινύσιν`. | Name the Harpies in line 77 while preserving the sonic connection with the earlier storm-winds. |
| R1-003 | 20.116 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.96 | Calling the mill-woman “the frailest slave in the house” improperly enlarges the superlative’s comparison class. She is the frailest of the twelve women operating the mills, not explicitly of the entire household staff. | `τῇσιν δώδεκα πᾶσαι ... γυναῖκες` introduces twelve mill-women (107), followed by `ἡ δὲ μίʼ ... ἀφαυροτάτη` (110), “the one … was the frailest.” | Limit the description to “the frailest of the twelve mill-women.” |
| R1-004 | 20.120–121 and note | GRAMMAR | MINOR | 0.94 | “He told himself the guilty would pay” suppresses Odysseus as the agent of punishment. The Greek says that he believed he would exact vengeance upon the wrongdoers, not merely that punishment would somehow befall them. The note repeats the agentless “ledger” framing. | `φάτο γὰρ τίσασθαι ἀλείτας`: the understood subject of middle `τίσασθαι` is Odysseus—he thought he would punish or take vengeance on the offenders. | Retain Odysseus’ agency: “for he believed he would punish the guilty” or equivalent. |
| R1-005 | 20.173 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.99 | “Melanthius’ third abuse … in three days” mistakes book divisions for elapsed days. His Book 17 encounter occurs on the day continuing through Books 18–19; the present encounter follows the next morning. The incidents therefore span two consecutive days, not three. | Books 18–19 continue the day begun in Book 17; 20.1 remains in the ensuing night, and Dawn arrives at 20.91 before Melanthius appears. | Describe this as another or third act of abuse across two consecutive days, without assigning one day to each intervening book. |

## Especially Successful Renderings

- **20.18–30:** The self-address preserves the canine wordplay, Odysseus’ disciplined endurance, and the grotesquely comic paunch simile without flattening their tonal complexity.
- **20.33–53:** Athena and Odysseus’ exchange retains both his strategic anxiety and her raid-scaled, characteristically Odyssean reassurance.
- **20.128–171:** The morning household sequence renders Eurycleia’s operative instructions clearly and preserves the moral force of the suitors’ `ἀτάσθαλα` as “reckless things.”

## Concerns Considered

| Lines | Confidence | Concern | Why not counted |
|---|---:|---|---|
| 20.2–4 note | 0.62 | “Every layer of the bedding is stolen livestock” extends the suitors’ expressly stated slaughter of the sheep to the oxhide and potentially the mantle. | The wider depletion of Odysseus’ estate makes the indictment plausible, and the prose can be read as rhetorical synthesis rather than exact provenance. |
| 20.14 and note | 0.60 | “Soft-boned pups” adds an anatomical image not explicit in `ἀμαλῇσι`, “tender/weak.” | The compound idiomatically conveys the pups’ softness and vulnerability, so the net semantic distortion is uncertain. |
| 20.66 note | 0.58 | “Annulled in an hour” supplies an unsupported time unit. | Here “an hour” reads naturally as a rhetorical minimizer for the short interval marked by `τόφρα`, rather than measured chronology. |
| 20.116 note | 0.68 | “Zeus thunders for her” may imply that the thunder answers the mill-woman, although Zeus thunders before she speaks and explicitly hears Odysseus’ prayer. | The thunder and her words jointly fulfill Odysseus’ requested double omen, allowing “for her” to mean divine validation rather than temporal response. |
| 20.129 note | 0.63 | The note presents Telemachus’ criticism of Penelope as deliberate camouflage laid over genuine grievance, although the narrator does not state that motive. | Concealment is plausible because Telemachus knows the stranger’s identity, but the psychological mixture remains interpretive rather than demonstrably false. |
| 20.156 note | 0.68 | The housekeeper’s generic `πᾶσιν ἑορτή` is described as an explicit Apollo festival on the dark of the moon. | Apollo is not named locally, but the later gathering in his grove and the lunar-transition oath provide substantial contextual support, leaving the inference’s materiality uncertain. |
