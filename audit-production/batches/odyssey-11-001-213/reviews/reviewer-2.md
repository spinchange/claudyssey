# Reviewer 2 — Odyssey 11.1–213

## Overall assessment

The translation closely tracks the voyage to Ocean, the necromantic rite,
Elpenor's plea, Tiresias' prophecy, and Anticleia's account. I found three firm
minor issues: a false modern-name etymology in a note, an incorrect line-distance
claim about the two oars, and a civic title shifted from judging to lawmaking.
No larger distortion of action, agency, ritual sequence, or Underworld
psychology emerged.

| ID | Lines | Category | Severity | Confidence | Finding | Greek evidence | Suggested direction |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---|---|---|
| R2-001 | 11.14 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.90 | The note says that Crimea “carries” the Cimmerians' name. Although ancient writers located Cimmerians around the northern Black Sea and called the strait the Cimmerian Bosporus, the modern name *Crimea* derives through Crimean Tatar *Qırım*, not from *Cimmerian*. The geographical association is defensible; the etymological claim is not. | The Greek identifies `Κιμμερίων ἀνδρῶν` and their dark land but makes no claim about the name of Crimea. | Retain the history of locating Cimmerians around the Black Sea, but remove the parenthetical etymology or replace it with the historically named Cimmerian Bosporus. |
| R2-002 | 11.77 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.99 | The note says that the oar image returns “thirty lines later” in Tiresias' prophecy. Elpenor's oar is at 11.77–78 and Tiresias' oar journey begins at 11.121, a gap of roughly forty-three line numbers, not thirty. | `ἐρετμόν` occurs in Elpenor's request at 11.77 and the next oar is `ἐυῆρες ἐρετμόν` at 11.121 (with further mentions at 11.125 and 11.129). | Replace “thirty lines later” with “roughly forty lines later” or cite 11.77 and 11.121 directly. |
| R2-003 | 11.185–186 | LEXICAL | MINOR | 0.88 | “Lawgiving man” turns `δικασπόλος` into a legislator. The Greek term identifies a judge or dispenser of justice, a concrete civic role relevant to the public banquets Telemachus attends. | `δαῖτας ἐίσας / δαίνυται, ἃς ἐπέοικε δικασπόλον ἄνδρʼ ἀλεγύνειν`: the compound `δικασπόλος` concerns administering judgments or justice, not making laws. | Use “a man who judges,” “a justice-giving man,” or a contextually clear equivalent such as “a ruler.” |

## Especially successful renderings

1. **11.23–50** repeats Circe's ritual instructions with disciplined accuracy,
   preserving the pit, libations, barley, vows, slaughter, arrival of the dead,
   prayers, sword, blood, and Tiresias-first restriction.
2. **11.100–137** renders Tiresias' long prophecy clearly while retaining its
   conditionals, agents, concrete sacrificial animals, and the ambiguity
   surrounding `ἐξ ἁλός`.
3. **11.197–208** keeps Anticleia's exclusions and true cause of death sharply
   ordered, then carries the three failed embraces and escalating grief with
   both semantic precision and emotional force.
