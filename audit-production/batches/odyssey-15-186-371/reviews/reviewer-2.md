# Reviewer 2 Report

## Overall assessment

This is a strong, controlled passage overall. The translation preserves the major narrative sequence, speaker relationships, recurrent diction, and changes of pace across the Pylos departure, Theoclymenus’ arrival, Telemachus’ voyage, and Eumaeus’ family history. I found one firm local error: at 15.277–278, the English syntax reverses who is supplicating whom. The remaining items below are ambiguities or interpretive risks rather than firm errors.

## Findings

| ID | Lines | Category | Severity | Confidence | Finding | Greek evidence | Suggested direction |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---|---|---|
| R2-001 | 15.277–278 | GRAMMAR | MODERATE | 0.99 | “Since in my flight I have found you / begging” makes Telemachus the one who is begging, although Theoclymenus says that he himself fled and supplicated Telemachus. This reverses the agency in the appeal and obscures the reason Telemachus should take him aboard. | `ἐπεί σε φυγὼν ἱκέτευσα, / μή με κατακτείνωσι` — “since, having fled, I supplicated you, lest they kill me.” | Make Theoclymenus the explicit suppliant: for example, “since I fled and came to you as a suppliant, lest they kill me.” |

## Concerns

| Lines | Confidence | Concern |
|---|---:|---|
| 15.228–230 | 0.72 | In “Neleus ... who held his vast estate by force,” the nearest grammatical antecedent can make “his” refer to Neleus, suggesting that he forcibly held his own property. The Greek `ὅς οἱ χρήματα πολλὰ ... εἶχε βίῃ` makes the possessions Melampus’. Explicitly naming Melampus would remove the ambiguity. |
| Note L272 | 0.66 | Calling Theoclymenus a “precise mirror” of Odysseus’ Book 13 Cretan disguise seems somewhat stronger than the correspondence supports. Both are dangerous strangers received under pressure, but Theoclymenus openly identifies himself as a fugitive killer, whereas Eumaeus is not given that exact identity in the Cretan tale. “A pointed parallel” would be safer. |
| 15.361–362 | 0.62 | “Ask for her and after her” is difficult to parse and may imply asking other people about Anticleia. The Greek `μεταλλῆσαι καὶ ἐρέσθαι` can describe questioning or inquiring of/about her, and the construction is interpretively uncertain; a less syntactically strained phrasing would help. |
| 15.367–370; Note L363 | 0.70 | “They gave her in marriage to Same” and the note’s “married to Same” can make the island sound like the bridegroom. Greek uses the pregnant construction `Σάμηνδʼ ἔδοσαν`: they gave Ctimene in marriage and sent her to Same. In addition, “the lady ... she dressed me” leaves Anticleia’s return as subject slightly vulnerable to confusion with Ctimene. Naming the destination and Anticleia explicitly would clarify both points. |

## Successful passages

1. **15.195–220:** Telemachus’ tactful attempt to avoid Nestor and Peisistratus’ amused cooperation are rendered with clear speech relations and an effective comic sense of Nestor’s overwhelming hospitality.
2. **15.282–300:** The departure and sailing sequence is lucid and well paced; the route, offerings, wind, and shift to the Ithacan ambush remain easy to follow without flattening the nautical texture.
3. **15.301–345:** Odysseus’ second test of Eumaeus and the swineherd’s answer preserve the disguised king’s probing strategy, Eumaeus’ loyalty, and the thematic force of service, appetite, and necessity.
