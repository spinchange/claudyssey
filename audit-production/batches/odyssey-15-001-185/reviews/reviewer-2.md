# Reviewer 2

## Overall assessment

This is a strong and generally attentive rendering of the opening movement of Book 15. It handles Athena's urgent instructions, Menelaus's measured hospitality, the gift sequence, and the departure omen with sustained clarity. The firm issues are limited to one consequential loss of Penelope's agency in Athena's warning, one calibrated number expansion, and two demonstrably false claims in the notes.

| R2-001 | 15.19 | GRAMMAR | MODERATE | 0.95 | “Take care nothing is carried from the house” turns Athena's warning into an agentless passive. In the Greek, Penelope is the understood subject who may carry a possession away for herself. Suppressing her agency materially softens the specifically gendered accusation that motivates the following generalization about a remarried woman's loyalties. | `μή νύ τι σεῦ ἀέκητι δόμων ἐκ κτῆμα φέρηται`: middle `φέρηται` has Penelope understood as agent and `κτῆμα` as its object—“lest she carry some possession out of the house against your will.” | Restore the agent: “lest she carry some possession from the house against your will,” or equivalent wording that explicitly makes Penelope the potential taker. |
| R2-002 | 15.31–32 | ADDITION | MINOR | 0.99 | “More than one” converts the singular indefinite `τινα` into an explicit plural prediction. This is a semantic scope expansion, not merely a fixed-formula wording difference: Athena says that the earth will cover one or some one of the suitors before the ambush succeeds. | `πρὶν καί τινα γαῖα καθέξει / ἀνδρῶν μνηστήρων` (“before the earth covers even one/some one of the suitor-men”). | Use “one of the suitor-men,” “some suitor-man,” or another singular indefinite. |
| R2-003 | 15.113–119, note L113 | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 1.00 | The note says that Sidon returns “fifty lines later” in the nurse's story. The Phoenicians first enter that story at 15.415, 302 line numbers after 15.113, and the nurse names Sidon at 15.425, 312 line numbers later. The literary connection is sound, but the stated interval is not. | The note itself cites the nurse's Sidonian account at 15.425–429; `Σιδῶνος` occurs at 15.425. | Replace “fifty lines later” with “roughly three hundred lines later,” or omit the numerical interval. |
| R2-004 | 15.180–181, note L180 | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.99 | The note conflates two different relationships when it calls this an echo of Telemachus's “promise to the disguised Athena” and then identifies 8.467 as its counterpart. The exact line at 8.467 is spoken by Odysseus to Nausicaa; Athena is neither speaker nor addressee. Telemachus offers disguised Athena a guest-gift in Book 1, but does not use this prayer-as-to-a-god formula there. | `τῷ κέν τοι καὶ κεῖθι θεῷ ὣς εὐχετοῴμην` occurs verbatim at 8.467 (Odysseus to Nausicaa) and 15.181 (Telemachus to Helen). | Identify this simply as Telemachus's exact reuse of Odysseus's farewell promise to Nausicaa; remove the attribution to disguised Athena. |

## Especially successful renderings

1. **15.7–42:** Telemachus's sleeplessness and Athena's speech retain their escalating logic—from household danger, through the ambush warning, to the precise route via Eumaeus—without sacrificing momentum.
2. **15.68–85:** Menelaus's maxims of measured hospitality preserve the antitheses between excessive love and hate, expelling and detaining, and cherishing and speeding, before pivoting naturally into the comic-generous gift tour.
3. **15.92–159:** The preparation of the meal, descent to the treasure chamber, presentation and stowing of the gifts, second meal, libation, and farewell unfold in a lucid ritual sequence with agency consistently clear.

| 15.16–18, note L16 | 0.63 | The note may overstate the case when it treats Athena's report as no more than strategic exaggeration and says Penelope's behavior contradicts it. Penelope later says that her parents press her hard to marry (19.158), and her resistance does not itself disprove pressure from them. | The note qualifies its judgment (“at best”), and its narrower point—that no other passage independently confirms pressure specifically to choose Eurymachus—remains defensible. |
| 15.46 | 0.68 | “Strong-hoofed” replaces the concrete force of `μώνυχας`, conventionally “single-hoofed,” “solid-hoofed,” or “uncloven-hoofed.” | The word is a conventional equine epithet, “strong-hoofed” retains a plausible heroic force, and the anatomical distinction has little consequence in this scene. |
| 15.105–108, note L104 | 0.62 | “Worked all over with figures” may be more pictorially specific than `παμποίκιλοι` and `ποικίλμασιν`, which securely indicate richly variegated patterning or embroidery but do not require representational figures. The Iliadic parallel does depict battle scenes, but this robe's designs are not described. | English “figures” can also denote ornamental forms or patterns, leaving a non-pictorial reading available. |
| 15.160–178, note L160 | 0.72 | “The goose fattened in the house,” repeated as a “fattened domestic bird” in the note, narrows `ἀτιταλλομένην` from a goose reared, tended, or nurtured in the house to one deliberately made fat. | The earlier `ἥμερον` and domestic setting establish a tame, provisioned bird, so “fattened” is a plausible contextual inference and does not materially alter Helen's omen-reading. |
