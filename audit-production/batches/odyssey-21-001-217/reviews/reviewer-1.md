# Reviewer 1 — Odyssey 21.1–217

The translation is generally strong and attentive to narrative agency, material detail, and rhetorical structure. I found seven firm issues: two moderate participant/subject shifts, one repeated concrete-object error, and four factual overstatements in the notes.

## Findings

| ID | Lines | Category | Severity | Confidence | Finding | Greek evidence | Suggested direction |
|---|---:|---|---|---:|---|---|---|
| R1-001 | 21.48 note | ADDITION | MINOR | 0.99 | “Twenty years shut” asserts that the storeroom remained continuously unopened throughout Odysseus’ absence. The Greek only narrates Penelope opening its doors now; it supplies no duration since their previous opening. | `θυρέων δʼ ἀνέκοπτεν ὀχῆας ... πετάσθησαν δέ οἱ ὦκα` describes the present unlocking and opening without a temporal measure. | Remove the exact duration, or attach the twenty-year wait to the bow rather than to an allegedly unopened room. |
| R1-002 | 21.98 note; cf. 21.418 | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.99 | The note says the idiom `γεύσεσθαι`, “taste,” returns at 418 and calls that payoff two hundred lines away. Line 418 instead has `πειρήσεσθαι`, “try/experience,” and lies 320 lines after line 98. The repeated English “taste” is a translator-created echo, not a repeated Greek idiom. | 98 `ὀϊστοῦ ... γεύσεσθαι`; 418 `τῶν τάχʼ ἔμελλον Ἀχαιοὶ πειρήσεσθαι`. | Describe the English as creating an echo across different Greek verbs, and correct or omit the line-distance claim. |
| R1-003 | 21.122–123 and note | GRAMMAR | MODERATE | 0.99 | “Every man / who watched … who had never seen it before” attaches the never-seeing to the spectators. In Greek, the spectators are plural, while singular `ὀπώπει` resumes Telemachus: everyone marvels that he arranged the axes properly although he himself had never seen the setup before. The note’s claim that both readings apply reinforces the false attachment. | Plural `πάντας ἰδόντας` denotes all the viewers; singular `πάρος δʼ οὐ πώ ποτʼ ὀπώπει` returns to Telemachus. | Make Telemachus the explicit subject: “Amazement took all who saw how neatly he set them, though he himself had never seen them before.” |
| R1-004 | 21.131–133 | GRAMMAR | MODERATE | 0.99 | “Defend a man” turns Telemachus into the protector of a third party. The middle `ἀπαμύνασθαι` with the accusative means to ward a man off from oneself or defend oneself against him. | `ἄνδρʼ ἀπαμύνασθαι, ὅτε τις πρότερος χαλεπήνῃ` means to repel a man when someone first attacks or grows hostile. | Use “defend myself against a man,” “hold my own against a man,” or “ward a man off when he attacks first.” |
| R1-005 | 21.138, 165 | LEXICAL | MINOR | 0.99 | The swift arrow is leaned against the bow’s tip, not a “door-hook.” The error recurs when Leodes puts the equipment down. | In this bow-and-arrow construction `καλῇ ... κορώνῃ` is the curved bow-tip on which the string is fitted; the door panels have already been supplied by `σανίδεσσιν`. | Render “against the fine bow-tip” or “against the bow’s fair horn” in both occurrences. |
| R1-006 | 21.141 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.98 | The note calls the slaughter “tomorrow’s” and says it follows roughly the same rotation as the trial. The slaughter begins later on this same festival day, and the sequences do not share their starting point: Leodes tries first, whereas Antinous dies first. | `ἑξείης ἐπιδέξια ... ἀρξάμενοι` establishes the trial order, and 144 explicitly makes Leodes first. | Say that Antinous ritualizes the trial order but later becomes the first victim on the same day; omit the claim of a repeated rotation. |
| R1-007 | 21.209 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.97 | The note turns Odysseus’ limited report of prayers he has personally heard among the slaves into a complete “census of loyalty on his whole estate.” The Greek neither surveys the entire estate nor defines an exhaustive list of loyal people. | `οἴοισι δμώων` limits “alone” to the slaves, while `οὔ τευ ἄκουσα` expressly limits the evidence to what Odysseus has heard. | Preserve both limits: these are the only two slaves whom he has heard praying for his return, not a complete estate-wide loyalty census. |

## Especially Successful Renderings

1. Lines 13–41 handle the bow’s complicated pedigree unusually well, preserving the distinct searches of Odysseus and Iphitus, Heracles’ violation of guest-friendship, and the bow’s status as a domestic memorial rather than Trojan war gear.
2. Lines 73–79 state the contest precisely: ease in stringing, passage through all twelve axes, Penelope’s departure from her marital house, and the lingering dream-memory all remain intact.
3. Lines 193–216 preserve the cautious hypothetical recruitment, the binary loyalty test, the loyalists’ parallel prayers, Odysseus’ recognition of their true intent, and the concrete terms of his promised reward.

## Concerns Considered

| Lines | Confidence | Concern | Why not counted |
|---|---:|---|---|
| 21.51 | 0.61 | “High floor” broadens `ὑψηλῆς σανίδος`, more specifically a high plank or platform. | “Floor” can denote elevated flooring, and the spatial function remains intelligible. |
| 21.55 note | 0.62 | Calling the bow “the third time” an object has stood in for Odysseus is difficult to reconcile with the parenthetical sequence, which names his clothes before it and the bed still to come. | The numbering may be intended as a loose thematic inventory rather than a strict chronological ordinal. |
| 21.150 | 0.67 | “Long before that” adds substantial temporal or quantitative force to simple `πρίν`: Leodes’ hands tire before he strings the bow, but the Greek does not say they fail long beforehand. | The added degree sharpens his weakness only slightly and does not change the causal sequence. |
| 21.177, 182 | 0.70 | `δίφρος` is a stool or chair, while “bench” normally denotes a longer, multi-person seat. | Both are low supports for the fleece beside the fire, so the scene’s action and function remain unchanged. |
| 21.214 note | 0.70 | “The only manumission scene in Homer” and “adoption” make the promised rewards into formal changes of legal and familial status that the Greek does not expressly name. | Wives, property, separate houses, and treatment as Telemachus’ brothers can reasonably support an inferred emancipation and incorporation into the household, though neither is explicit. |
