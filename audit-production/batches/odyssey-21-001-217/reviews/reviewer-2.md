# Reviewer 2 — Semantic Review

The translation is generally accurate, vivid, and attentive to the Greek’s syntax and narrative movement. The firm problems are concentrated in the commentary: several notes assert unsupported chronology, enumeration, or social consequences. The main translation has one clear semantic-role error at 21.132–133. No fixed-wording-only formula deviations are counted.

| R2-001 | 21.42–50 note | ADDITION | MINOR | 0.96 | The note calls the storeroom “Twenty years shut.” The bow has remained home during Odysseus’ absence, but these lines do not say that the room itself has gone unopened for twenty years. | Penelope reaches the room and opens its bolts, but `θάλαμον ... θυρέων δʼ ἀνέκοπτεν ὀχῆας` supplies no duration since its previous opening. | Remove the exact duration or attach the twenty years to the bow’s waiting in the house, not to the room’s being continuously shut. |
| R2-002 | 21.55–56 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.90 | The note calls this “the third time an object has stood in for the man,” but its own sequence is the clothes in Book 19, the bow here, and the bed “to come.” Within that stated sequence, the bow is second, not third. | Here Penelope lays the bow-case on her knees and weeps (`φίλοις ἐπὶ γούνασι θεῖσα, / κλαῖε`); the other comparanda cited by the note fall before and after this scene. | Call the bow the second object in the stated sequence, or omit the ordinal. |
| R2-003 | 21.113–117 note | MISTRANSLATION | MODERATE | 0.98 | The note reverses Telemachus’ stated logic by saying that if he strings the bow, “she need not leave.” He actually says that her departure would no longer grieve him because he would remain behind capable of taking up his father’s gear. | `οὔ κέ μοι ἀχνυμένῳ ... μήτηρ / λείποι ... ὅτʼ ἐγὼ κατόπισθε λιποίμην / οἷός τʼ ἤδη...`: his competence removes his grief at her leaving; it does not cancel her departure. | Recast the motive as readiness to remain and assume his father’s place even if Penelope leaves. |
| R2-004 | 21.120–123 note | GRAMMAR | MINOR | 0.97 | The note manufactures an ambiguity over who “had never seen it before” and answers “Both,” allowing the spectators to be the subject. The singular verb points to Telemachus: despite never having seen the axes set up, he arranges them expertly. | `πάρος δʼ οὐ πώ ποτʼ ὀπώπει` has a third-person singular subject, whereas the spectators are plural in `πάντας ἰδόντας`. | Attribute the prior non-observation solely to Telemachus; retain the spectators only as those amazed by his work. |
| R2-005 | 21.132–133 | GRAMMAR | MINOR | 0.98 | “Cannot trust my hands / to defend a man” reverses the accusative man’s role, making him the person Telemachus protects. Telemachus says he cannot trust himself to repel or withstand a man who attacks first. The associated note repeats the misleading wording. | `χερσὶ πέποιθα / ἄνδρʼ ἀπαμύνασθαι, ὅτε τις πρότερος χαλεπήνῃ`: `ἄνδρα` is the hostile man to be warded off. | Use “to fight off a man,” “to repel an attacker,” or “to defend myself when a man attacks first.” |
| R2-006 | 21.141 note; 21.209 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.99 | Both notes move the suitors’ deaths to “tomorrow.” The bow trials, Odysseus’ disclosure to the herdsmen, and the slaughter form one continuous same-day sequence. The further claim that the deaths follow “roughly the same rotation” is also unsupported by the trial order. | Antinous orders the men to proceed `ἑξείης` in the present contest; Odysseus’ disclosure at 207–217 occurs during that same contest, shortly before the attack, with no intervening night. | Replace “tomorrow” with “later today” or “shortly,” and avoid claiming that the death order reproduces the bow-trial rotation. |
| R2-007 | 21.209–211 note | ADDITION | MINOR | 0.88 | The note turns Odysseus’ report of whom he has heard praying for his return into a “complete” census of loyalty across his “whole estate.” Prayer heard during the disguise is not identical with exhaustive loyalty, and the proposed census omits other loyal dependants disclosed later. | Odysseus limits his evidence to desire and audible prayer: `σφῶϊν ἐελδομένοισιν` and `οὔ τευ ἄκουσα / εὐξαμένου`. | Describe this as the loyalty Odysseus has personally verified at this point, not a complete estate-wide census. |
| R2-008 | 21.212–216 note | ADDITION | MODERATE | 0.94 | The note presents the promise as formal “manumission” and “adoption.” The Greek promises wives, property, nearby houses, and honored standing as Telemachus’ companions and brothers, but contains no explicit act of freeing or legal adoption. | `ἄξομαι ... ἀλόχους`, `κτήματʼ ὀπάσσω`, `οἰκία ... τετυγμένα`, and `ἑτάρω τε κασιγνήτω τε ἔσεσθον` enumerate benefits and relational status without language of emancipation or adoption. | Present manumission as a possible implication and “brothers” as elevated or quasi-kin status; do not state formal emancipation or adoption as textual fact. |

## Especially Successful Renderings

1. **21.18–24:** The intertwined accounting of three hundred stolen sheep, twelve missing mares, their mule offspring, and Iphitus’ resulting doom remains exceptionally clear without flattening the Greek’s sequence.
2. **21.75–79:** “Whoever strings the bow most easily” preserves the decisive force of `ῥηΐτατα`, while the rendering of Penelope’s marital house and dream-memory keeps the emotional cost of the contest present.
3. **21.124–129:** Telemachus’ three failed pulls, near-success on the fourth, and Odysseus’ silent headshake are rendered with precise pacing and agency.
