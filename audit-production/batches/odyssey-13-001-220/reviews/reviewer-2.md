# Reviewer 2 — Fidelity Review of *Odyssey* 13.1–220

The translation is consistently strong in narrative sequence, imagery, and tonal control. I find five material issues: one moderate grammatical relationship error in Zeus' reply to Poseidon, two localized semantic weakenings in the verse, and two materially inaccurate claims in the explanatory notes. I have not counted fixed-register wording differences, and the three lower-confidence cases are separated below.

| ID | Lines | Category | Severity | Confidence | Finding | Greek evidence | Suggested direction |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---|---|---|
| R2-001 | 13.42 note | `MISTRANSLATION` | `MINOR` | 0.95 | The note says that Odysseus' wife is “named at last” here and that the reticence of 7.225 is repealed only when the ship is waiting. But Odysseus had already spoken to this same Phaeacian audience of his “wedded wife” at 11.177, and Penelope is explicitly named at 11.446. Moreover, 13.42 itself again says only “wife,” not Penelope. The attractive claim of a first disclosure is therefore false. | At 13.42 Homer has only `ἀμύμονα ... ἄκοιτιν`; compare Odysseus' `μνηστῆς ἀλόχου` at 11.177 and `κούρη Ἰκαρίοιο, περίφρων Πηνελόπεια` at 11.446. | Describe this as a renewed, unembedded farewell acknowledgment of his wife, not her first mention or naming before the Phaeacians. |
| R2-002 | 13.47–48 | `MISTRANSLATION` | `MINOR` | 0.91 | “Urged” weakens the Phaeacians' operative direction that the guest be sent home. Their approval is already supplied by the preceding verb, so the second verb should retain the distinct force of bidding or ordering the dispatch. | `ἐπῄνεον ἠδʼ ἐκέλευον / πεμπέμεναι τὸν ξεῖνον`: they approved and `ἐκέλευον` that the guest be sent. | Use “bade,” “directed,” or another wording that preserves the operative force alongside their approval. |
| R2-003 | 13.143–144 | `GRAMMAR` | `MODERATE` | 0.98 | “Yielding to his own force and power” changes the relation in Zeus' argument. The mortal yields to Poseidon in force and might—that is, he is manifestly Poseidon's inferior—yet fails to honor him. The English instead makes the man yield to his own power, suggesting self-subjection to pride and removing Zeus' pointed contrast between mortal weakness and divine superiority. | In `τίς σε βίῃ καὶ κάρτεϊ εἴκων`, `σε` is the person to whom `τίς` yields in `βίῃ καὶ κάρτεϊ`; `οὔ τι τίει` then supplies the paradoxical failure to honor Poseidon. | Restore the second-person relation, for example: “if any man, yielding to you in force and might, fails to honor you.” |

Only the two-line ethical taxonomy at 13.201–202 recurs in both earlier scenes. The opening “whose country” question at 13.200 repeats 6.119 but has no counterpart at 9.175–176. | 13.200–202 equals 6.119–121 as a three-line unit; 9.175–176 corresponds only to 13.201–202 and 6.120–121. | State that the two-line taxonomy recurs verbatim three times, while the preceding country-question recurs only in Books 6 and 13. |

## Especially Successful Renderings

1. **13.31–35:** The plowman's `λιλαίεται` becomes the forceful “aches for his supper,” while the welcome sunset and buckling knees preserve the simile's physical exhaustion and Odysseus' impatience without flattening the rural detail.
2. **13.79–92:** “Unwaking, sweetest sleep, and most like death” closely preserves the remarkable sequence `νήγρετος, ἥδιστος, θανάτῳ ἄγχιστα ἐοικώς`; the horse-team and falcon comparisons then carry the ship's speed cleanly around the sleeping passenger.
3. **13.128–138:** Poseidon's honor grievance, his explicit concession that he never wholly denied the homecoming, and the exact inventory and Troy comparison of Odysseus' gifts are all kept lucid through a syntactically difficult divine speech.
