# Reviewer 2 — Odyssey 10.383–574

## Overall assessment

This is a strong rendering of the restoration, the year on Aeaea, Circe's
Underworld instructions, and Elpenor's death. I found three firm localized
issues: one explanatory note assigns the Cyclops deaths partly to an event that
occurred afterward; the rendering and note on `δαιμόνιε` turn a forceful
vocative into literal possession; and a vocative epithet for Odysseus is
grammatically attached to Tiresias in English.

| R2-001 | 10.431–437 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.89 | The note says that “the six dead in the cave were the price of one man's curiosity and boasting.” Odysseus' boasting occurs only after the surviving party has escaped the cave; it helps provoke Poseidon's later persecution but cannot have caused the six earlier cave deaths. The larger point that Eurylochus blames Odysseus' recklessness remains valid, so the error is limited. | Eurylochus attributes the companions' deaths to Odysseus' `ἀτασθαλίῃσιν` (10.437), referring to the men who entered the Cyclops' yard with him (10.435–436). These six had already been eaten before Odysseus disclosed his name from the departing ship in Book 9. | Attribute the six cave deaths to Odysseus' decision to enter and remain in the cave; discuss the boast separately as the cause of Poseidon's subsequent vendetta. |
| R2-002 | 10.472 and note | LEXICAL | MODERATE | 0.90 | “You are possessed!” concretizes the exclamatory vocative `δαιμόνιε` as supernatural seizure, and the note then builds an extended interpretation on that asserted possession. In Homeric address the word can rebuke someone as strange, misguided, or extraordinary without asserting that a divinity possesses them. Here the crew urgently recalls Odysseus to his homecoming; literal possession is a consequential narrowing and addition. | `δαιμόνιʼ` is a vocative of forceful or astonished address, followed directly by the operative reminder `ἤδη νῦν μιμνήσκεο πατρίδος αἴης`; no clause states that Odysseus is divinely seized or enchanted. | Use a rebuking address such as “Strange man!” or “Madman!” and present the lotus/possession comparison in the note as an interpretive analogy, not the lexical content of `δαιμόνιε`. |
| R2-003 | 10.538 | GRAMMAR | MINOR | 0.96 | English word order makes “leader of peoples” an apposition to “the seer,” so it characterizes Tiresias. In Greek `ὄρχαμε λαῶν` is vocative and addresses Odysseus. | `ἔνθα τοι αὐτίκα μάντις ἐλεύσεται, ὄρχαμε λαῶν`: `τοι` addresses Odysseus, `μάντις` is the nominative subject, and `ὄρχαμε` is morphologically vocative. | Reorder to make the address explicit: “Then, leader of peoples, the seer will quickly come to you.” |

## Especially successful renderings

1. **10.383–399** preserves both Odysseus' ethical refusal to eat and the full
   sequence of restoration, recognition, weeping, and Circe's pity.
2. **10.490–540** handles a long series of navigational and ritual imperatives
   with exceptional clarity while retaining concrete details such as the
   confluence, cubit-square pit, triple libation, future vows, victims, and
   control of access to the blood.
3. **10.551–560** accurately and economically renders Elpenor's unheroic death,
   including his youth, lack of martial and mental steadiness, drunken
   isolation, forgotten ladder, broken neck, and descent to Hades.
