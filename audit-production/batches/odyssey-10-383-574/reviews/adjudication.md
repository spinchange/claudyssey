# Adjudication Report

Reviewer 1 proposed five findings and Reviewer 2 proposed three. The proposals
about literal possession at 10.472 and the misattached vocative at 10.538 are
duplicate pairs and are merged. Six distinct findings are sustained: the
10.431-437 note makes a later boast partly responsible for earlier deaths;
“blind folly” weakens culpable recklessness; the 10.438 note assigns a specific
marital relationship not established by the poem; the verse and note at
10.467-472 turn emphatic reproach into divine possession; the 10.495 note
overstates the dead's universal inability to think or speak without blood; and
the word order at 10.538 transfers Odysseus' vocative epithet to Tiresias. No
fixed-wording-only formula deviation is counted.

## Especially Successful Renderings

- **10.383-399:** Odysseus' principled refusal to eat and the companions'
  restoration, recognition, embrace, weeping, and Circe's pity remain exact
  and emotionally legible.
- **10.508-540:** Circe's dense navigational and ritual instructions preserve
  the confluence, pit, ordered libations, vows, victims, orientation, burning,
  prayer, and controlled access to the blood.
- **10.551-560:** Elpenor's anti-heroic death retains the full causal sequence:
  youth and instability, drunken isolation, sudden waking, forgotten ladder,
  headlong fall, broken neck, and descent to Hades.

## Calibration Implications

Chronological claims in interpretive notes must separate consequences that
precede an action from later consequences caused by it. The calibrated moral
force of the `ἀτασθαλίαι` family remains important inside direct speech even
when a note supplies the stronger gloss. General affinity terms do not license
a precise family-tree assignment. Emphatic Homeric vocatives should not be
literalized into supernatural propositions, especially when a note then builds
its interpretation on that literalization. Claims about underworld rules
should be tested against the ensuing narrative's exceptions. Finally,
morphological vocatives must remain attached to their addressee even when
English word order would otherwise make them appositional.
