# Adjudication: *Odyssey* 2.1-217

Both reviewers independently identified the same material issue at 2.53-54. The proposal is sustained: the English assigns the dative of favor to Penelope, while this passage keeps Icarius as the relevant chooser and recipient of pleasingness. No formula-only register findings are counted in this semantic adjudication.

| O02-001-217-A-001 | 53-54 | GRAMMAR | MODERATE | 0.88 | Sustained from R1-001 and R2-001 | "Whoever comes and pleases her" changes the local marriage-agency structure. In this clause, Icarius is the one who would set the bride-terms and give his daughter to the man he wished, one pleasing to him; the translation instead anticipates the later 2.114 construction where Penelope's preference is explicit. | `ὥς κʼ αὐτὸς ἐεδνώσαιτο θύγατρα, / δοίη δʼ ᾧ κʼ ἐθέλοι καί οἱ κεχαρισμένος ἔλθοι`; `αὐτός`, `ἐθέλοι`, and `οἱ` point back to Icarius. At 2.114, by contrast, the Greek uses `ἁνδάνει αὐτῇ` for "pleases her." | Revise line 54 to keep Icarius as the chooser, e.g. "and give her to whom he wished, whoever came pleasing to him." |

## Rejected Or Unresolved Proposals

| Proposal | Decision | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| R1 concern, note 14 | Rejected | "No one has sat there in twenty years" is an explanatory inference from Telemachus sitting in Odysseus' seat and the elders yielding. The verse does not state it directly, but the note's point about Odysseus' public place is contextually defensible and not a material mistranslation. |
| R1 concern, note 15 / lines 17-24 | Rejected | The note's "not knowing his son Antiphus is already dead" concerns Ithacan ignorance of the Cyclops episode, not Aegyptius' emotional state. The Greek explicitly says he has not forgotten Antiphus and grieves him, and the translation preserves that grief at 23-24. |
| R1 concern, lines 190-191 | Rejected | "For all these words" narrows `εἵνεκα τῶνδε`, but Eurymachus is threatening Halitherses for using words to stir Telemachus toward anger. The local context makes the compression defensible. |
| R2 concern, line 153 | Rejected | The Greek more naturally presents the eagles clawing their own cheeks and necks, while "each other's" makes the action reciprocal. The distinction is real, but the omen remains a violent paired sign and the note itself supplies "self-tearing"; the net material loss stays below the firm-finding threshold. |
