# Reviewer 1 — *Odyssey* 13.221–440

## Overall assessment

The translation is consistently strong, especially in the Cretan tale, the exchange of cunning between Athena and Odysseus, the Ithacan recognition scene, and the clause-by-clause beggar transformation. I find three firm, localized fidelity problems, all minor: two explanatory notes make demonstrably inaccurate cross-passage claims, and the verse twice turns Athena's indefinite singular prediction about one suitor into an explicit prediction about multiple suitors. Fixed-wording-only formula differences are excluded from the count.

## Especially successful renderings

1. **13.228–249:** Odysseus' cautious supplication and Athena's geographic description preserve the speech acts, island/mainland alternatives, Ithaca's roughness, resources, and far-reaching fame with clear syntactic control.
2. **13.256–310:** The improvised Cretan tale and Athena's delighted answer sustain the linked vocabulary of concealment, guile, counsel, and advantage across a long exchange without losing the fabricated narrative's concrete sequence.
3. **13.429–438:** Athena's promised transformation is executed clause by clause—withered skin, lost hair, aged hide, dimmed eyes, smoke-stained clothing, deer hide, staff, and torn pouch—with unusually close correspondence to the Greek.

## Concerns Considered

| 13.255, 296–299 | 0.68 | Repeated “profit” may narrow the cunning, shrewd devices, and advantageous counsel in `πολυκερδέα`, `κέρδεα`, and `κέρδεσιν` toward commercial gain. | The gain-sense belongs to the Greek word-family, and the surrounding “master-thief,” “tricks,” “guile,” “cheating,” “counsel,” and `μήτις` language strongly restores the strategic-cunning sense. |
| 13.287–289 note | 0.70 | Calling the adult-woman appearance “Athena as she is” may overstate a true-form epiphany: `δέμας δʼ ἤϊκτο γυναικί` says her form was likened to a beautiful, tall, craft-skilled woman. | The transformation functions as a self-revelation, and the craft-skilled female form is strongly Athena-coded; whether the shorthand materially misstates the scene remains uncertain. |
| 13.370 note | 0.66 | “The last stone set at a door this size was the Cyclops'” asserts comparable size although the present Greek merely says Athena set `λίθον` at the cave doors. | A stone closing a cavern entrance plausibly suggests substantial size, and the door-and-stone wording does support an echo; only the exact scale equivalence is unsupported. |
