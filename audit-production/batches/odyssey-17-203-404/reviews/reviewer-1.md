# Reviewer 1 Audit — Odyssey 17.203–404

## Overall assessment

Overall, the verse is exceptionally faithful, especially through the Melanthius encounter, the Argus episode, and the hall speeches. The firm issues are confined to three explanatory notes that add unsupported causal, chronological, or character-knowledge claims.

## Findings

| ID | Lines | Category | Severity | Confidence | Finding | Greek evidence | Suggested direction |
|---|---:|---|---|---:|---|---|---|
| R1-001 | Note to 297–299 | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.93 | The note says the manure represents “work not done because the master is gone” and “suspended labor.” The Greek assigns neglect caused by the master’s absence to Argus, not to manure removal; the dung is deliberately accumulated for the slaves to cart to the estate as fertilizer. | At 296, `ἀπόθεστος ἀποιχομένοιο ἄνακτος` describes the dog as neglected while his master is absent. Lines 297–299 separately describe dung heaped up `ὄφρʼ ἂν ἄγοιεν / δμῶες ... κοπρήσοντες`, for or until the slaves carry it away to manure the estate. | Keep the contrast between Argus’ neglect and the dung’s productive agricultural use; remove the claim that manure work has stopped because Odysseus is absent. |
| R1-002 | Note to 326–327 | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.99 | Calling Argus “the first creature in Ithaca to complete a recognition” ignores Telemachus’ completed recognition of Odysseus in Book 16. Argus is the first member of the palace household to recognize the disguised Odysseus on his return there, not the first creature anywhere in Ithaca. | At 16.187–219, Odysseus identifies himself, Telemachus accepts him as his father, embraces him, and weeps with him. Argus’ recognition at 17.301–327 occurs afterward. | Narrow the priority claim, for example: “the first creature at the palace to recognize the disguised king.” |
| R1-003 | Note to 339–341 | ADDITION | MINOR | 0.95 | The note turns the narrator’s architectural description into Odysseus’ focalized memory and asserts that “he knows the man who trued that post.” The Greek names no carpenter and supplies no indication that Odysseus personally knows him. | `ὅν ποτε τέκτων / ξέσσεν ἐπισταμένως καὶ ἐπὶ στάθμην ἴθυνεν` says only that “a carpenter once planed [the post] skillfully and trued it to the line.” `τέκτων` is indefinite, and the narrator remains the grammatical voice. | Retain the threshold’s careful craftsmanship and possible resonance for the returning owner, but remove the claim of personal acquaintance with its carpenter. |

## Especially successful renderings

1. Lines 203–211 reproduce the fountain’s civic and sacred landscape with close control of construction, water movement, grove, and altar.
2. Lines 233–246 preserve the sequence from Melanthius’ kick through Odysseus’ violent deliberation and restraint to Eumaeus’ prayer, including the shifting agency and emotional force.
3. Lines 291–327 render the Argus episode with excellent fidelity: the dog’s former hunting ability, present neglect, physical recognition, Odysseus’ concealed tear, and the timing of Argus’ death all remain clear.

## Concerns Considered

| Lines | Confidence | Concern | Why not counted |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Note to 212 | 0.70 | Calling Melanthius and Melantho “the disloyal family” may overgeneralize, since their father Dolius and his other sons later welcome and fight beside Odysseus. | The note may be using “family” loosely for the explicitly named disloyal sibling pair rather than classifying the whole house of Dolius. |
| Note to 286–289 | 0.67 | Adding “commerce” to the belly-driven catalogue goes beyond ships explicitly described as carrying evil to enemies, which directly evokes raiding or war. | “Belly” may be interpreted broadly as material appetite underlying seafaring, and the note’s central claims about raiding, war, and the Trojan expedition remain well grounded. |
| 366 and note | 0.62 | “As if he had begged all his life” makes `πάλαι`, “for a long time” or “long since,” into a lifetime universal. | “All his life” can function idiomatically for long-established experience, and the simile’s essential point—Odysseus’ completely convincing performance—survives. |
