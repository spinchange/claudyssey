# Reviewer 2 — *Odyssey* 17.203–404

## Overall assessment

The translation is consistently vivid and generally faithful, especially in narrative sequence, characterization, and rhetorical force. I found four firm but minor issues: two unsupported semantic expansions, one displaced insult, and one duplicated-word error. No issue reaches moderate severity.

## Findings

| ID | Lines | Category | Severity | Confidence | Finding | Greek evidence | Suggested direction |
|---|---:|---|---|---:|---|---|---|
| R2-001 | 220, 377 and note L220 | LEXICAL | MINOR | 0.90 | “Licker of feast-plates” turns the hostile `ἀπολυμαντήρ`—a spoiler or ruiner of feasts—into someone who merely cleans plates by eating scraps. “Beggar-pest” nearby preserves some nuisance value, but not the destructive force; the note also incorrectly presents the English phrase as the lexeme’s meaning. | `πτωχὸν ἀνιηρόν δαιτῶν ἀπολυμαντῆρα` (220); repeated at 377. | Use “spoiler/ruiner of feasts” or another insult retaining active depredation, identically at both occurrences; revise the note’s gloss. |
| R2-002 | 320–323 and note L320 | GRAMMAR | MINOR | 0.99 | “The day the day of slavery” contains an accidental duplicate that corrupts the sentence; the same duplication is reproduced in the note’s quotation. The Greek has only one temporal phrase. | `εὖτʼ ἄν μιν κατὰ δούλιον ἦμαρ ἕλῃσιν`—“when the day of slavery seizes him.” | Delete the second “the day” in both verse and note. |
| R2-003 | 339–341 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.98 | The verse correctly places Odysseus “inside the doors,” but the note contradicts both it and the Greek by calling his station “neither in nor out.” The threshold is liminal symbolically, but its stated physical location is inside. | `ἐπὶ μελίνου οὐδοῦ ἔντοσθε θυράων`—on the ash threshold, inside the doors. | Preserve the symbolic threshold reading while stating that Odysseus sits just inside the doors. |
| R2-004 | 366 and note L366 | ADDITION | MINOR | 0.97 | “As if he had begged all his life” makes Odysseus’ apparent experience lifelong. `πάλαι` says that he seemed to have been a beggar for a long time, not necessarily from birth; the note repeats and builds upon the unsupported universal. | `ὡς εἰ πτωχὸς πάλαι εἴη`—as if he had long been a beggar. | Use “as if he had long been a beggar” or “as if he had begged for years,” and temper the note accordingly. |

## Especially successful renderings

1. Lines 233–239 preserve the kick, Odysseus’ two violent alternatives, and his deliberate self-restraint with unusually clear agency and sequence.
2. Lines 286–289 carry the “belly” argument from immediate hunger through ship-fitting and war without losing its widening causal structure.
3. Lines 381–387 render Eumaeus’ distinction between an uninvited beggar and invited skilled workers accurately, including the seer, healer, carpenter, and singer.
