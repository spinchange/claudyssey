# Reviewer 2 Report

## Overall assessment

This is a vivid and generally faithful rendering of Telemachus' arrival, the hut conversation, Athena's transformation of Odysseus, and the father-son recognition. It is especially strong in sustained dialogue and emotional staging. I found five localized fidelity problems: one possessive construction obscures and naturally reverses the central genealogy, one verse adds an unsupported Cretan detail, and three notes make false cross-passage claims. Fixed-wording-only formula differences are excluded.

| R2-001 | Note L13 | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.99 | The note invokes “the fallen distaff of Book 4” as a parallel shock gesture, but no distaff falls in Book 4. Helen's golden distaff is brought in and lies across the yarn basket; it is not dropped in response to emotion. | At 4.125–135, Phylo brings the basket and `ἠλακάτη ... ἐπέκειτο`, “the distaff lay upon it.” Nothing there corresponds to the vessels falling from Eumaeus' hands at 16.13. | Remove the Book 4 comparison or replace it with an actual Homeric recognition or shock gesture. |
| R2-002 | 16.62 | ADDITION | MINOR | 0.92 | “Crete of the wide dancing-grounds” adds dancing grounds absent from the Greek. The source calls Crete broad or far-extending; it supplies no dance imagery here. | `ἐκ μὲν Κρητάων γένος εὔχεται εὐρειάων` — “he claims his descent from broad Crete.” | Preserve the spatial epithet without adding a cultural feature: “He claims his birth from broad Crete.” |
| R2-003 | 16.118–120 | GRAMMAR | MODERATE | 0.97 | “Arceisius fathered Laertes ... / his father got Odysseus” makes the natural antecedent of “his” Laertes, so Arceisius appears to father both Laertes and Odysseus. The Greek gives Arceisius → Laertes → Odysseus → Telemachus. Because this genealogy explains Telemachus' isolation, the possessive ambiguity damages the passage's governing point. | `μοῦνον Λαέρτην Ἀρκείσιος υἱὸν ἔτικτε, / μοῦνον δʼ αὖτʼ Ὀδυσῆα πατὴρ τέκεν· αὐτὰρ Ὀδυσσεὺς / μοῦνον ἔμʼ ... τέκων`. | Name the next father: “Laertes in turn fathered Odysseus, an only son.” |
| R2-004 | Note L145 | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.99 | The note says “the son who forbade telling him here” will make Laertes weep before revealing himself in Book 24. These are different agents: Telemachus gives the present instruction concerning Laertes, while Odysseus later tests and reveals himself to his father. The note fuses grandson and son to create a false structural symmetry. | At 16.146–153 Telemachus controls how Laertes is notified. In Book 24 it is Odysseus, Laertes' son, who withholds his identity during the test and then reveals it. | Keep the Book 24 connection, but distinguish Telemachus' protective instruction from Odysseus' later recognition test. |
| R2-005 | Note L227 | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.98 | The note calls “Phaeacians, sleep, treasure in the caves” the same facts Odysseus wrapped in his Cretan lie to Eumaeus at 14.321ff. That tale transposes a stored treasure to Thesprotia, but contains no Phaeacian transport, sleeping passenger, or cave cache. The larger truth-versus-fiction contrast is valid; the claimed factual correspondence is not. | At 14.321–330 Pheidon shows the disguised narrator treasure stored in his house and reports Odysseus away at Dodona; 16.227–232 instead names the Phaeacians, transport during sleep, and gifts lying in caves. | Say that the Cretan tale reworks the treasure-cache motif, rather than claiming the whole factual cluster is the same. |

## Especially successful renderings

1. **16.11–29:** Eumaeus' stunned welcome and the father-and-son simile retain the Greek's physical immediacy, tenderness, and dramatic irony.
2. **16.167–212:** Athena's transformation, Telemachus' fear, and Odysseus' revelation remain lucid through rapid changes of appearance, belief, and speaker.
3. **16.213–239:** The reunion lament, bereaved-bird simile, practical ship question, and turn toward planning preserve both emotional amplitude and the swift recovery of purpose.

## Concerns Considered

| Lines | Confidence | Concern | Why not counted |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Note L17 | 0.58 | “The real father sits three feet away” supplies an exact distance not given by the hut scene. | “Three feet away” can function idiomatically as “very close,” and the geometry has no consequence for the valid dramatic point. |
| Note L35 | 0.68 | The note can suggest that Odysseus' marriage bed has stood unused and gathering webs for the whole twenty-year absence, whereas Telemachus describes the webbed bed as the imagined consequence if Penelope has now remarried. | The wording is poetic compression, and the central image of abandonment remains consonant with Telemachus' hypothetical fear. |
| Note L136 | 0.70 | Saying Odysseus will trust Eumaeus “with the armory keys of Book 21” is literally inaccurate: Penelope handles the storeroom key, while Eumaeus carries the bow and relays an order to bar the women's doors. | “Armory keys” may be metaphorical for Eumaeus' crucial role in the weapons handoff and containment plan, so a material factual assertion is not certain. |
