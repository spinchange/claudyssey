# Reviewer 1 Audit — Odyssey 16.1–240

Overall, the translation is highly faithful and often exceptionally effective in its handling of recognition, disguise, and lament. I found one consequential pronoun-reference problem in the verse and one materially false cross-book claim in a note. Fixed-wording-only formula variants are excluded from the findings count.

| R1-001 | 16.118–120 | GRAMMAR | MODERATE | 0.88 | “His father got Odysseus” gives *his* the natural antecedent Laertes and therefore makes Laertes’ father, Arceisius, beget Odysseus. The Greek instead advances one generation at a time: Arceisius begot only Laertes, Laertes as father begot only Odysseus, and Odysseus begot only Telemachus. The attached note states the correct genealogy, but the verse’s pronoun still disrupts the defining only-son sequence. | `μοῦνον Λαέρτην Ἀρκείσιος υἱὸν ἔτικτε, / μοῦνον δ’ αὖτ’ Ὀδυσῆα πατὴρ τέκεν· αὐτὰρ Ὀδυσσεὺς / μοῦνον ἔμ’ ... τεκών` | Make Laertes the explicit subject: for example, “Laertes in turn fathered Odysseus, an only son.” |
| R1-002 | 16.227–232 (note `[^L227]`) | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.95 | The note says the truthful Phaeacian passage, sleeping transport, and treasure in caves are “the same facts” Odysseus wrapped in his Cretan lie at 14.321ff. ## What the Translation Does Especially Well

1. Lines 17–21 preserve every important pressure in Eumaeus’ father-and-son simile—the distant land, tenth year, only late-born son, accumulated suffering, and escape from death—while keeping the emotional recognition scene fluid.
2. Lines 95–111 render Odysseus’ disguised indignation with excellent control of the conditional threat, his hypothetical identities, the possibility of death by numbers, and the catalogue of abuses he refuses to tolerate.
3. Lines 213–219 carry the reunion lament into the bereaved-bird simile without sentimental simplification, preserving both the shrill density of the cries and the young taken before they can fly.
