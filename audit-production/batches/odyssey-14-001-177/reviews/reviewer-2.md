# Reviewer 2

## Overall assessment

The translation is vivid, metrically controlled, and generally faithful to the sequence, dramatic force, and social texture of 14.1–177. The principal verse-level problem is a reversal of Eumaeus's reasoning at 14.89–92. The remaining firm findings are local: two lexical intensifications or substitutions and four commentary claims that make the evidence more specific than the Greek permits.

| ID | Lines | Category | Severity | Confidence | Finding | Greek evidence | Suggested direction |

| R2-001 | 14.7–10, note L7 | ADDITION | MINOR | 0.94 | The note's “Unsupervised, unpaid, twenty years” turns the enclosure into a precisely twenty-year, uncompensated project. The passage establishes only that Eumaeus built it during Odysseus's absence and without Penelope's or Laertes' involvement; it supplies neither the construction date nor a claim that this labor was unpaid. | `αὐτὸς δεῖμʼ ... νόσφιν δεσποίνης καὶ Λαέρταο γέροντος` (“I myself built [it] ... apart from the mistress and old Laertes”). | Retain the strong point that Eumaeus independently improved his absent master's estate, but remove the exact duration and “unpaid.” |
| R2-002 | 14.15 | LEXICAL | MINOR | 0.84 | “Wallowing sows” substitutes mud-wallowing for the source's concrete epithet “ground-sleeping.” In this unusually exact account of how the breeding animals are housed, that is a different husbandry detail rather than a stylistic synonym. | `σύες χαμαιευνάδες` (“ground-sleeping sows”). | Use “ground-sleeping sows,” “sows bedded on the ground,” or another wording that retains the sleeping arrangement. |
| R2-003 | 14.29–31, note L29 | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.90 | The note says Odysseus deliberately “drops his staff” as dog-lore. The Greek marks his sitting down as the cunning act; the staff then falls from his hand. The note transfers intention to the second action and presents that agency as fact. | `ἕζετο κερδοσύνη, σκῆπτρον δέ οἱ ἔκπεσε χειρός` (“he sat down by cunning, and the staff fell from his hand”). | Attach the tactic securely to his sitting down; say that the staff falls, and qualify any further dog-handling interpretation as conjecture. |
| R2-004 | 14.42, note L39 | LEXICAL | MINOR | 0.88 | “Is starving for bread” materially intensifies and narrows Odysseus's condition. The Greek says that he wanders somewhere longing for food; it does not assert starvation or specify bread. The nearby note itself more accurately reduces the point to “is hungry.” | `ἐελδόμενός που ἐδωδῆς / πλάζετʼ` (“wanders somewhere longing for food”). | Render “longing for food,” “hungry somewhere,” or equivalent language short of starvation. |
| R2-005 | 14.49–51, note L50 | ADDITION | MINOR | 0.95 | “The man with one of everything gives the one” asserts material scarcity that the passage does not establish. It is moving that Eumaeus gives the guest his own bed-cover, but “his own” does not mean it is the only bedding he possesses. | `αὐτοῦ ἐνεύναιον` identifies the thick wild-goat skin as Eumaeus's own bedding. | Emphasize that he gives the stranger his personal bedding, without converting it into his sole possession. |
| R2-006 | 14.80–81, note L80 | ADDITION | MINOR | 0.88 | The note calls the suitors' animals “fatted five-year hogs.” Here the Greek distinguishes young pigs from fattened hogs but gives no age. A five-year-old hog does appear at 14.419 (`πενταέτηρον`), so the note appears to generalize a later animal's explicit age to the whole class at 14.81. | Here: `χοίρεʼ ... σιάλους σύας` (“young pigs ... fattened hogs”); the age term occurs only later in `ὗν ... πενταέτηρον` (14.419). | Say “young pigs below, fatted hogs above”; reserve “five-year-old” for the animal explicitly so described later. |
| R2-007 | 14.89–92 | GRAMMAR | MODERATE | 0.96 | “They have heard ... telling his grim death: that is why they will not woo / in the right way” reverses Eumaeus's inference. In the Greek, their refusal to woo lawfully or leave is evidence from which he concludes that they know or have heard of Odysseus's death; the English instead makes news of the death the cause of their conduct. | `καί τι ἴσασι ... κείνου λυγρὸν ὄλεθρον, ὅτʼ οὐκ ἐθέλουσι δικαίως / μνᾶσθαι οὐδὲ νέεσθαι` (“they know something ... of his grim death, since they are unwilling to woo lawfully or go home”). | Restore the evidential direction: “they must know something ... since they will neither woo lawfully nor go home.” |

## Especially successful renderings

1. **14.5–13:** The enclosure's walls, coping, oak stakes, and twelve adjacent pens are rendered with unusually clear spatial progression while preserving Eumaeus's unaided competence.
2. **14.56–71:** The hospitality exchange keeps both Eumaeus's courteous restraint and his bitter moral contrast between god-fearing servants and predatory masters; the catalogue of Odysseus's rewards remains concrete and affecting.
3. **14.137–147:** Eumaeus's refusal to believe the stranger, followed by his declaration that no future master could love him as Odysseus did, preserves the speech's movement from suspicion to intimate grief.
