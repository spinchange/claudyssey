# Reviewer 2

The translation handles the opening slaughter with strong clarity and force. Most action, agency, and tactical detail are preserved. The firm issues include two consequential verse attachments during the suitors’ counterattack, plus several notes that misstate grammar, staging, chronology, or formula history. No fixed-wording-only formula deviations are counted.

| R2-001 | 22.1 note | GRAMMAR | MINOR | 0.99 | The note calls `γυμνώθη` a participle and glosses it passively as “he was stripped bare.” It is a finite third-person aorist form, used intransitively or with middle force here: Odysseus strips the rags from himself. | `αὐτὰρ ὁ γυμνώθη ῥακέων` has Odysseus as the acting subject and `ῥακέων` as the clothing removed. | Call it a finite aorist and retain the verse’s accurate “he stripped himself of his rags.” |
| R2-002 | 22.1 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.98 | “Thirteen books of costume” miscounts the duration of Odysseus’ beggar disguise. Athena imposes it in Book 13, and he sheds it at the opening of Book 22: it occupies Books 13–21, not thirteen books. | The present line removes the `ῥάκεα`; the physical disguise begins after the Ithacan return in Book 13. | Use “nine books,” “since Book 13,” or avoid an exact count. |
| R2-003 | 22.9–11 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.94 | The note places Antinous’ cup “at his lips.” The Greek says he was about to lift it and was handling it so that he could drink; the arrow prevents completion of that motion. | `ἄλεισον ἀναιρήσεσθαι ἔμελλε` means he was about to lift the cup, and `ὄφρα πίοι` gives its intended purpose. | Say “cup in hand,” “lifting the cup,” or “about to drink.” |
| R2-004 | 22.31–41 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.99 | The note calls the cords-of-death sentence “verbatim” when Odysseus repeats the narrator. The recurrence is close but changes person, temporal framing, and verbal form. | Line 33 has `ὡς δή σφιν ... ἐφῆπτο`; line 41 has `νῦν ὑμῖν ... ἐφῆπται`. | Call it a pointed recurrence or near-repetition, not verbatim repetition. |
| R2-005 | 22.48–59 note | ADDITION | MINOR | 0.98 | The note says Eurymachus classifies Antinous’ death as destiny “within a minute” of the shot. The scenes are contiguous, but the Greek supplies no measured one-minute interval. | The narrative moves directly from the death to Odysseus’ revelation and Eurymachus’ reply without clock-time language. | Use “almost immediately,” “moments later,” or “while the body is still lying before him.” |
| R2-006 | 22.74–78 | LINEATION | MODERATE | 0.96 | “Shove him off the threshold and out of the doors, / then through the town” makes Odysseus appear to be the object driven through town. Eurymachus’ plan is to force him from the doorway, then have the suitors themselves reach the town and raise the alarm. | After `ἀπώσομεν` takes Odysseus as object at 76, a new first-person plural verb begins the next action: `ἔλθωμεν δʼ ἀνὰ ἄστυ`, “let us go through/up the town.” | Supply the new subject and verb explicitly: “then let us reach the town and raise the alarm.” |
| R2-007 | 22.89–91 | AMBIGUITY | MODERATE | 0.96 | “Amphinomus went for glorious Odysseus … to see if he might give way from the doors” lets “he” refer most naturally to Amphinomus. The Greek means that Amphinomus charges in hope that Odysseus will yield the doorway before him. | In `εἴ πώς οἱ εἴξειε θυράων`, dative `οἱ` refers to Amphinomus, while the unexpressed subject of `εἴξειε` is Odysseus. | Use “to see if Odysseus might give way before him” or “hoping to drive Odysseus from the doors.” |
| R2-008 | 22.139–141 | GRAMMAR | MINOR | 0.87 | Melanthius’ “I think” becomes “I am certain.” The assertion that the arms are nowhere else occurs within his stated supposition, so the English strengthens his epistemic commitment. | `ὀΐομαι` explicitly means “I think/suppose”: `ἔνδον γάρ, ὀΐομαι, οὐδέ πη ἄλλῃ`. | Retain the modality: “For it is inside, I think—and nowhere else—that Odysseus and his son stored the arms.” |
| R2-009 | 22.147–149 note | MISTRANSLATION | MODERATE | 1.00 | The note says the knees-and-heart formula is applied to Odysseus “for the only time in the poem” and contrasts this moment with “the storm.” The identical formula is applied to Odysseus twice during the storm and landing crisis in Book 5. The note’s central claim of uniquely extreme fear is therefore false. | `καὶ τότʼ Ὀδυσσῆος λύτο γούνατα καὶ φίλον ἦτορ` here repeats the wording at 5.297 and 5.406. | Describe this as the formula’s return in Odysseus’ own house, contrasting domestic battle with its earlier applications at sea, rather than calling it unique. |

## Especially Successful Renderings

1. **22.8–25:** Antinous’ interrupted drink, the arrow through his neck, the blood, overturned table, fouled feast, and suitors’ search for absent weapons are staged with exceptional physical clarity.
2. **22.45–67:** Eurymachus’ attempt to isolate blame and offer restitution, followed by Odysseus’ absolute refusal, preserves both the financial language and the opposed speech acts.
3. **22.79–104:** The deaths of Eurymachus and Amphinomus and Telemachus’ rapid decision to fetch armor retain the Greek’s momentum and concrete bodily detail.
