# Reviewer 1

The translation is consistently attentive to syntax, scene structure, and Homeric characterization. I find three firm local fidelity issues: one transferred epithet, one consequential misreading amplified by its note, and one weakening of violent royal action. Formula-register-only deviations are excluded.

| ID | Lines | Category | Severity | Confidence | Finding | Greek evidence | Suggested direction |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---|---|---|
| R1-001 | 4.11 | LEXICAL | MINOR | 0.86 | “Born to him in his strength” transfers the force of “strong” from Megapenthes to Menelaus or the circumstances of the birth. This loses the son's explicit characterization. “Well-loved” is a defensible treatment of `τηλύγετος`, but does not restore `κρατερός`. | `ὅς οἱ τηλύγετος γένετο κρατερὸς Μεγαπένθης` identifies Megapenthes himself as `κρατερός`. | Keep the chosen sense of `τηλύγετος`, but attach strength to the son: e.g. “his cherished/late-born son, strong Megapenthes.” |
| R1-002 | 4.140 and note | MISTRANSLATION | MODERATE | 0.96 | “Shall I lie” turns Helen's uncertainty about whether her identification will prove false into a contemplated act of deliberate deception. The note compounds this by claiming that she “flags her own unreliability as a narrator” while introducing a story; here she is cautiously identifying Telemachus, not beginning a narrative. | `ψεύσομαι ἦ ἔτυμον ἐρέω;` contrasts being wrong or speaking falsely with speaking truly; it does not require intentional lying. | Render the uncertainty directly, such as “Shall I be mistaken, or speak the truth?” Revise the note so it does not build narrator unreliability on a volitional lie absent from the Greek. |
| R1-003 | 4.176 and note | LEXICAL | MODERATE | 0.90 | “Emptying out one town” weakens `ἐξαλαπάξας`, which denotes sacking or laying waste a city, into comparatively neutral depopulation. The note repeats that weaker account. This materially softens the violence and sovereign power in Menelaus' fantasy of resettling Odysseus. | `μίαν πόλιν ἐξαλαπάξας` means “having sacked/laid waste one city.” | Restore the destructive action, e.g. “after sacking one town,” and let the note acknowledge that Menelaus would destroy a subject city to make room for his friend. |

## Especially Successful Renderings

1. **4.33–36:** “We two ate plenty of strangers' bread” gives `ξεινήια` concrete poetic force while preserving Menelaus' appeal to reciprocal hospitality.
2. **4.90–109:** The wealth-and-grief passage tracks the Greek argument cleanly, culminating effectively in “a grief never to be forgotten” for `ἄχος αἰὲν ἄλαστον`.
3. **4.183–202:** The communal lament and Peisistratus' intervention preserve both the shifting mourners and his precocious restraint, including the understated “hardly the meanest / of the Argives.”
