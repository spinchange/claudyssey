# Reviewer 2

## Overall assessment

The translation sustains the closing movement of Book 14 with strong narrative clarity, especially through Eumaeus's rejection of the homecoming report, the sacrifice, and the cloak-fable. The firm issues are limited: three local verse-level shifts in address, agency, and speech force, plus three notes that overstate what Eumaeus rejects, whether he is fooled, and the literal final line of the book.

| R2-001 | 14.363–365, note L363 | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.95 | The note says Eumaeus rejects “precisely the one clause that is true.” He rejects the whole Odysseus section of the tale, which contains several inventions—the Thesprotian hosting, stored treasure, Dodona journey, and prepared escort—alongside the true core claim that Odysseus lives and will return. The grief-filter insight remains apt, but its claimed precision is false. | `τά γʼ οὐ κατὰ κόσμον ... εἰπὼν ἀμφʼ Ὀδυσῆϊ` rejects “those things” said about Odysseus, not one isolated proposition. | Say that Eumaeus rejects the tale's true conclusion together with its fabricated supporting details, rather than precisely isolating one true clause. |
| R2-002 | 14.443 | LEXICAL | MINOR | 0.94 | “My god-touched guest” literalizes `δαιμόνιε` into a claim of divine contact. In this vocative construction, `δαιμόνιε ξείνων` expresses astonishment or evaluative address—roughly “unaccountable,” “strange,” or “poor” stranger—not that the guest has been touched by a god. | `ἔσθιε, δαιμόνιε ξείνων` is an emphatic vocative, followed by the ordinary invitation to eat what is available. | Use “strange guest,” “poor stranger,” “remarkable stranger,” or another nonliteral vocative suited to Eumaeus's tone. |

## Especially successful renderings

1. **14.360–389:** Eumaeus's response preserves the difficult balance between emotional engagement, hardened disbelief, and unconditional observance of hospitality toward a man he does not trust.
2. **14.414–445:** The slaughter, offering, sevenfold division, guest's honor-portion, and Eumaeus's modest theology are rendered in a clear ritual sequence without losing the scene's dramatic irony.
3. **14.475–506:** The cloak-fable keeps its physical cold, nested deception, quiet tactical speech, and gradual transformation from heroic reminiscence into a transparent present request.

## Concerns Considered

| Lines | Confidence | Concern | Why not counted |

| 14.386–389, note L386 | 0.68 | “Every other host in the poem was testing the stranger's story” may overstate Eumaeus's uniqueness, since other hosts feed and protect strangers before learning or verifying their histories. | The narrower and more important claim—that Eumaeus expressly decouples his duty from belief—is well supported and may be all the rhetorical comparison intends. |
| 14.437–438, note L437 | 0.72 | The note's phrase “ἄναξ meaning both the host doing honor and the hidden owner receiving it” can wrongly suggest that `ἄνακτος` denotes Eumaeus as well as Odysseus; grammatically it is the genitive owner of `θυμόν` and refers to Odysseus. | Read as a description of the whole clause's dramatic double exposure rather than a lexical claim about `ἄναξ`, the note correctly explains that Eumaeus unknowingly pleases his actual master while honoring the apparent beggar. |
| 14.449–452, note L449 | 0.69 | “No ancient text otherwise bothers to record that a swineherd could hold an asset” is an extremely broad uniqueness claim; other ancient evidence certainly records slaves administering property and even sub-slaves, though the exact swineherd parallel may be rare. | The claim's narrowly worded occupational qualifier makes a direct contradiction uncertain, while the secure local point—Eumaeus bought Mesaulius with his own goods—remains correct. |
