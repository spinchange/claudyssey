# Reviewer 1

The translation is consistently strong in narrative sequence, sacrificial procedure, and the cloak-fable’s larger rhetorical design. Five issues qualify as firm findings: two localized verse distortions and three materially false explanatory claims. No fixed-wording-only formula deviation is counted.

| R1-001 | 443 | LEXICAL | MINOR | 0.93 | “My god-touched guest” literalizes `δαιμόνιε` into a positive supernatural characterization. In this vocative use the adjective marks Eumaeus’ emphatic, familiar address—roughly “strange fellow” or “poor man”—rather than asserting divine contact. | `ἔσθιε, δαιμόνιε ξείνων`: Eumaeus urges the stranger to eat; nothing in the clause identifies him as inspired, possessed, or touched by a god. | Use a forceful human address such as “strange guest,” “poor stranger,” or an idiomatic equivalent suited to Eumaeus’ voice. |
| R1-002 | 470–471 | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.96 | “They asked it themselves” weakens the operative force of `ἄνωγον`. Odysseus and Menelaus did not merely request the teller’s participation; they bade or appointed him to command with them. “Third in command” preserves the resulting role but not how it was imposed. | `τοῖσι δʼ ἅμα τρίτος ἄρχον ἐγών· αὐτοὶ γὰρ ἄνωγον`: “with them I commanded as the third, for they themselves ordered/bade it.” | Retain the command: “with them I commanded as the third—they themselves had ordered it.” |
| R1-003 | 437 note | MISTRANSLATION | MINOR | 0.98 | The note claims that `ἄναξ` means both “the host doing honor” and “the hidden owner receiving it.” The grammar allows no such double reference: genitive `ἄνακτος` modifies `θυμόν` and designates Odysseus, the master whose heart Eumaeus gladdens or honors. Eumaeus is not called `ἄναξ` here. The dramatic irony is real, but the proposed grammatical mechanism is false. | `κύδαινε δὲ θυμὸν ἄνακτος`: “he honored/gladdened the heart of the master.” | Keep the dramatic double effect while stating the grammar accurately: Eumaeus honors the apparent beggar and thereby unknowingly gladdens his actual master, whom `ἄνακτος` names. |
| R1-004 | 508 note | MISTRANSLATION | MODERATE | 0.97 | Calling Eumaeus “the only listener Odysseus never fools” materially reverses their narrative relationship. Eumaeus recognizes the cloak-story’s profitable point, but he remains deceived about the speaker’s identity and the Cretan autobiography, accepting or being moved by much of the fabricated travelogue while rejecting only its true Odysseus report. Seeing through one indirect request does not make him globally undeceived. | At 363–365 Eumaeus isolates the Odysseus claim as the unacceptable part of the tale; at 508–512 he praises the `αἶνος` as apt and grants its practical request. Neither passage shows recognition of Odysseus or exposure of the Cretan fiction. | Narrow the claim to the local feat: Eumaeus detects the cloak-fable’s self-serving point and rewards it knowingly, even while Odysseus’ larger disguise and false autobiography continue to deceive him. |

## Especially Successful Renderings

1. **393–406:** The wager and Eumaeus’ ironic refusal preserve the conditional structure, the proposed penalty, the deterrent purpose, and the moral absurdity of praying to Zeus after murdering a protected guest.
2. **418–438:** The compact sacrifice retains the ordered sequence of bristles, prayer, stunning and slaughter, raw first portions in fat, barley, roasting, sevenfold division, divine share, and the honored chine.
3. **475–502:** The cloak-fable vividly preserves the freezing ambush, the lone tunic, the whispered appeal, Odysseus’ invented dream, Thoas’ rapid departure, and the teller’s relief beneath the abandoned cloak.
