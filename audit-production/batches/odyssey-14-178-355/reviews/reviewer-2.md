# Reviewer 2

## Overall assessment

The Cretan tale is rendered with considerable narrative momentum and generally preserves its shifts among social autobiography, military failure, supplication, enslavement, and shipwreck. The firm issues are limited but material: one acquisition action is replaced by an emotional response, a six-day duration becomes a six-day delay, and three notes overstate chronology or convert suggestive parallels into exact facts.

## Especially successful renderings

1. **14.178–190:** Eumaeus's fear for Telemachus widens naturally into fear for the extinction of Arceisius's line, and the ensuing identity questions preserve both their formal sequence and the closing joke about arriving on foot.
2. **14.199–228:** The false Cretan's marginal birth, reduced inheritance, former martial confidence, and aversion to domestic work remain distinct parts of a coherent invented character rather than being flattened into generic hardship.
3. **14.273–315:** The disarmament and supplication retain their physical clarity and xenia theology, while the later shipwreck closely preserves the deliberate Book 12 recurrence without confusing its new fictional setting.
