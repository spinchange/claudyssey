# Reviewer 1

Overall, the translation is highly faithful across the shipwreck sequence, preserving action, syntax, and the major similes with strong line-by-line control. I find four firm, localized issues: one moderate lexical weakening and three minor technical or grammatical deviations. Formula-register-only differences are excluded.

| ID | Lines | Category | Severity | Confidence | Finding | Greek evidence | Suggested direction |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---|---|---|
| R1-001 | 257 | ADDITION | MINOR | 0.95 | “For ballast” assigns the brushwood a function the Greek does not state and that is technically unlikely for light brush. The material is laid or heaped on the raft, possibly as flooring or additional wave protection. | `πολλὴν δʼ ἐπεχεύατο ὕλην`, “he heaped/strewed much brushwood upon it.” | Remove “for ballast”; retain the neutral action, such as “and heaped brushwood over it.” |
| R1-002 | 261 | LEXICAL | MINOR | 0.97 | “On rollers” replaces the explicit launching tools: Odysseus hauls the raft down with bars, crowbars, or levers. The launching action survives, but the concrete craft detail changes. | `μοχλοῖσιν ... κατείρυσεν`, “with levers/crowbars he hauled her down.” | Use “with levers” or “with crowbars” rather than “on rollers.” |
| R1-003 | 423 | LEXICAL | MODERATE | 0.88 | “Is at odds with me” preserves the Odysseus wordplay but weakens Poseidon’s explicit anger or hostile grievance into ordinary disagreement. The surrounding danger makes the enmity inferable, but it does not fully restore the emotional force of Odysseus’ conclusion. This is a semantic loss distinct from mechanical formula conformity. | `ὀδώδυσται ... ἐννοσίγαιος`, “the shaker of earth is angered/hostile toward me,” with the pun on Odysseus’ name. | Preserve the pun while marking hostility, for example “how bitterly the shaker of earth is at odds with me.” |
| R1-004 | 492–493 and note 488 | GRAMMAR | MINOR | 0.94 | The English makes Athena herself the subject who folds Odysseus’ eyelids shut, and the note repeats that direct attribution. In the Greek, Athena pours Sleep upon him, while personified Sleep is the masculine agent that covers his eyelids and releases him from weariness. Athena remains the ultimate cause, but the closing personification is transferred away from Sleep. | `Ἀθήνη / ὕπνον ... χεῦʼ ... ἀμφικαλύψας`: Athena “poured Sleep,” followed by masculine `ἀμφικαλύψας`, “covering his dear eyelids.” | Make Sleep the proximate subject: Athena pours Sleep on him, “and Sleep covered his dear eyelids”; revise the note accordingly. |

## Especially Successful Renderings

- **306–312:** Odysseus’ preference for a witnessed heroic death is sharply preserved, including rites, communal fame, and the disgrace of an anonymous death at sea.
- **394–399:** The father-and-children recovery simile retains its reversed emotional structure and carries `ἀσπάσιος` effectively into the land’s welcome appearance.
- **432–435:** The octopus simile preserves both the precise sucker-and-pebble observation and its violent reversal onto the skin stripped from Odysseus’ hands.
