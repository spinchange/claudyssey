# Note corrections — Book 13 (draft for review)

## 13.42 — "the wife is named at last" (O13-001-220-A-001, MINOR)

**Current** (`translation/book-13.md:462`):
> [^L42]: His farewell wish: to find at home "my blameless wife... with those I love unharmed." Before the Phaeacians — who offered him a princess — the wife is named at last, freely, now that the ship is waiting. The reticence of 7.225 (possessions, slaves, house — no wife) is repealed at the exact moment it stops being protective.

**Proposed:**
> [^L42]: His farewell wish: to find at home "my blameless wife... with those I love unharmed." Before the Phaeacians — who offered him a princess — he wishes for his wife openly now, in his own person and not through the tale (the Nekyia had already named Penelope). The reticence of 7.225 (possessions, slaves, house — no wife), first loosened inside the storytelling, is dropped entirely now that the ship is waiting and it stops being protective.

**Why:** His Book 11 narration already mentioned his wife and named Penelope before this audience; and 13.42 itself says only "wife," naming no one.

## 13.200 — the "verbatim" three-line recurrence (O13-001-220-A-004, MINOR)

**Current** (`translation/book-13.md:492`):
> [^L200]: The arrival-question, third and last time (= 6.119–121 = 9.175–176), verbatim: "violent, savage, without justice, or kind to strangers?" Asked of Phaeacia, the answer was the kindest people in the poem; of the Cyclops' coast, the cruelest. Asked of *Ithaca*, the question stops being rhetorical: his own island now contains both answers — the loyal swineherd and the hall full of men eating his house. The formula has been aimed at this target for seven books.

**Proposed:**
> [^L200]: The arrival-taxonomy, third and last time (= 6.120–121 = 9.175–176): "violent, savage, without justice, or kind to strangers?" — in Books 6 and 13 preceded by the same bewildered whose-country-is-this, a preamble the Cyclops' coast never got. Asked of Phaeacia, the answer was the kindest people in the poem; of the Cyclops' coast, the cruelest. Asked of *Ithaca*, the question stops being rhetorical: his own island now contains both answers — the loyal swineherd and the hall full of men eating his house. The formula has been aimed at this target for seven books.

**Why:** Only the two-line ethical taxonomy recurs in all three books; the preceding country-question is absent in Book 9, so the three-line verbatim claim overstates.

## 13.237 — the catalogue misattributed to 9.21–27 (O13-221-440-A-001, MINOR)

**Current** (`translation/book-13.md:498`):
> [^L237]: Her teasing opens with the put-down formula the Cyclops used on him ("You are a fool, stranger, or you have come from far away," 9.273) — recycled now as affectionate irony. And her description of Ithaca is a catalogue of everything its exiles have said of it: rough, no horse-country (Telemachus at 4.601–608), but grain, wine, rain, dew, goat-pasture, timber (his own praise at 9.21–27). She identifies the island by quoting its lovers.

**Proposed:**
> [^L237]: Her teasing opens with the put-down formula the Cyclops used on him ("You are a fool, stranger, or you have come from far away," 9.273) — recycled now as affectionate irony. And her description of Ithaca answers everything its exiles have said of it: rough, no horse-country (Telemachus at 4.601–608; his own "rugged, but a good nurse of men," 9.27) — and then grain, wine, rain, dew, goat-pasture, timber, watering-places: a bounty the lovers never claimed. She identifies the island by praising it past its own praisers.

**Why:** The resource catalogue is Athena's (13.244–247); 9.21–27 contains none of it. The corrected note keeps the quoting-game and adds Athena's actual move — outbidding the exiles.

**Why:** Only the final Greek line is identical across the two passages.

## 13.370 — the rolled, Cyclops-sized stone (O13-221-440-A-003, MINOR)

**Current** (`translation/book-13.md:520`):
> [^L370]: The treasure sealed in the cave, "and a stone was set at the door by Pallas Athena." A goddess rolls the stone; the last stone set at a door this size was the Cyclops' (9.240–243). The poem's doors keep their memory: this one closes on wealth, not victims, and opens on the poem's endgame.

**Proposed:**
> [^L370]: The treasure sealed in the cave, "and a stone was set at the door by Pallas Athena." The last time the poem set a stone at a cave door, the hand was the Cyclops' (9.240–243). The poem's doors keep their memory: this one closes on wealth, not victims, and opens on the poem's endgame.

**Why:** The Greek says only that Athena set a stone at the doors — no rolling, no size. The echo of 9.240–243 needs neither.
